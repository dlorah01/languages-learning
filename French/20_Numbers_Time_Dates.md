# Lesson 20 — Numbers, Time, and Dates: The Practical Toolkit
### Phase 1: Core System (A1 → A2)

**Competency axes trained:** 📚 Lexicon (primary) · 🗣️ Productive fluency
**Prerequisite:** None specific — this is a practical reference lesson
**Time investment:** One thorough read + daily practice saying numbers/times/dates aloud for a week

---

## 1. Why This Practical Lesson Now (Intuitive Explanation)

Numbers, time, and dates are unglamorous but appear in nearly every real conversation — prices, schedules, ages, phone numbers. This lesson consolidates them into one reference, with special attention to French's famously quirky counting system beyond 60, which has no equivalent logic in Spanish or English.

---

## 2. Numbers 0–20 (memorize as a solid block)

zéro, un, deux, trois, quatre, cinq, six, sept, huit, neuf, dix, onze, douze, treize, quatorze, quinze, seize, dix-sept, dix-huit, dix-neuf, vingt

---

## 3. Numbers 20–69 (regular pattern, like Spanish)

Tens: vingt (20), trente (30), quarante (40), cinquante (50), soixante (60) — combine with units via hyphen, and "et" for "one": vingt-et-un (21), trente-deux (32), quarante-cinq (45).

---

## 4. Numbers 70–99: The Famous Quirk

This is where French genuinely diverges from both Spanish and most of the world's counting logic. Standard (France) French doesn't have separate words for 70, 80, 90 — it builds them arithmetically:

| Number | French | Literal breakdown |
|---|---|---|
| 70 | soixante-dix | "sixty-ten" |
| 71 | soixante-et-onze | "sixty-and-eleven" |
| 80 | quatre-vingts | "four-twenties" |
| 81 | quatre-vingt-un | "four-twenty-one" |
| 90 | quatre-vingt-dix | "four-twenty-ten" |
| 99 | quatre-vingt-dix-neuf | "four-twenty-ten-nine" |

**Historical note (connects to Lesson 2):** this vigesimal (base-20) counting pattern is a relic of Celtic/Gaulish influence on French, predating even the Latin layer — a genuine linguistic fossil, similar in spirit to the silent letters you learned about in Lesson 2/5. Belgium and Switzerland use simpler forms (septante=70, nonante=90, and in Switzerland huitante=80) — worth knowing if you ever interact with Francophone Belgians or Swiss, but standard/Parisian French uses the quirky system above.

---

## 5. Beyond 100

cent (100), cent un (101), deux cents (200 — note the -s on "cent" when multiplied and not followed by another number), deux cent un (201 — no -s when followed by another number), mille (1000, invariable — never takes -s).

---

## 6. Telling Time

French uses both a 24-hour format (official/formal — trains, TV schedules, appointments) and informal 12-hour spoken format:

> **Il est quinze heures.** — "It's 3:00 PM." (formal/24-hour)
> **Il est trois heures.** — "It's 3:00." (informal/12-hour, context clarifies AM/PM)
> **Il est trois heures et quart.** — "It's 3:15." (quarter past)
> **Il est trois heures et demie.** — "It's 3:30." (half past)
> **Il est quatre heures moins le quart.** — "It's 3:45." (quarter to four — literally "four hours minus the quarter")

**Structural note:** "moins" (minus) for the second half of the hour is a genuinely different mental model from Spanish's "y/menos" system, though the underlying logic (counting toward the next hour) is similar to some Spanish regional usage.

---

## 7. Dates

**Format: day + month + year** (like Spanish, unlike US English's month-first format):
> **le 15 août 2026** — "August 15, 2026"

**Key rule:** use cardinal numbers for the date (quinze, not "quinzième"), **except the 1st**, which uses the ordinal:
> **le premier janvier** — "January 1st" (NOT "le un janvier")
> **le deux février** — "February 2nd" (cardinal, not ordinal)

---

## 8. Mental Model: Numbers as a Base-20 Fossil Sitting Inside a Base-10 System

Picture French numbers as mostly following the same base-10 logic as Spanish and English — until you hit 70, where an older, base-20 (vigesimal) counting system, inherited from Gaulish, suddenly resurfaces and takes over until 100. This isn't illogical, it's **two historical systems layered on top of each other** — exactly the kind of "fossil" phenomenon Lesson 2 taught you to expect from French.

---

## 9. Common Mistakes

**Spanish speakers:** trying to find a direct one-word equivalent for 70/80/90 as Spanish has (setenta/ochenta/noventa); using ordinal numbers for all dates by over-applying the "premier" exception.

**English speakers:** using month-day date order (English/US convention) instead of day-month; misreading 24-hour time formats in formal/written contexts.

---

## 10. Progressively Harder Examples

1. J'ai vingt-cinq ans. — "I'm 25 years old."
2. Le train part à quatorze heures trente. — "The train leaves at 2:30 PM."
3. Il est quatre heures moins dix. — "It's 3:50." (ten minutes to four)
4. Mon anniversaire, c'est le premier mars. — "My birthday is March 1st."
5. Quatre-vingt-quinze pour cent des étudiants ont réussi l'examen. — "95% of the students passed the exam."
