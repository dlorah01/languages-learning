## 1. Why Study Versification?

French poetry is a **rule-governed sound system**. Once you know the rules, three things happen:
- Poems that looked like opaque walls become **readable**, because you can hear their rhythm.
- Your **pronunciation** of the mute *e*, liaison, and syllable boundaries gets sharper (this is the same machinery as ordinary speech, made explicit).
- You gain access to a huge part of French cultural memory: schoolchildren learn and recite poems, and lines from poetry appear in songs, speeches, and newspaper headlines.

**Mental model: verse is a counted sound-grid.** In English poetry you count stresses; in Spanish you count syllables up to the last stressed one (plus one); in **French you count syllables**, and since French has no lexical stress, the rhythm comes from **grouping** syllables into phrases and a **caesura** (pause).

---

## 2. Counting Syllables: The Core Rules

### 2.1 The basic principle
Count every pronounced syllable, with these specific conventions:

| Situation | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Mute *e* before a consonant** (inside the line) | **counts** as a syllable | *tombe sur* → *tom-be sur* (3) |
| **Mute *e* before a vowel or mute *h*** | **elides**, doesn't count | *ville endormie* → *vil-len-dor-mi* |
| **Mute *e* at the end of a line** | **does not count** | *endormie* ends as *…dor-mi* |
| **Mute *e* after a vowel inside a line** (*vie*, *joue*, *ils jouent*) before a consonant | forbidden in classical verse (cannot be elided and would add an awkward syllable); poets avoid it | *la vie est belle* ✔ (before vowel, elides); *la vie passe* ✘ in classical verse |
| **Final *-es* and *-ent* (verb)** | behave like mute *e* | *elles chantent* at line end → ends on *chan-* |

### 2.2 Hiatus is avoided
In classical French verse, a word ending in a **pronounced vowel** cannot be followed by a word beginning with a vowel: *il a eu une idée* breaks the rule (vowel + vowel hiatus). Poets choose other words or use consonant-ending words. This is stricter than ordinary speech.

### 2.3 Diérèse and synérèse
When a vowel sequence is pronounced as **two syllables** (diérèse) or **one** (synérèse), it affects the count.

| Term | Meaning | Typical examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Diérèse** | split into two syllables | *na-ti-on, am-bi-ti-on, vi-o-lent, li-on* |
| **Synérèse** | merged into one syllable | *pied* (1), *hier* (1), *lion* in everyday speech (1) |

**Practical convention (classical verse):** words ending in *-tion/-sion* are often given diérèse (*na-ti-on*); words like *pied, hier, lier* stay single. Poets of the nineteenth century and later use both freely; the context (the syllable total the poet needs) tells you which one is intended. **Rule of thumb:** count, and if the line is one syllable short or long, test diérèse or synérèse.

### 2.4 Worked examples (original lines)

**Décasyllabe (10 syllables; typical caesura after the 4th or 5th):**
> *La nuit tombe sur la ville endormie*
> La(1) nuit(2) tom(3) be(4) | sur(5) la(6) vil(7) len(8) dor(9) mi(10)
> (the final *e* is not counted; *ville endormie* elides *le + en*)

**Alexandrine (12 syllables, caesura after the 6th):**
> *Le vent souffle à travers les branches du vieux chêne*
> Le(1) vent(2) souf(3) flà(4) tra(5) vers(6) | les(7) bran(8) ches(9) du(10) vieux(11) chê(12) (ne)
> (*souffle à* elides into one syllable *flà*; the closing *e* of *chêne* is not counted)

**Octosyllabe (8 syllables):**
> *Le soir pose un voile léger*
> Le(1) soir(2) po(3) zun(4) voi(5) le(6) lé(7) ger(8)
> (*pose un*: the *e* elides before *un*; the *e* of *voile* is before a consonant, so it counts)

## 3. The Main Meters

| Meter | Syllables | Typical caesura | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Octosyllabe** | 8 | none or after 4 | songs, light poetry, ballads, medieval narrative |
| **Décasyllabe** | 10 | 4/6 (or 5/5) | epic and lyrical poetry of the Middle Ages and Renaissance |
| **Alexandrin** | 12 | **6/6** (classical) | classical theatre (Corneille, Racine, Molière's verse comedies), Romantic and Parnassian poetry |
| **Vers libre** | free | free | modern poetry (from the late nineteenth century on) |
| **Poème en prose** | no verse | no line | prose poem (Baudelaire, Rimbaud) |

### 3.1 The classical alexandrine: 4 groups of 3 (or 2 hemistichs of 6)
The classical alexandrine splits into two **hémistiches** of 6 syllables, each usually containing two rhythmic groups. The caesura must fall at a natural syntactic break (not in the middle of a word or tight phrase).

### 3.2 Romantic loosening
Romantic poets introduced the **trimètre** (3 groups of 4 syllables: 4//4//4), **enjambement** (a sentence running over the line end), and **rejet/contre-rejet** (important words thrown to the beginning of the next line) to break the classical regularity.

---

## 4. Rhyme

### 4.1 Rhyme is about **sound**, not spelling
Rhyme counts the **final pronounced sounds** from the last stressed vowel. Spelling-based pairs may not rhyme (*pas / bras* both rhyme in [ɑ], *chant / champ* in [ɑ̃]) and similar spelling may not rhyme.

### 4.2 Quality of rhyme
| Type | Shared sounds | Example (pairs) |
|---|---|---|
| **Pauvre** (poor) | one sound (the vowel) | *ami / joli* (only [i]) |
| **Suffisante** (sufficient) | two sounds | *cheval / canal* ([a] + [l]) |
| **Riche** (rich) | three or more sounds | *tendresse / caresse* ([r] + [ɛ] + [s]) |

*Rule of thumb:* the more sounds (including the *consonne d'appui*, the consonant before the rhyming vowel), the richer the rhyme.

### 4.3 Masculine and feminine rhymes
- **Feminine rhyme:** the line ends in a **mute *e*** (including *-es* and verbal *-ent*): *lune / dune*, *rivière / lumière*.
- **Masculine rhyme:** all other endings (*soir / voir*).
- **Alternation rule (classical):** feminine and masculine rhymes **alternate** by couplet or stanza. It is a rule of classical and Parnassian verse; later poets relax it.

Notice: a word like *ils chantent* counts as feminine at the end of a line (final *-ent* silent), while *le chant* is masculine.

### 4.4 Schemes
| Scheme | Pattern | Name |
|---|---|---|
| **AABB** | paired | rimes plates (or suivies) |
| **ABAB** | alternating | rimes croisées |
| **ABBA** | enclosed | rimes embrassées |

Rhyme in French is normally **consonantal/complete**, unlike Spanish verse, where **assonance** (matching vowels only) is common in traditional forms such as the *romance*.

## 5. Forms and Genres

| Form | Structure |
|---|---|
| **Sonnet** | 14 lines: two quatrains + two tercets; typical rhymes ABBA ABBA CCD EDE (variations exist); usually alexandrines |
| **Ballade** | stanzas + envoi, refrain line (medieval) |
| **Rondeau / rondel** | repeated opening lines as a refrain |
| **Fable** | short narrative in verse with a moral, often in mixed meters (La Fontaine) |
| **Ode, élégie** | lyric forms of praise or lament |
| **Alexandrin dramatique** | the line of tragedy and verse comedy |
| **Vers libre** | free verse |
| **Poème en prose** | prose with poetic density |
| **Chanson** | song lyrics: strong tradition of the *auteur-compositeur-interprète* (Brassens, Brel, Barbara, Gainsbourg) |

## 6. Reading Aloud: What Changes Compared with Speech

| Feature | In ordinary speech | In recited verse |
|---|---|---|
| **Mute *e* before a consonant** | often dropped | **pronounced** as a light vowel when it counts as a syllable |
| **Mute *e* at line end** | dropped | silent, but may be lightly sustained in song |
| **Liaison** | variable | follows the more formal pattern |
| **Rhythm** | follows sentence stress (last syllable of a phrase) | follows the groups and the caesura of the meter |
| **Intonation** | sentence-based | still sentence-based; avoid "singsong" at the line break unless the syntax pauses there |

**Technique:** read the poem first for **sense**, then mark syllable counts, then read aloud respecting syntax. Do not stop mechanically at every line end; stop where the sentence stops (enjambement matters).

---

## 7. Spanish ↔ French Comparison

| Feature | Spanish | French |
|---|---|---|
| **Counting** | to the **last stressed syllable + 1** | **all syllables**; final mute *e* not counted |
| **Contact of vowels between words** | *sinalefa* (merge) is general | only the **mute *e* elides**; other vowel contacts are hiatus (avoided in classical verse) |
| **Typical long line** | *endecasílabo* (11), *alejandrino* (14) | *alexandrin* (12) |
| **Rhyme** | consonant or **assonant** | normally full (consonantal) rhyme |
| **Stress** | lexical stress shapes rhythm | no lexical stress; grouping and caesura shape rhythm |
| **Free verse** | widespread | widespread since the late nineteenth century |

The conceptual shift for you: in Spanish, a final stressed syllable at the end of a line (*agudo*) changes the count; in French, the count is simply of **pronounced syllables**, with the end-of-line mute *e* not counting.

---

## 8. Common Mistakes

- **Counting spelled vowels rather than pronounced ones** (silent *e* inside words like *vraiment*; remember *vrai-ment* = 2 or 3 depending on the pronunciation convention, test with the line total).
- **Forgetting the end-of-line mute *e***: it never counts.
- **Eliding the wrong *e*:** elision happens only before a vowel or mute *h*, not before *h aspiré* (*la honte* → no elision).
- **Confusing masculine/feminine rhyme by spelling:** it is about the final **sound**.
- **Reading like prose:** ignoring the caesura and the mute *e*'s on stage.
- **Applying classical rules to modern poems:** modern poets deliberately break them.

## 9. A Reading Path (Public-Domain Core)

| Era | Poets and works to explore | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Renaissance** | Ronsard, Du Bellay | sonnet tradition, love and exile |
| **Classical** | La Fontaine (*Fables*), Racine, Molière | clear alexandrines, wit |
| **Romantic** | Hugo, Lamartine, Musset, Nerval | lyric, rhythm, enjambement |
| **Parnassian/Symbolist** | Baudelaire, Verlaine, Rimbaud, Mallarmé | musicality, the prose poem, complexity |
| **Modern** | Apollinaire, Valéry, Prévert, Éluard | free verse, surrealism |
| **Francophone** | Senghor, Césaire, Anna de Noailles | poetry beyond France |

Start with **fables** (short, narrative, humorous) and **Verlaine** (musical, short lines), and use a bilingual or annotated edition if needed. For modern poets still under copyright, read them in legitimate editions or libraries and use anthologies for study.

## 11. Progressively Harder Practice

1. **Count (10 min):** count the syllables of these original lines:
   a. *Je marche seul au bord de l'eau*
   b. *Les étoiles brillent sur la mer immense*
2. **Fix the line:** the line *Le vent chante dans la nuit* is one syllable short of an octosyllable; add a word.
3. **Classify:** is each rhyme masculine or feminine? *rivière / lumière*, *chemin / matin*, *ils parlent / elles brûlent*.
4. **Caesura marking:** mark the natural caesura of *Le vent souffle à travers les branches du vieux chêne*.
5. **Read aloud:** record yourself reading a fable with natural intonation; compare to a professional recording.
6. **Write:** compose a quatrain in rimes croisées in octosyllables about your city; check the count aloud.
7. **Paraphrase:** rewrite a poem in plain prose in French, then explain what was lost.

## 12. Mini Quiz (active recall)

1. How many syllables does a classical alexandrine have, and where is the caesura?
2. Does the mute *e* at the end of a line count?
3. What happens to a mute *e* before a vowel inside a line?
4. Is *ils chantent* at line end a masculine or feminine ending?
5. What is the difference between rimes croisées and embrassées?
6. What is diérèse?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. 12; after the 6th syllable. 2. No. 3. It elides (does not count). 4. Feminine (the final *-ent* is silent, like a mute *e*). 5. Croisées = ABAB; embrassées = ABBA. 6. Pronouncing a vowel sequence as two syllables (e.g., *na-ti-on*) to meet the count.

**Practice answers:**
- **1a.** Je(1) mar(2) che(3) seul(4) au(5) bord(6) de(7) l'eau(8) = 8 (the mute *e* of *marche* counts before *seul*).
- **1b.** Les(1) é(2) toi(3) les(4) bril(5) lent(6) sur(7) la(8) mer(9) im(10) men(11) (se) = 11. An 11-syllable line fits none of the classical meters; add or remove a syllable to make it a 10 or 12.
- **2.** Le(1) vent(2) chan(3) te(4) dans(5) la(6) nuit(7) = 7. Adding *noire* gives *Le vent chante dans la nuit noire* = 8 (the final *e* of *noire* is not counted).
- **3.** *rivière / lumière*: feminine; *chemin / matin*: masculine; *ils parlent / elles brûlent*: feminine.
- **4.** Caesura after *travers*: *Le vent souffle à travers | les branches du vieux chêne*.
