# Lesson 51 — Writing in French: Emails, Messages, and Short Texts
### Phase 4: Native Naturalness (B2 → C1)

**Competency axes trained:** ✍️ Productive fluency (primary) · 🔗 Discourse competence · 🎭 Pragmatics/Register
**Prerequisite:** Lesson 32 (professional register), Lesson 33 (argumentative connectors), Lesson 47 (full register command)
**Time investment:** One thorough read, then write something real every day for a week.

---

## 1. Why a Dedicated Writing Lesson (Intuitive Explanation)

So far, production has been mostly spoken. Writing is a different skill: nobody interrupts you, no one hears your tone, and every register choice is visible on the page. French also has strong **written conventions** (formulas, punctuation, layout) that Spanish and English speakers do not guess. Writing is also the best **deliberate-practice** tool you have: you can slow down, apply grammar consciously, and see your own errors, which feeds your spoken fluency.

---

## 2. French Punctuation and Typography (Small Details, Big Signal)

| Rule | French | English/Spanish contrast |
|---|---|---|
| Space before **? ! : ;** | Tu viens **?** Voilà **!** | No space in English; Spanish uses ¿ ¡ |
| Quotation marks | **« Bonjour »** (guillemets, with spaces) | " " in English |
| Capitalization | days, months, languages, nationalities-as-adjectives are **lowercase**: *lundi, mars, français* | Capitalized in English |
| Dates | *le 4 novembre 2026* | month-first in US English |
| Decimal / thousands | *3,5* and *1 000* (or *1.000*) | reversed in English |
| Numbers in time | *14 h 30* | 2:30 PM |

**Memory trick:** French punctuation "breathes": the double marks (? ! : ;) get a small space before them. Many learners write like English and look instantly foreign in print.

## 3. The Anatomy of an Email

### Formal / professional (building on Lesson 32)

```
Objet : Demande de report de réunion

Madame, Monsieur,

Je me permets de vous contacter au sujet de notre réunion prévue le jeudi 12 novembre.
Serait-il possible de la reporter à la semaine suivante ? Je serais disponible
le mardi ou le mercredi, selon vos préférences.

Je vous remercie par avance de votre compréhension.

Je vous prie d'agréer, Madame, Monsieur, l'expression de mes salutations distinguées.

Hernán García
```

**Structure:** **objet** (subject) → greeting → **purpose in the first sentence** → details → request/next step → thanks → closing formula → signature.

### Semi-formal (colleague you address as *vous* or *tu* professionally)

```
Objet : Point sur le dossier Martin

Bonjour Claire,

Je reviens vers toi concernant le dossier Martin. Peux-tu m'envoyer
le compte-rendu d'ici demain midi ? Merci d'avance.

Bien cordialement,
Hernán
```

### Informal (friend)

```
Salut Léa !

Ça va ? Je voulais te demander si tu étais libre samedi soir. On pourrait
dîner ensemble, ça fait longtemps !

Dis-moi, et bises,
Hernán
```

## 4. Formulas You Can Reuse (Chunks, Not Translations)

| Function | Formal | Neutral | Casual |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening | Madame, Monsieur, | Bonjour Claire, | Salut Léa ! |
| Purpose | Je me permets de vous contacter au sujet de... | Je t'écris à propos de... | Je voulais te dire... |
| Request | Pourriez-vous m'indiquer... ? | Peux-tu m'envoyer... ? | Tu peux m'envoyer... ? |
| Thanks | Je vous remercie par avance. | Merci d'avance. | Merci !! |
| Apology | Veuillez excuser ce retard. | Désolé pour le retard. | Désolé, j'ai complètement oublié ! |
| Closing | Je vous prie d'agréer... | Cordialement, / Bien cordialement, | À bientôt ! / Bises, |
| Following up | Je me permets de vous relancer. | Je reviens vers toi. | Alors, des nouvelles ? |
| Attachment | Veuillez trouver ci-joint... | Tu trouveras ci-joint... | Je te joins... |

## 5. Text Messages and Social Writing

Texting French uses a distinct, informal code. Learn to **recognize** it, then use sparingly:

| Text | Full form | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| slt / salut | salut | hi |
| cc | coucou | hey |
| tkt | t'inquiète | don't worry |
| mdr | mort de rire | lol |
| ptdr | pété de rire | lmao |
| stp / svp | s'il te / vous plaît | please |
| bcp | beaucoup | a lot |
| qq1 | quelqu'un | someone |
| pk / pq | pourquoi | why |
| jsp | je ne sais pas | idk |
| a+ / à tt | à plus / à tout à l'heure | later |
| dsl | désolé | sorry |

**Register rule:** this code belongs to texts and chats with friends. Never in professional or academic writing.

## 6. Writing a Paragraph: The French Structure

Building on Lesson 33, French paragraphs often follow:
1. **Idée principale** (topic sentence)
2. **Développement** (explanation, with connectors)
3. **Exemple** (*par exemple, notamment*)
4. **Conclusion/transition** (*ainsi, c'est pourquoi, en revanche*)

Model (opinion paragraph):

> *À mon avis, apprendre une langue étrangère exige de la régularité plutôt que du talent. En effet, quelques minutes de pratique chaque jour valent mieux qu'une longue séance hebdomadaire. Par exemple, relire dix collocations par jour permet de les retenir sans effort. Ainsi, la constance est la clé de la progression.*

## 7. Self-Correction Checklist (Deliberate Practice)

Run this list on every text you write (5 minutes, worth more than ten new grammar rules):

1. **Register:** tu or vous? Consistent all the way through?
2. **Verbs:** tenses logical (passé composé vs. imparfait)? Auxiliary être/avoir correct? Agreement with être?
3. **Gender and agreement:** articles, adjectives, past participles after *être*.
4. **Pronouns:** *y*, *en*, direct/indirect objects in the right position.
5. **Negation:** *ne ... pas* complete in formal writing.
6. **Prepositions and collocations:** *prendre une décision*, *depuis* vs. *pendant*.
7. **Punctuation:** spaces before ? ! : ; and the use of « ».
8. **Connectors:** does each sentence connect to the previous one?
9. **Read aloud:** if it sounds awkward spoken, it is probably awkward written.

**Technique:** write, **wait one day**, reread, correct. Errors you miss today become visible tomorrow.

---

## 8. Mental Model: Writing as Slow Speech with a Visible Dress Code

Think of writing as speech slowed down with the **dress code printed on the page**. In speech, tone and context forgive small mismatches. In writing, the register is naked: a *tu* in a formal email, or an *objet* missing from a professional message, is visible permanently. The upside is control: you can apply every grammar rule you have studied consciously, which makes writing the fastest way to convert **knowing** into **automatic habits**.

---

## 9. Common Mistakes

**Spanish speakers:** using inverted punctuation (¿ ¡) in French; omitting the *objet*; copying Spanish email openers (*Estimado Señor…*) literally as *Cher Monsieur* in situations where *Madame, Monsieur,* is better; capitalizing days and months.

**English speakers:** forgetting the space before ? ! : ;; making emails too brief and direct (French business writing is more formal and elaborate); missing *objet*; using *Hi* equivalents (*Salut*) too early in professional contexts.

**Both:** translating idioms and closings literally ("Best regards" → *Meilleures salutations* is understood but not the usual choice; use *Cordialement*).

## 10. Progressively Harder Writing Tasks

1. **(Informal, 3 lines)** Invite a friend to dinner on Saturday.
2. **(Neutral, 5 lines)** Ask a colleague (*tu*) to send you a document by tomorrow.
3. **(Formal, 6–8 lines)** Email a company to ask for information about a product, with *objet* and formal closing.
4. **(Formal, 8–10 lines)** Postpone a meeting and propose two alternative dates, using conditional politeness.
5. **(Opinion paragraph, 6–8 sentences)** "Pourquoi j'apprends le français" using at least two argumentative connectors, one subjunctive trigger, and one collocation from Lesson 49.
