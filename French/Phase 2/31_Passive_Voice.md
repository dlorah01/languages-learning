# Lesson 31 — The Passive Voice: Formation and When Native Speakers Avoid It
### Phase 2: Expansion (A2 → B1)

**Competency axes trained:** 🧩 Morphosyntax (primary) · 🎭 Pragmatics/Register (secondary)
**Prerequisite:** Lesson 7 (être), Lesson 12 (past participle agreement)
**Time investment:** One read-through — this is a shorter lesson, since passive voice is less central to spoken French than you might expect

---

## 1. Why This Lesson Comes With a Twist (Intuitive Explanation)

Here's something genuinely important for a native-like feel: **French uses the passive voice far less than English does**, and less than Spanish's "se" passive in many contexts. Native French speakers actively prefer active-voice or "on" (impersonal "one/they") constructions in speech. So this lesson teaches you the passive's mechanics — because you'll need to recognize it in writing/news — but immediately teaches you the more natural alternatives real speakers reach for instead.

---

## 2. Formation: Être + Past Participle (agreement always applies)

**Formula:** être (conjugated in the tense you need) + past participle (agreeing with the subject, exactly like Lesson 12's être-verb agreement)

> **La lettre est écrite par Marie.** — "The letter is written by Marie." (present passive)
> **Le livre a été publié en 2020.** — "The book was published in 2020." (passé composé passive)
> **Les fenêtres seront réparées demain.** — "The windows will be repaired tomorrow." (future passive)

**Agreement is mandatory here** — since être is always the auxiliary, the past participle always agrees with the subject, in every tense, no exceptions (unlike avoir-verbs in Lesson 12, which never agree).

---

## 3. The Agent: "Par" (by)

The person/thing doing the action, if mentioned, follows **par**:
> **Le tableau a été peint par un artiste célèbre.** — "The painting was painted by a famous artist."

(Occasionally "de" is used instead of "par" for certain static/emotional-state passives — "il est aimé de tous" — "he is loved by everyone" — a stylistic nuance you'll absorb through exposure rather than rule.)

---

## 4. Why Native Speakers Often Avoid It: Two Preferred Alternatives

**Alternative 1 — Active voice, when the agent matters:**
> Instead of: La lettre a été écrite par Marie.
> Prefer (more natural in speech): **Marie a écrit la lettre.**

**Alternative 2 — "On" (impersonal), when the agent is unknown/unimportant — this is the single most important French-specific pattern to internalize here:**
> Instead of: Le français est parlé ici.
> Prefer (much more natural/spoken): **On parle français ici.** — "French is spoken here" / literally "one speaks French here"

**"On"** functions grammatically like "il/elle" (third person singular) but semantically covers "we" (extremely common in casual speech, replacing "nous"), "people in general," or an unspecified "someone" — making it the natural, idiomatic substitute for a huge range of English/Spanish passive or impersonal constructions.

> **On dit que...** — "It is said that... / People say that..."
> **On m'a dit que...** — "I was told that..." (literally "someone told me that")
> **Ici, on mange bien.** — "Here, people eat well" / colloquially "Here, we eat well."

---

## 5. Mental Model: The Passive as a Formal/Written Tool, "On" as the Spoken Workhorse

Picture the true être-passive as a formal, slightly bureaucratic register — you'll see it constantly in news headlines, official documents, and academic writing ("la loi a été votée..."). But in everyday spoken French, native speakers reach for **"on"** or simple active voice almost automatically. As a learner aiming for native-like naturalness (your stated goal), prioritize getting comfortable with "on" over over-practicing the formal passive — this is a genuine register/frequency insight, not just a grammar point.

---

## 6. Common Mistakes

**Spanish speakers:** over-relying on the passive because Spanish's "se pasiva" (se habla español) is extremely common and directly invites a literal être-passive translation — but the far more natural French equivalent is "on parle français," not a literal être-passive construction.

**English speakers:** over-using the être-passive by direct habit transfer from English, which uses the passive voice far more freely and in far more casual registers than French does.

---

## 7. Progressively Harder Examples

1. Le musée a été construit en 1900. — "The museum was built in 1900." (formal passive, appropriate here — historical fact)
2. On parle espagnol et français dans cette région. — "Spanish and French are spoken in this region." (natural spoken alternative)
3. Cette décision a été prise par le gouvernement. — "This decision was made by the government." (formal/news register passive)
4. On m'a volé mon téléphone hier. — "My phone was stolen yesterday" / "Someone stole my phone yesterday." (on, not passive — natural spoken French)
5. Le rapport sera terminé avant vendredi. — "The report will be finished before Friday." (future passive, appropriate in professional/written context)

## 8. Speaking Practice

Take 3 English passive sentences ("The car was fixed," "The window was broken," "The email was sent") and produce BOTH the formal être-passive AND the more natural "on" version for each, aloud.
