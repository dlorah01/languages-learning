## 1. Why Idioms Now, and Why "Tier 1" (Intuitive Explanation)

You'll learn idioms throughout the rest of this program, organized by frequency tier so you invest effort where it pays off fastest. Tier 1 idioms are used so constantly in everyday French that not knowing them makes native speech sound oddly bare, even if your grammar is otherwise excellent.

## 2. Tier 1 Idioms: Everyday Essentials

| Idiom | Literal | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Ça marche. | "That walks." | "That works / Sounds good" (agreement) |
| Ça dépend. | "That depends." | "It depends" |
| Ça vaut le coup. | "That's worth the blow." | "It's worth it" |
| C'est pas grave. | "It's not serious." | "No worries / It's fine" |
| Tant pis. | "So much worse." | "Too bad / Oh well" |
| Tant mieux. | "So much better." | "Good, that's great" |
| Avoir la flemme. | "To have the laziness." | "To not feel like doing something" (very casual) |
| Ça me dit rien. | "That says nothing to me." | "I don't feel like it / I'm not interested" |
| Je n'en peux plus. | "I can no more of it." | "I can't take it anymore" |
| Il y a un problème. | "There is a problem." | (Lesson 6's il y a — flagging it as a fixed high-frequency chunk) |

## 5. Mental Model: Idioms as Pre-Assembled Furniture

Grammar rules let you build any sentence from raw materials, but native speakers don't build every sentence from scratch — they reach for **pre-assembled chunks** (idioms, fixed expressions) constantly, the way you'd grab ready-made furniture instead of cutting your own wood every time. Learning these chunks as whole units, rather than analyzing their individual words, is exactly how native speakers store and retrieve them — and it's why literal translation so often fails for idioms (Lesson 17's "avoir la flemme" doesn't mean anything if you translate word-by-word).

---

## 6. Common Mistakes

**Spanish speakers:** trying to translate idioms literally word-for-word instead of learning them as fixed chunks (a natural instinct when two languages share so much vocabulary, but idioms are precisely where literal translation breaks down in any language pair).

**English speakers:** the same literal-translation trap, often even stronger, since English idioms rarely have transparent French equivalents (e.g., "it's raining cats and dogs" has zero relation to "il pleut des cordes" — "it's raining ropes").

---

## 7. Progressively Harder Examples

1. On se voit à quelle heure? — Vers 18h, ça marche? — "What time are we meeting? — Around 6, does that work?"
2. J'ai la flemme de cuisiner ce soir. — "I don't feel like cooking tonight."
3. Il a perdu son travail du jour au lendemain. — "He lost his job overnight."
4. Sans blague, tu as vraiment fait ça? — "No kidding, you actually did that?"
5. Ça dépend, mais je pense que ça vaut le coup d'essayer. — "It depends, but I think it's worth trying."
