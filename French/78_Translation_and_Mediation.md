# Lesson 78 — Translation and Mediation: Moving Between Spanish, English, and French
### Phase 5: Mastery & Maintenance (C1 → C2+)

**Competency axes trained:** 📚 Lexicon · 🔗 Discourse competence · 🎭 Pragmatics · 🧩 Morphosyntax
**Prerequisite:** Lesson 49 (collocations), Lesson 63 (variation), Lesson 71 (thinking in French), Lesson 76 (language in society)
**Time investment:** One read, then a weekly 20-minute translation/mediation exercise.

---

## 1. Why This Lesson Exists (Intuitive Explanation)

You speak Spanish natively, English at a high level, and French in progress. That trilingual position is a rare asset: you can **compare** three languages and notice what each one does differently. Translation and mediation are the training ground for this noticing.

There is an apparent tension with Lesson 71 ("think in French, stop translating"). The resolution is simple:

- **As a production strategy**, silent word-by-word translation is slow and produces calques. Avoid it.
- **As an analytical tool and a professional skill**, deliberate translation is powerful: it reveals structural differences, trains precision, and prepares you for real situations (explaining a French document to a Spanish-speaking relative, summarizing an English article for French colleagues, interpreting at a meeting).

The European framework (CEFR Companion Volume) names this skill **mediation** (*médiation*): conveying meaning between people or languages, not word by word but **idea by idea**.

---

## 2. The Core Principle: Translate Meaning, Not Words

A good translation answers one question: **"How would a native speaker say this idea in French?"**

| Source | Word-for-word (bad) | Natural French |
|---|---|---|
| English: "I'm looking forward to it." | *Je regarde en avant à ça.* | *J'ai hâte.* |
| English: "It's raining cats and dogs." | *Il pleut des chats et des chiens.* | *Il pleut des cordes.* |
| Spanish: *Me da igual.* | *Ça me donne égal.* | *Ça m'est égal.* |
| Spanish: *Tengo ganas de verte.* | *J'ai des envies de te voir.* | *J'ai envie de te voir.* / *J'ai hâte de te voir.* |
| English: "I'll call you back." | *Je t'appellerai en arrière.* | *Je te rappelle.* |

Everything else in this lesson is a set of techniques for doing this reliably.

## 3. A Classic Toolkit: Seven Translation Procedures

The French-Canadian scholars **Vinay and Darbelnet** (1958) described seven procedures that remain a standard framework, from the most literal to the most free.

| # | Procedure | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Emprunt** (borrowing) | keep the source word | *le week-end*, *un sandwich* |
| 2 | **Calque** | translate the parts literally into a new expression | *gratte-ciel* (skyscraper); *fin de semaine* in Québec |
| 3 | **Traduction littérale** | word-for-word, when it works | *Il a mangé une pomme.* ("He ate an apple.") |
| 4 | **Transposition** | change the grammatical category | *He limped across the street* → *Il a traversé la rue **en boitant*** (verb → gerund) |
| 5 | **Modulation** | change the point of view | *It's not difficult* → *C'est facile*; "You're welcome" → *Je vous en prie* |
| 6 | **Équivalence** | different wording, same function | "Beware of the dog" → *Chien méchant* / *Attention, chien méchant* |
| 7 | **Adaptation** | replace a cultural reality with an equivalent | "Dear Sir or Madam" → *Madame, Monsieur,* |

**How to use it:** when a literal translation sounds odd, climb the ladder: try transposition, then modulation, then equivalence. Often one of these is the natural form.

## 4. Spanish → French: What Transfers and What Doesn't

### 4.1 What transfers well (use it)
| Area | Example |
|---|---|
| **Vocabulary (about 75% shared Latin base)** | *organización → organisation*, *importante → important* |
| **Verb-framed motion** | *Entró corriendo* → *Il est entré en courant* (Spanish and French both put the path in the verb and the manner in a gerund) |
| **Subjunctive logic** | *Quiero que vengas* → *Je veux que tu viennes* |
| **Imperfect vs. preterite** | the imparfait / passé composé distinction maps well (Lesson 13) |
| **Reflexive structures** | *levantarse* → *se lever* |
| **Gender** | often shared, with exceptions (*el problema → le problème*, *la sangre → le sang*, *el mar → la mer*, *la leche → le lait*) |

### 4.2 Where Spanish misleads you
| Spanish | French | Trap |
|---|---|---|
| *Tengo calor* | *J'ai chaud* | same "have" structure; never *je suis chaud* (which has a different, sexual connotation) |
| *Me gusta* | *J'aime* / *Ça me plaît* | structure inverted |
| *Extraño a mi familia* | *Ma famille me manque* | inverted subject/object |
| *Hace dos años* | *Il y a deux ans* | no *hace* |
| *Se me cayó el vaso* | *J'ai fait tomber le verre* / *Le verre m'a échappé* | French has no "accidental se" |
| *Se vende* | *À vendre* | impersonal |
| *Está prohibido fumar* | *Défense de fumer* / *Il est interdit de fumer* | |
| *Dijo que vendría* | *Il a dit qu'il viendrait* | similar; watch sequences |
| *Estoy de acuerdo* | *Je suis d'accord* | no article |
| *Pensar de / en* | *Penser à / Penser de* | prepositions differ |
| *Esperar* (wait / hope) | *attendre* / *espérer* | two verbs in French |
| *Actualmente* | *actuellement* (currently) | same meaning, good friend |
| *Embarazada* | *enceinte* | *embarrassée* = embarrassed |
| *Librería* | *librairie* (bookshop) | *bibliothèque* = library |
| *Constipado* | *enrhumé* | *constipé* = constipated |

### 4.3 Structures to retrain
- **Subject pronouns are obligatory** in French (*Je pars*, not *Pars*).
- **Object pronouns** have fixed order and positions (Lesson 24).
- **Prepositions** after verbs vary (Lesson 50).
- **The partitive** (*du, de la, des*) has no Spanish equivalent (*Je bois de l'eau*).
- **Questions** use inversion or *est-ce que* (Lesson 9).
- **Adjective placement** is similar but not identical.

## 5. English → French: What Changes

| English pattern | French reality | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Satellite-framed motion** ("run into the room") | verb-framed: path in the verb | "He ran into the room" → *Il est entré dans la pièce en courant* |
| **Noun stacks** ("database migration tool") | prepositions required | *un outil de migration de base de données* |
| **Phrasal verbs** ("give up", "carry on") | single verbs or other structures | *abandonner*, *continuer* |
| **-ing forms as nouns** ("Swimming is fun") | infinitive or noun | *Nager, c'est amusant.* / *La natation est un plaisir.* |
| **"Get" as a universal verb** | many verbs | "get tired" = *se fatiguer*, "get a job" = *trouver un emploi*, "get it" = *comprendre* |
| **"Be" + age/state** ("I'm 25", "I'm cold") | *avoir* | *J'ai 25 ans*, *J'ai froid* |
| **Progressive** ("I'm working") | present (or *être en train de*) | *Je travaille.* / *Je suis en train de travailler.* |
| **Auxiliary "do"** ("Do you know?") | inversion or *est-ce que* | *Savez-vous... ?* |
| **Short, direct commands/requests** ("Send me the file.") | softer forms | *Pourriez-vous m'envoyer le fichier ?* |
| **Contractions and informal tone** | register choices | *tu/vous* decision |
| **Passive voice** very common | less natural; *on* or active | "It is said that..." → *On dit que...* |
| **Possessives with body parts** ("He raised his hand") | definite article | *Il a levé la main.* |

### Typical false friends English ↔ French
*actually/actuellement, eventually/éventuellement, library/librairie, sensible/sensé, college/collège, lecture/lecture (reading), location/location (rental), assist/assister (attend), support/supporter (bear), sympathetic/sympathique (nice), demand/demander (ask), resume/résumé (summary), injury/injure (insult), pretend/prétendre (claim), prejudice/préjudice (harm).*

## 6. Register and Culture in Translation

Translation fails more often through **tone** than through grammar.

| Issue | Example |
|---|---|
| **Tu / vous** | English "you" does not mark it: choose by context (Lesson 16) |
| **Politeness level** | English "Could you…?" ≈ French *Pourriez-vous…* (formal) / *Tu peux… ?* (casual) |
| **Directness** | an English "Please do X by Friday" may need softening in French (*Pourriez-vous… d'ici vendredi ?*) |
| **Humor and irony** | wordplay rarely survives; find an equivalent effect instead (Lesson 65) |
| **Cultural references** | explain, replace, or keep with a gloss depending on the audience |
| **Titles and forms of address** | *Mr. Smith* → *Monsieur Smith*; *Dear John* → *Cher John* (informal) / *Bonjour John* |
| **Dates, numbers, units** | *5/11/2026* is 5 November in France; decimal comma; 24-hour clock |

## 7. Mediation: Beyond Word-for-Word Translation

CEFR mediation covers several skills you may need in real life.

| Skill | What it means | French phrases |
|---|---|---|
| **Relaying specific information** | passing on key facts | *En résumé, il dit que..., L'essentiel, c'est que...* |
| **Summarizing** | condensing a text or talk | *Pour résumer..., En gros..., En une phrase :...* |
| **Explaining** | making a concept clear | *Autrement dit..., Concrètement..., C'est un peu comme...* |
| **Interpreting (consecutive)** | translating a speaker's turns | *Il dit que..., Elle demande si..., Il précise que...* |
| **Facilitating a conversation** | helping two people understand each other | *Si je comprends bien, vous voulez dire que..., Je traduis pour vous :...* |
| **Clarifying** | checking meaning | *Pardon, voulez-vous dire que... ?* |

### Interpreting etiquette
- Use the **first person** when interpreting (*Je voudrais…*, speaking as the person) in professional interpreting, or **third person** (*Il dit que…*) when mediating informally. Be clear which you are using.
- Keep **sentence-sized chunks** and take **notes** (key words, numbers, names).
- Signal uncertainty honestly: *Je ne suis pas sûr du terme exact, il parle de...*
- Never add your opinion.

## 8. Machine Translation: A Tool to Use Wisely

Machine translation (MT) is excellent for drafts and for checking comprehension, but it still makes characteristic errors in French:

| Typical MT weakness | What to check |
|---|---|
| **Tu/vous** | context-free; verify the register |
| **Gender and agreement** with unknown referents | check participles and adjectives (Lesson 70) |
| **Idioms and collocations** | literal output (Lessons 49, 73) |
| **Homophones and ambiguity** | wrong word chosen |
| **Terminology** | field-specific terms (law, medicine, tech) |
| **Subjunctive and mood** | sometimes dropped or misapplied |
| **Tone** | too stiff or too casual |
| **Names, titles, culture-specific items** | mistranslated or over-translated |

**Productive uses for a learner:**
1. **Compare:** write your own French first, then compare with MT. Study differences, not just the "correct" answer.
2. **Back-translation test:** translate your French into Spanish/English with MT. If the meaning shifts, your French may be unclear.
3. **Post-editing practice:** take an MT output and improve it for naturalness. This is a real professional skill (*post-édition*).
4. **Never submit MT output for formal documents without review.**

## 9. Mental Model: Three Rooms, Two Doors

Imagine three rooms (Spanish, English, French). **Doors** between them let you carry ideas, not furniture. If you try to move a Spanish sofa (a structure) into the French room, it won't fit through the door. You break it down into the idea (a seat for several people) and rebuild a French *canapé* inside. Your job as a translator or mediator is to carry the **idea** through the door and **rebuild it with local materials**.

## 10. Common Mistakes

**Spanish speakers:** over-trusting cognates (*realizar → réaliser* only in some senses; *carpeta* is *dossier*, not *carpette*); copying Spanish word order or subject dropping; using the gerund for everything; translating Spanish "se" constructions literally; forgetting the partitive; mistaking *tú/usted* mapping with *tu/vous* (they do not overlap perfectly, especially across Latin American countries).

**English speakers:** translating phrasal verbs, noun stacks, and "get" literally; keeping English politeness patterns; ignoring gender and agreement in translated text; using passive too often.

**Both:** translating idioms literally; ignoring register; translating without understanding the whole sentence first; not reading the translation aloud to check naturalness; trusting MT without verification.

## 11. Progressively Harder Practice

1. **Triangulation table (20 min):** pick 10 everyday sentences in Spanish. Write English and French versions, then mark which structures changed (transposition, modulation, etc.).
2. **Back-translation test:** translate a paragraph from English to French (by hand), then use MT to translate your French back to English. Compare with the original.
3. **Modulation drill:** rewrite ten negative English/Spanish sentences using a positive French equivalent (*not difficult → facile*; *no tardes → dépêche-toi*).
4. **Register shift:** translate the same short email into French twice: formal and informal.
5. **Summary mediation:** read a short English or Spanish article and give a 1-minute oral summary in French.
6. **Consecutive interpreting practice:** have a partner speak 3 sentences in Spanish or English; you restate them in French with note-taking, then check accuracy.
7. **Post-editing:** take an MT French translation of a 150-word text and improve it. List the errors you fixed and classify them (register, agreement, collocation, idiom).
