# Lesson 84 — Deep Dive 4: Etymology and Word Families
### Phase 5: Mastery & Maintenance — Optional Deep Dive

**Competency axes trained:** 📚 Lexicon (primary) · 📖 Reading · 🌍 Cultural/literary competence · 🔊 Phonology (sound history)
**Prerequisite:** Lesson 2 (history of French), Lesson 10 (word families), Lesson 39 (prefixed verb families), Lesson 53 (building a field lexicon)
**Time investment:** One read, then a four-week deep-dive cycle (Lesson 80, Section 7).

---

## 1. Why Etymology Is a Superpower for You (Intuitive Explanation)

You are an analytical, systems-oriented learner with Spanish as a native language and strong English. French vocabulary sits **between** them historically:

- **Spanish and French** both descend from Latin (they are sister languages). Their words follow **regular sound laws**, so you can predict French from Spanish, and the reverse, with surprising accuracy.
- **English** absorbed thousands of French words after 1066 and thousands more directly from Latin and Greek; much of its formal vocabulary is a **cousin** of French.

This means you can learn French vocabulary **as a system of conversions and families**, not as isolated words. Etymology also explains many "irrational" spellings (silent letters, circumflex accents, *h aspiré*) and helps you recognize hundreds of learned words in reading.

**Goal of this lesson:** give you the conversion rules, the word-family patterns, and the layers of French vocabulary so that new words become *predictable*, not just memorized.

## 2. The Layers of French Vocabulary

French vocabulary is like geological strata: each layer entered at a different time and sounds different.

| Layer | When | Examples | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Gaulish (Celtic)** | before Roman conquest | *chemin, chêne, charrue, alouette, bruyère* | few words, rural and everyday |
| **2. Popular Latin (inherited words)** | continuous evolution from spoken Latin | *eau, œil, fenêtre, cheval, mer, père, feu, loup* | very common, **heavily changed** in sound |
| **3. Germanic (Frankish)** | 5th–9th centuries | *guerre, blanc, bleu, gris, riche, jardin, honte, hache, garder, gagner* | war, color, law, daily life |
| **4. Learned borrowings from Latin and Greek** | Middle Ages to today | *aquatique, fragile, optique, hospitalier, démocratie, téléphone* | formal, scientific, **close to the original** |
| **5. Italian** | Renaissance | *balcon, banque, carnaval, opéra, soldat* | arts, finance, war |
| **6. Arabic** (often via Spanish, Italian, or Latin) | Middle Ages | *algèbre, alcool, chiffre, zéro, sucre, jupe, matelas, magasin* | science, trade, textiles |
| **7. English** | 18th century → today | *week-end, tunnel, budget, parking, e-mail* | sport, technology, business (Lesson 76) |
| **8. Other languages** | various | *chocolat, tomate* (Nahuatl via Spanish), *café* (Arabic via Turkish/Italian), *kiosque* (Turkish/Persian) | trade, exploration |

**Key observation:** the **same Latin word** often entered French twice, once by oral inheritance (layer 2, transformed) and once by learned borrowing (layer 4, close to the original). These pairs are called **doublets** (see Section 5).

## 3. Regular Sound Changes: From Latin to French (and Spanish)

French and Spanish both changed Latin sounds, but in **different but regular ways**. Learning the main correspondences lets you predict cognates.

### 3.1 Table of key correspondences

| Latin | Spanish | French | Examples (Lat → Sp → Fr) |
|---|---|---|---|
| initial **f-** | **h-** (silent) | **f-** | *facere* → *hacer* → *faire*; *farina* → *harina* → *farine*; *fumus* → *humo* → *fumée* |
| **-ct-** | **-ch-** | **-it-** | *nocte* → *noche* → *nuit*; *factum* → *hecho* → *fait*; *lacte* → *leche* → *lait*; *octo* → *ocho* → *huit* |
| initial **pl-, cl-, fl-** | **ll-** | **pl-, cl-, fl-** | *plenus* → *lleno* → *plein*; *clavis* → *llave* → *clé*; *flamma* → *llama* → *flamme*; *pluvia* → *lluvia* → *pluie* |
| initial **s + consonant** | **es-** | **é-** (earlier *es-*) | *schola* → *escuela* → *école*; *stella* → *estrella* → *étoile*; *Hispania* → *España* → *Espagne*; *status* → *estado* → *état* |
| **-alis / -ale** | **-al** | **-el** (popular) or **-al** (learned) | *mortalis* → *mortal* → *mortel*; *legalis* → *legal* → *légal* / *loyal* |
| **-tionem** | **-ción** | **-tion** | *nationem* → *nación* → *nation*; *actionem* → *acción* → *action* |
| **-tatem** | **-dad** | **-té** | *civitatem* → *ciudad* → *cité*; *libertatem* → *libertad* → *liberté*; *veritatem* → *verdad* → *vérité* |
| **-osus** | **-oso** | **-eux** | *famosus* → *famoso* → *fameux*; *curiosus* → *curioso* → *curieux* |
| **-arius** | **-ario / -ero** | **-aire / -ier** | *necessarius* → *necesario* → *nécessaire*; *primarius* → *primero* → *premier* |
| **-mente** (adverb) | **-mente** | **-ment** | *rápidamente* → *rapidement* |
| initial **ca-** | **ca-** | **cha-** | *cantare* → *cantar* → *chanter*; *caballus* → *caballo* → *cheval*; *castellum* → *castillo* → *château*; *carum* → *caro* → *cher* |
| **-ticus/-icus** | **-ico** | **-ique** | *politicus* → *político* → *politique* |
| **-orem** | **-or** | **-eur** | *professorem* → *profesor* → *professeur* |
| **-ura** | **-ura** | **-ure** | *cultura* → *cultura* → *culture* |
| **-antia/-entia** | **-ancia/-encia** | **-ance/-ence** | *importantia* → *importancia* → *importance* |
| **-izare** | **-izar** | **-iser** | *organizar* → *organiser* |

### 3.2 Other important changes in French
- **Loss of unstressed final syllables:** Latin *mare*, *porta*, *amicus* → French *mer*, *porte*, *ami* (the final syllables vanished).
- **Loss of intervocalic consonants:** *aetatem* → *âge*, *rotundus* → *rond*, *vita* → *vie*, *rege(m)* → *roi*.
- **Diphthongization and nasalization:** the source of vowel complexity (Lessons 1, 4): *bonum* → *bon* [bɔ̃], *ventum* → *vent* [vɑ̃].
- **The vanished *s* before consonants** (marked by the circumflex): *hospitalem* → *hôpital*, *festa* → *fête*, *forestem* → *forêt*, *insula* → *île*, *testa* → *tête*, *bestia* → *bête*, *magister* → *maître*, *costa* → *côte*, *crista* → *crête*. In Spanish the *s* remained (*hospital*, *fiesta*, *foresta*, *isla*, *testa*, *bestia*, *maestro*, *costa*), and English kept it in earlier-borrowed words (*hospital*, *feast*, *forest*, *isle*, *beast*, *master*, *coast*).

> **Circumflex as detector:** when you see ^ in French, think "a lost *s*" and find the cousin in Spanish or English (*fenêtre*: *fenestra*, cf. "defenestrate"; *hôte*: *hospes*, cf. "host").

### 3.3 The *h aspiré* explained
French words beginning with a "silent but blocking" *h* (*le héros*, *la honte*, *les haricots*, *la haine*, *la hache*) usually come from **Germanic** words whose initial /h/ was once pronounced (cf. English *hate*, *hatchet*). That is why liaison and elision are blocked before them (Lesson 5). Words with "mute *h*" (*l'homme*, *l'hôpital*, *l'heure*) come from Latin, where *h* had already disappeared.

## 4. Using Spanish as a Decoding Key

### 4.1 Spanish → French conversions that work (very high yield)

| Spanish pattern | French pattern | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **-ción** | **-tion** | información → information; organización → organisation |
| **-sión** | **-sion** | decisión → décision; televisión → télévision |
| **-dad** | **-té** | universidad → université; realidad → réalité; actividad → activité |
| **-oso** | **-eux** | peligroso → dangereux (not cognate); delicioso → délicieux |
| **-mente** | **-ment** | finalmente → finalement |
| **-ico** | **-ique** | histórico → historique; técnico → technique |
| **-ario** | **-aire** | necesario → nécessaire; ordinario → ordinaire |
| **-or** (agent) | **-eur** | director → directeur; actor → acteur |
| **-ancia/-encia** | **-ance/-ence** | importancia → importance; experiencia → expérience |
| **-ismo / -ista** | **-isme / -iste** | turismo → tourisme; artista → artiste |
| **-ura** | **-ure** | cultura → culture; aventura → aventure |
| **-ar (verbs)** | **-er** | estudiar → étudier; cantar → chanter (note the *ch-*) |
| **-izar** | **-iser** | organizar → organiser |
| **-ble** | **-ble** | posible → possible |
| **initial es-** | **é-/es-** | escuela → école; espacio → espace; estudiar → étudier |
| **-ll-** (Latin pl/cl/fl) | **pl/cl/fl** | lleno → plein; llave → clé; llama → flamme |
| **h-** (Latin f-) | **f-** | hacer → faire; hijo → fils; hierro → fer; hablar → *parler* (different: *fabulari* → Sp *hablar*; French *parler* from *parabolare*) |

### 4.2 Be careful: when the shortcut fails
| Case | Example |
|---|---|
| **Semantic drift (false friends)** | *librería/librairie* (bookshop, not library: *bibliothèque*), *carpeta/carpette*, *embarazada/embarrassée*, *constipado/constipé*, *actual/actuel* (OK), *largo/large* (long vs wide: *long/large*), *asistir/assister*, *éxito/exit* (success: *succès*) |
| **Different Latin source for the same concept** | Sp *hablar* (fabulari) vs Fr *parler* (parabolare); Sp *comer* (comedere) vs Fr *manger* (manducare); Sp *buscar* vs Fr *chercher*; Sp *mirar* vs Fr *regarder*; Sp *llegar* vs Fr *arriver*; Sp *tomar* vs Fr *prendre* |
| **Germanic words in French with no Spanish cognate** | *guerre* (Sp *guerra* actually borrowed from the same Germanic source), *blanc* (Sp *blanco*: same source), *riche* (Sp *rico*: same) |
| **Gender differences** | *el árbol → l'arbre* (m), *la leche → le lait* (m), *la sangre → le sang* (m), *el mar → la mer* (f), *la sal → le sel* (m) |

## 5. Doublets: One Latin Word, Two French Words

When Latin words entered French twice, the **popular** (transformed) and **learned** (close-to-Latin) forms often have **different meanings**. Recognizing doublets lets you decode formal vocabulary from common words.

| Popular word | Learned word | Latin source |
|---|---|---|
| **frêle** (frail) | **fragile** | *fragilis* |
| **hôtel** | **hôpital** | *hospitalem / hospitale* |
| **entier** (whole) | **intègre** (upright), *intégral* | *integrum* |
| **loyal** | **légal** | *legalis* |
| **chose** (thing) | **cause** | *causa* |
| **livrer** (deliver) | **libérer** (free) | *liberare* |
| **poison** | **potion** | *potionem* |
| **étroit** (narrow) | **strict** | *strictum* |
| **écouter** | **ausculter** (medical) | *auscultare* |
| **raide** (stiff) | **rigide** | *rigidus* |
| **sûr** (sure) | **sécurité** | *securus* |
| **cher** (dear) | **cher/caritatif?** (*carus*, cf. *charité*) | *carus* |

> **Use:** if you know the common word, you can guess the formal one by its shape: *eau* → *aquatique, aqueduc, aquarium*; *œil* → *oculaire, oculiste*; *main* → *manuel, manuscrit, manœuvre*; *dent* → *dentiste, dentaire, dentition*; *doigt* → *digital*; *jour* → *journal, journée, quotidien (cotidianus)*, *diurne*; *mer* → *marin, maritime, marée*; *père* → *paternel, paternité*; *frère* → *fraternel, fraternité*; *roi* → *royal, régner, règne*; *cœur* → *cordial, courage (cor)*, *cardiaque (Greek)*; *pied* → *pédestre, pédale, piéton, pédicure*; *feu* → *foyer, focal*; *cheval* → *cavalier, cavalerie, chevalier*; *ville* → *village, villa*; *nuit* → *nocturne*.

**Reading benefit:** newspapers, academic texts, and legal documents (Lessons 74, 81) use the learned layer. Mapping each common word to its learned "cousin" multiplies your reading vocabulary.

## 8. Etymology and Spelling: Reasons Behind the Madness

| Spelling feature | Etymological reason | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Silent final consonants | Latin endings still written | *petit, grand, temps, doigt, corps* |
| Silent *p* in *temps, corps, doigt* | scholarly re-Latinizing of the spelling | *tempus, corpus, digitus* |
| *th, ph, ch, rh, y* | Greek letters | *théâtre, philosophie, chaos, rhume, système* |
| *oi* for [wa] | evolution of Latin *e/ei* | *roi, loi, moi* |
| Double consonants | marks short/stressed vowels and Latin assimilations | *appeler (ad-pellare), occuper, difficile, accepter* |
| Circumflex | lost *s*, or long vowel, or distinction | *forêt, île, âge, sûr vs sur, mûr vs mur* |
| Cedilla *ç* | soft *c* before *a, o, u* | *français, garçon, reçu* |
| *ae/oe* ligature *œ* | Latin/Greek diphthong | *œuf, cœur, sœur, œil* |

Etymology does not make spelling easy, but it makes it **explicable**, and explanations are easier to remember than arbitrary facts.

## 9. Mental Model: Archaeology and Genetic Cousins

Treat vocabulary as **archaeology**: each word is a layered artifact, with older stone (Latin or Gaulish) and newer plaster (learned or borrowed layers). The circumflex is a scar; silent letters are fossils; doublets are twins who grew up in different environments. Treat related languages as **cousins who share grandparents** (Latin): you can often recognize a cousin by their face (the word shape) once you learn the family resemblance rules (the sound laws in Section 3). Rather than memorizing 10,000 words, you learn 30 rules and 500 roots, and the rest follows.

## 10. Common Mistakes

**Spanish speakers:**
- Trusting the conversion rules without checking meaning (*librairie, éventuellement, actuellement* in English contexts, *constipé, embarrassée*).
- Forgetting that French often uses a **different Latin word** for a common concept (*parler, manger, regarder, arriver, chercher, prendre*).
- Spelling the French word with Spanish endings (*-ción* for *-tion*; *universidad* for *université*).
- Missing gender changes (*le sang*, *le lait*, *la mer*, *le sel*).

**English speakers:**
- Assuming an English-French cognate has the same meaning (*actuellement, éventuellement, sensible, assister, support, library, lecture*).
- Using the Anglo-Norman layer wrongly: *forest* (OK) but modern French is *forêt* (with a circumflex).
- Ignoring the learned/popular distinction (using a learned word where the common one is natural: *aquatique* vs. *eau*).

**Both:**
- Over-generalizing from similar-looking words (*terreur/terre*).
- Memorizing word lists without families.
- Neglecting pronunciation of long Latinate words (stress and rhythm, Lesson 59).

## 11. A Four-Week Deep-Dive Plan

| Week | Focus | Activities |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Map** | sound laws and conversions | learn Section 3 and 4 tables; list 40 Spanish→French conversions you can predict; test yourself by predicting French words from Spanish before looking them up |

For serious study, comparing three entries (Spanish, French, English) of the same Latin root is highly instructive.
