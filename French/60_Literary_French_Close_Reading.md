# Lesson 60 — Literary French: Reading a Short Story Closely
### Phase 4: Native Naturalness (B2 → C1)

**Competency axes trained:** 📖 Reading (primary) · 🌍 Cultural/literary competence · 📚 Lexicon
**Prerequisite:** Lesson 13 (passé composé vs. imparfait), Lesson 55 (reading ladder, passé simple recognition), Lesson 58 (literary moods)
**Time investment:** 60–90 minutes for the close reading, then ongoing literary reading.

---

## 1. Why Literature Needs Its Own Lesson (Intuitive Explanation)

Literary French is not "harder French," it is **French with different defaults**: a different past tense, freer word order, richer imagery, and sentences built for rhythm rather than speed. If you only know spoken and journalistic French, a novel can feel like a foreign dialect.

This lesson gives you a **toolkit for close reading**: a short original text written in classic narrative style (so you can study the techniques without a copyright question), followed by a systematic method you can apply to any author: Maupassant, Camus, Duras, Ernaux, or a contemporary novel.

---

## 2. What Changes in Literary Narration

| Feature | Everyday French | Literary French |
|---|---|---|
| Main past tense | passé composé | **passé simple** (for events), **imparfait** (for background) |
| Word order | S-V-O, predictable | inversion, fronted adverbials, delayed subjects |
| Sentence length | short to medium | long periodic sentences with subordinate clauses |
| Vocabulary | high-frequency | precise, sensory, sometimes archaic |
| Pronoun *ne* | often dropped | kept, plus *ne* alone (*Il ne cessait de...*, *Je n'osais*) |
| Moods | indicative, present subjunctive | also imparfait du subjonctif, conditional of probability |
| Figures of speech | few | metaphor, personification, litotes, hyperbole |
| Punctuation | functional | rhythmic: semicolons, dashes, ellipses |

**The core pair:** **passé simple = the plot line** (events that move the story: *il ouvrit la porte*); **imparfait = the scenery and mood** (what was going on: *il pleuvait, la maison dormait*).

---

## 3. The Passé Simple: Recognition Cheat Sheet

You only need to recognize it. The forms are regular in pattern:

| Verb group | Endings (third person) | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| -er verbs | **-a / -èrent** | il parl**a**, ils parl**èrent**, elle march**a** |
| -ir and many -re verbs | **-it / -irent** | il fin**it**, ils fin**irent**, il part**it**, il pr**it** |
| irregular -u verbs | **-ut / -urent** | il v**int**/**vit**, il **eut**, il **fut**, il **voulut**, il **put**, il **dut** |
| *venir, tenir* | **-int / -inrent** | il v**int**, ils v**inrent**, il t**int** |

**High-frequency irregulars (memorize):**

| Infinitive | il / elle | ils / elles |
|---|---|---|
| être | **fut** | **furent** |
| avoir | **eut** | **eurent** |
| faire | **fit** | **firent** |
| dire | **dit** | **dirent** |
| voir | **vit** | **virent** |
| prendre | **prit** | **prirent** |
| venir | **vint** | **vinrent** |
| pouvoir | **put** | **purent** |
| savoir | **sut** | **surent** |
| mettre | **mit** | **mirent** |

**Warning:** *il parla* (passé simple) looks like *il parla* in no other tense, but *il finit* is **also** the present tense of *finir*. Context (past narration, other passé simple verbs) tells you which.

## 4. A Short Text to Read Closely

*(An original text written for this lesson in a classic narrative register.)*

> **La Maison du Verger**
>
> Le soir tombait lentement sur le village, et les dernières hirondelles tournoyaient autour du clocher. Marthe, qui n'avait pas quitté la maison depuis le matin, s'approcha de la fenêtre. Dans le verger, les pommiers, noircis par l'humidité, semblaient attendre quelque chose.
>
> Elle hésita un instant, puis ouvrit la porte. Un souffle froid l'enveloppa ; elle frissonna, serra son châle contre elle et descendit les marches. L'herbe, mouillée de rosée, étouffait le bruit de ses pas. Elle avait traversé la moitié du jardin lorsqu'elle aperçut, au pied du plus vieux des arbres, une silhouette immobile.
>
> — Qui est là ? demanda-t-elle d'une voix qu'elle ne reconnut pas.
>
> La silhouette ne bougea point. Marthe fit encore trois pas, le cœur battant ; et ce fut alors qu'elle comprit : ce n'était qu'un vieux manteau, jeté là par on ne savait qui, que le vent gonflait comme un corps vivant. Elle rit, d'un rire bref et nerveux, mais ne rentra pas tout de suite. Quelque chose, dans ce soir si calme, la retenait encore.

## 5. Close Reading: A Four-Layer Method

### Layer 1: Tenses (who is doing what)

Highlight every verb and sort them:

| Plot (passé simple) | Background (imparfait) | Earlier (plus-que-parfait) |
|---|---|---|
| s'approcha, hésita, ouvrit, enveloppa, frissonna, serra, descendit, aperçut, demanda, reconnut, bougea, fit, fut, comprit, rit, rentra | tombait, tournoyaient, semblaient, étouffait, battant (participle), retenait, gonflait | avait quitté, avait traversé |

**Observation:** the **actions** are in the passé simple (a chain of quick events), the **atmosphere** in the imparfait (the village at dusk, the orchard "waiting"), and the **prior situation** in the plus-que-parfait (*qui n'avait pas quitté la maison depuis le matin*). This is the same logic as your *photograph vs. video* model (Lesson 13), only with a different tense for the "video."

**The subtle one:** *Elle avait traversé la moitié du jardin lorsqu'elle aperçut...* The plus-que-parfait sets the stage ("she had already crossed half the garden") and the passé simple delivers the event. This `plus-que-parfait + lorsque + passé simple` pattern is a classic narrative structure.

### Layer 2: Sentence structure and rhythm

- **Long, flowing opening sentence:** *Le soir tombait lentement sur le village, et les dernières hirondelles tournoyaient autour du clocher.* Two imparfaits joined by *et*: a slow, visual scene-setting.
- **Short, abrupt sentences for action:** *Elle hésita un instant, puis ouvrit la porte.* The pace quickens with the passé simple.
- **Inserted relative clause:** *Marthe, qui n'avait pas quitté la maison depuis le matin, s'approcha...* The subject is delayed by the clause, a typical literary move.
- **Participial phrases:** *noircis par l'humidité* (past participle used adjectivally), *mouillée de rosée*, *le cœur battant* (absolute construction: "with her heart pounding"). Learn to spot these, because they replace entire clauses.
- **The semicolon:** *Un souffle froid l'enveloppa ; elle frissonna, serra son châle...* A semicolon links closely related actions, with more rhythm than a period.
- **Inversion/fronted structure:** *au pied du plus vieux des arbres, une silhouette immobile* (place first, subject last) delays the discovery for suspense.

### Layer 3: Vocabulary and register

| Literary / precise | Everyday equivalent | Note |
|---|---|---|
| tournoyer | tourner | circling flight, visual |
| un verger | un jardin avec des arbres | orchard |
| frissonner | trembler (de froid) | shiver |
| étouffer (le bruit) | atténuer | muffle |
| apercevoir | voir | catch sight of (sudden) |
| une silhouette | une forme | a figure, outline |
| ne ... point | ne ... pas | literary negation |
| on ne savait par qui | je ne sais pas qui | impersonal narration |

**Observation:** literary French chooses **specific verbs** (*frissonner, tournoyer, étouffer, apercevoir*) where everyday French uses neutral ones (*trembler, tourner, couvrir, voir*). That specificity creates image and mood.

### Layer 4: Figurative language and effect

- **Personification:** *les pommiers... semblaient attendre quelque chose.* Trees given a mind: creates unease.
- **Simile:** *que le vent gonflait comme un corps vivant.* The coat looks alive: the fear is made physical.
- **Sound:** *Un souffle froid l'enveloppa* uses soft consonants; *étouffait le bruit de ses pas* has a muffled, dull sound that matches the meaning.
- **Ending without closure:** *Quelque chose, dans ce soir si calme, la retenait encore.* The last sentence returns to the *imparfait* (the mood persists) and leaves a mystery. Many literary texts end on an imparfait for a "lingering" feeling.

## 6. Reading Routine for Any Literary Passage (6 Steps)

1. **First read, no stopping:** get the situation (who, where, what happens).
2. **Highlight verbs and sort by tense:** plot vs. background vs. earlier.
3. **Spot the long sentences:** break them into clauses (main verb first, then each subordinate).
4. **Pick 5 words/expressions to keep** (precise verbs, fixed collocations). Put them in your mining log (Lesson 53).
5. **Note one stylistic device** (metaphor, inversion, rhythm) and ask: "what effect does it create?"
6. **Rewrite two sentences in modern spoken French**, then compare. This reveals exactly what literary French changes.

**Example of step 6:**
- Literary: *Elle hésita un instant, puis ouvrit la porte.*
- Spoken: *Elle a hésité une seconde, puis elle a ouvert la porte.*

## 7. Mental Model: A Film Camera

Imagine a movie. The **imparfait** is the *camera panning* across the scene: dusk, trees, silence. The **passé simple** is the *cuts and actions*: she turns, opens, steps, sees. The **plus-que-parfait** is a *flashback*. The writer controls your attention by shifting among them. Once you see the tenses as camera moves, literary French stops feeling like a puzzle and starts feeling like a director's choices.

## 8. Common Mistakes

**Spanish speakers:** confusing the passé simple with the Spanish *pretérito indefinido*: it is similar in function (*habló, abrió*), so you have an intuition, but the French passé simple is **only written and literary**. Also confusing *il finit* (past) with *il finit* (present) and missing the nuance of the imparfait as scene-setter (Spanish *imperfecto* works the same way, which helps).

**English speakers:** looking for "was + -ing" patterns and misreading the imparfait; ignoring the passé simple because English has no equivalent; getting lost in long sentences instead of locating the main verb.

**Both:** translating word for word; stopping at every unknown adjective; not rereading. Literary prose rewards **a second and third read**, each revealing more.

## 9. Progressively Harder Practice

1. **Short:** re-read the passage and list the five most vivid verbs. Use each in a new sentence.
2. **Medium:** rewrite the first paragraph in the passé composé and imparfait (spoken style). What changes in tone?
3. **Harder:** continue the story in 5–6 sentences using at least two passé simple verbs, one plus-que-parfait, one absolute participial phrase (*le cœur battant*), and one simile.
4. **Real texts:** apply the six-step routine to a **one-page passage** from a short story by an author you pick (Maupassant, Colette, Ernaux, or a contemporary author). Choose a page you can understand 90%.
5. **Aloud:** read the passage twice, observing the rhythm of commas and semicolons (Lesson 59).

## 10. Mini Quiz (active recall)

1. Which tense carries the plot and which carries the background in literary narration?
2. Give the passé simple of *être, avoir, faire, voir, venir* (il/elle form).
3. What effect does ending on an imparfait often create?
4. What does *le cœur battant* represent grammatically, and what does it replace?
5. What is the function of the plus-que-parfait in the pattern *elle avait traversé... lorsqu'elle aperçut...*?
6. Why is « ne ... point » rare in speech?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Passé simple = plot; imparfait = background. 2. *fut, eut, fit, vit, vint*. 3. A lingering, open, atmospheric feeling. 4. An absolute participial construction; it replaces a clause like *alors que son cœur battait*. 5. It sets what was already done before the sudden event. 6. It is a literary or archaic negation; spoken French uses *pas* (and often drops *ne*).
