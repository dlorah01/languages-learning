# Lesson 12 — The Passé Composé: Talking About the Past (Part 1: Formation)
### Phase 1: Core System (A1 → A2)

**Competency axes trained:** 🧩 Morphosyntax (primary) · 🗣️ Productive fluency
**Prerequisite:** Lesson 7 (être/avoir) is essential — this whole tense is built on those two verbs
**Time investment:** One thorough read + daily conjugation drills — this is one of the most important lessons so far

---

## 1. Why This Tense Is a Landmark Lesson (Intuitive Explanation)

The passé composé is French's most common way to talk about completed past actions — and it works completely differently from Spanish's simple past (comí, hablé) or English's simple past (I ate, I spoke). Instead of a single conjugated verb form, French builds this tense **compositionally**: an auxiliary verb (avoir or être, conjugated in the present — Lesson 7!) plus a **past participle**. This is why Lesson 7 called être/avoir "foundation columns" — this is the tense standing on them.

---

## 2. The Formula

> **Subject + avoir/être (present tense) + past participle**

> **J'ai mangé.** — "I ate / I have eaten." (avoir + past participle of manger)
> **Je suis allé(e).** — "I went / I have gone." (être + past participle of aller)

**Critical mindset shift:** unlike English, which distinguishes "I ate" (simple past) from "I have eaten" (present perfect), **French's passé composé covers both meanings** — context, not verb form, tells you which nuance is meant. This is actually a simplification compared to English, and closer to how Spanish's "he comido" can also cover both, depending on region.

---

## 3. Forming the Past Participle (Regular Verbs)

| Infinitive ending | Past participle ending | Example |
|---|---|---|
| -ER | -é | manger → mangé |
| -IR (Pattern A) | -i | finir → fini |
| -RE | -u | vendre → vendu |

This maps directly onto the three verb families from Lesson 6 — one predictable participle ending per family, no new categories to learn.

---

## 4. The Big Question: Avoir or Être?

This is the single most important thing to internalize in this lesson. **Most French verbs use avoir** as their auxiliary. But a specific, memorizable list of verbs — mostly verbs of **movement or state change** — use **être** instead:

**The "house of être" verbs (often taught via the mnemonic DR & MRS VANDERTRAMP or "house" diagram):**
> aller (to go), venir (to come), arriver (to arrive), partir (to leave), entrer (to enter), sortir (to exit), monter (to go up), descendre (to go down), naître (to be born), mourir (to die), rester (to stay), tomber (to fall), retourner (to return), passer (to pass by — sometimes), devenir (to become)

Plus **all reflexive verbs** (covered in a later lesson) always use être.

**Why this split exists (technical/historical explanation):** this isn't arbitrary — historically, these être-verbs describe a change of **state or location of the subject itself** (going from one place/state to another), while avoir-verbs describe an action **done to something external**. This is a genuine conceptual split, not just a memorization list, though in practice, memorizing the list is still the fastest path.

---

## 5. Agreement Rule — Only for Être Verbs (this is where it gets Spanish-relevant)

When using **être**, the past participle **agrees in gender and number with the subject** — exactly like an adjective would (recall Lesson 8's agreement logic):

> **Il est allé.** — "He went." (masculine, no change)
> **Elle est allée.** — "She went." (feminine, add -e)
> **Ils sont allés.** — "They (masc./mixed) went." (add -s)
> **Elles sont allées.** — "They (fem.) went." (add -es)

**With avoir, the past participle does NOT agree with the subject** (a major structural difference from être-verbs):
> **Il a mangé. / Elle a mangé.** — no change regardless of subject gender

This agreement pattern is conceptually similar to Spanish's "ser" + adjective agreement, but Spanish doesn't have this exact avoir/être split for its own past tense — so treat this as a genuinely new French-specific mechanism, not a direct transfer.

| voir | vu | avoir |

| prendre | pris | avoir |
| pouvoir | pu | avoir |
| vouloir | voulu | avoir |
| devoir | dû | avoir |
| savoir | su | avoir |
| venir | venu | être |

## 7. Mental Model: A Two-Story Building — Auxiliary Downstairs, Meaning Upstairs

Picture the passé composé as a two-story structure: the **ground floor** is the auxiliary (avoir/être), conjugated in the present tense — this floor tells you *who* did the action, using the Lesson 7 conjugations you already know. The **upper floor** is the past participle, which tells you *what* action happened, and (for être-verbs only) also gets a matching "coat" (gender/number agreement) to match the subject downstairs. Once you see it as two connected but distinct jobs, the whole system stops feeling arbitrary.

---

## 8. Common Mistakes

**Spanish speakers:** trying to use only one auxiliary (as if French worked like a single-verb Spanish preterite); forgetting être-verb agreement since nothing in Spanish's own past tense requires this; applying avoir-verb (no agreement) logic to être-verbs by over-generalizing.

**English speakers:** conflating passé composé too rigidly with only "have eaten" (present perfect) and missing that it also covers simple past "ate" — leading to overuse of a separate imperfect-only mindset (we'll clarify passé composé vs. imparfait in the next lesson).

---

## 9. Progressively Harder Examples

1. J'ai mangé une pomme. — "I ate an apple."
2. Elle est partie tôt. — "She left early." (être-verb, feminine agreement)
3. Nous avons vu ce film. — "We saw this film."
4. Ils sont allés au marché, et ils ont acheté du pain. — "They went to the market, and they bought bread." (both auxiliaries in one sentence!)
5. Ma sœur est née en France, mais elle a grandi en Colombie. — "My sister was born in France, but she grew up in Colombia."
