# Lesson 16 — Formal vs. Informal Register: Tu vs. Vous and the Social Logic Behind It
### Phase 1: Core System (A1 → A2)

**Competency axes trained:** 🎭 Pragmatics/Register (primary) · 🔗 Discourse competence
**Prerequisite:** Lesson 6 (verb conjugation — vous takes its own conjugated form)
**Time investment:** One read-through; this is a social/cultural lesson as much as a grammatical one, so let it sink in through observation over time

---

## 1. Why This Deserves Its Own Lesson (Intuitive Explanation)

You already have this exact system in Spanish — tú vs. usted. So the grammar itself isn't new. What's genuinely new is **where French draws the line**, because the social rules differ meaningfully from most Spanish-speaking cultures, and getting this wrong is one of the most noticeable "foreigner" mistakes you can make — more socially costly, in some contexts, than a grammar error.

---

## 2. The Basic System

| Form | Used for |
|---|---|
| tu | one person, informal/familiar relationship |
| vous | one person, formal relationship — OR any group of 2+ people (formal or informal!) |

**Key structural point:** unlike Spanish, which has a separate plural informal form in some dialects (vosotros) and a clean tú/usted/ustedes three-way split, French **"vous" does double duty** — it's both the formal "you" (singular) AND the neutral/default plural "you" for any group, even a group of close friends. This is a genuine structural simplification compared to most Spanish variants, but also a source of ambiguity you'll need context to resolve.

---

## 3. Where French Draws the Line Differently Than You Might Expect

This is the part that requires real cultural recalibration, not just grammar:

- **French professional culture defaults to "vous" much more broadly and for much longer** than most Latin American Spanish-speaking cultures. Colleagues who've worked together for years may still use "vous," especially in more traditional or larger companies — this would feel unusually distant in many Colombian workplaces.
- **The switch from vous to tu ("tutoiement")** is often an explicit, sometimes even verbally marked, social event: someone might literally say "On peut se tutoyer?" ("Can we use tu with each other?") — a level of explicitness that has no strong equivalent in most Spanish-speaking contexts, where the switch tends to happen more implicitly.
- **Younger/informal/tech/startup culture** in France has shifted heavily toward "tu" by default, so context (industry, age, region, even specific company culture) matters enormously — there's no single universal rule.
- **Strangers, service staff, authority figures (teachers, doctors, police)**: default to "vous" essentially always, unless explicitly invited otherwise.

---

## 4. The Grammar: Vous Takes Its Own Verb Form (Lesson 6 Callback)

Remember from Lesson 6, "vous" always uses the **-ez** ending (or its irregular equivalent): vous parlez, vous finissez, vous êtes, vous avez. This doesn't change whether "vous" refers to one formal person or a whole group — the verb form is identical either way. Context (and sometimes explicit plural markers like "vous tous" — "all of you") resolves the ambiguity.

---

## 5. Mental Model: A Social Dial, Not a Binary Switch

Rather than picturing tu/vous as an on/off switch, picture it as a **dial** that starts at "vous" by default with anyone new, and can be explicitly or implicitly turned toward "tu" as a relationship develops — professionally, socially, or generationally. As a learner, **the safe default is always to start with vous** and let a native speaker signal (explicitly or through their own usage) when it's appropriate to shift. This is lower-risk than defaulting to tu and risking coming across as presumptuous or overly familiar.

---

## 6. Common Mistakes

**Spanish speakers:** under-using "vous" because many Latin American Spanish-speaking cultures (especially in everyday Colombian contexts) use "tú" more broadly than French professional/social norms allow — this is a genuine cultural gap, not just a grammar one; assuming tu/usted's cultural boundaries map directly onto tu/vous, when French skews noticeably more formal by default in many contexts (workplace, strangers, service interactions).

**English speakers:** since English has no T-V distinction at all ("you" covers everything), English speakers often don't intuitively grasp that a wrong choice here carries real social weight — it's not just a grammatical error, it can read as rude or oddly familiar depending on direction.

---

## 7. Progressively Harder Examples

1. Bonjour, comment allez-vous? — "Hello, how are you?" (formal/default with a stranger)
2. Salut, ça va, toi? — "Hi, how's it going?" (informal, with a friend)
3. On peut se tutoyer, si tu veux. — "We can use 'tu,' if you want." (the explicit switch-offer)
4. Vous êtes tous les bienvenus. — "You are all welcome." (vous as plural, addressing a group)
5. Excusez-moi, madame, pourriez-vous m'aider? — "Excuse me, ma'am, could you help me?" (formal register, service/stranger context)

---

## 8. Collocations

- **s'il vous plaît** — "please" (formal — the vous-based form, extremely common as a fixed phrase even among tu-users in certain contexts)
- **s'il te plaît** — "please" (informal equivalent)
- **je vous en prie** — "you're welcome" (formal)
- **de rien** — "you're welcome / no problem" (neutral/informal)

---

## 9. Speaking/Thinking Practice

Think through 3 people in your life and decide, if you were speaking French with them, whether the relationship would default to tu or vous, and why — this is a genuinely useful habit to build now, before you're in a real situation needing to decide in real time.
