# Lesson 13 — Passé Composé vs. Imparfait: The Aspect Distinction
### Phase 1: Core System (A1 → A2)

**Competency axes trained:** 🧩 Morphosyntax (primary) · 🗣️ Productive fluency · 🔗 Discourse competence
**Prerequisite:** Lesson 12 (passé composé formation) — this lesson introduces the second past tense and contrasts the two
**Time investment:** One thorough read + this is a concept that clicks over weeks of exposure, not one sitting — revisit often

---

## 1. Why This Is Taught as a Concept, Not a Rule (Intuitive Explanation)

This is famously the hardest grammar point for English speakers learning French — but you have a massive advantage: **Spanish has almost the exact same distinction** (pretérito vs. imperfecto). So instead of teaching you "rules for when to use each tense" (the English-speaker approach, which usually fails), we're going to name the concept you already intuitively use in Spanish, and just re-map it onto French forms.

---

## 2. The Core Concept: Aspect, Not Just "Time"

Both passé composé and imparfait describe the **past** — the difference isn't *when*, it's **aspect**: how the speaker frames the internal structure of the action.

- **Passé composé** = a **completed, bounded** action — something that happened and finished, viewed as a single event or point. (Like Spanish pretérito: *comí, hablé, fui*.)
- **Imparfait** = an **ongoing, habitual, or background** action — something in progress, repeated, or describing a state, without focus on its completion. (Like Spanish imperfecto: *comía, hablaba, era*.)

**This is a direct, high-fidelity transfer from Spanish.** If you can correctly choose between "comí" and "comía" in Spanish, you already have the intuition — you just need the French forms attached to it.

---

## 3. Forming the Imparfait (much easier than passé composé — one pattern, almost no exceptions)

**Formula:** take the **nous** form of the present tense, drop **-ons**, add the imparfait endings:

| Person | Ending |
|---|---|
| je | -ais |
| tu | -ais |
| il/elle/on | -ait |
| nous | -ions |
| vous | -iez |
| ils/elles | -aient |

**Example — parler:** nous parlons → parl- + ais/ais/ait/ions/iez/aient → je parlais, tu parlais, il parlait, nous parlions, vous parliez, ils parlaient

**Only one true irregular stem: être** → **ét-** (j'étais, tu étais, il était, nous étions, vous étiez, ils étaient). Every other verb, even irregular ones like faire, follows the "nous-form minus -ons" rule (faisons → fais- → je faisais).

---

## 4. Side-by-Side: The Same Sentence, Two Aspects

> **Il pleuvait quand je suis sorti.** — "It was raining (ongoing background) when I went out (single completed event)."
> **J'ai mangé à midi.** — "I ate at noon." (single completed action, no ongoing framing)
> **Je mangeais tous les jours à midi.** — "I used to eat / I would eat every day at noon." (habitual, repeated — imparfait)
> **Quand j'étais petit, j'habitais à Bogotá.** — "When I was little, I lived in Bogotá." (description of an ongoing past state)

Notice the classic pattern in the first example: **imparfait sets the scene/background, passé composé interrupts it with a completed event.** This "background vs. foreground" pairing is one of the most common and useful patterns in real narration.

---

## 5. Where Spanish and French Line Up Almost Perfectly

| Concept | Spanish | French |
|---|---|---|
| Habitual past action | comía todos los días | je mangeais tous les jours |
| Ongoing background state | era joven | j'étais jeune |
| Single completed event | comí a las 12 | j'ai mangé à midi |
| Interrupted action | dormía cuando llamó | je dormais quand il a appelé |

This table alone should feel like the biggest "unlock" so far in the program — you're not learning a new grammatical concept, you're learning new vocabulary for a concept you already fully control.

---

## 6. Where It Gets Subtle: Verbs That "Change Meaning" Based on Aspect

A small set of verbs shift meaning noticeably depending on which past tense is used — this exists in Spanish too (conocí vs. conocía), so trust the parallel:

| Verb | Imparfait meaning | Passé composé meaning |
|---|---|---|
| savoir | knew (ongoing state) | found out (sudden realization) |
| connaître | knew/was acquainted with | met (for the first time) |
| vouloir | wanted (ongoing desire) | decided to / tried to |
| pouvoir | was able to (general capacity) | managed to (specific instance) |

> **Je savais qu'il viendrait.** — "I knew he would come." (ongoing knowledge)
> **J'ai su qu'il était parti.** — "I found out he had left." (sudden realization)

Compare Spanish: *sabía* (knew) vs. *supe* (found out) — the exact same shift.

---

## 7. Mental Model: A Photograph vs. A Video

**Passé composé** is a **photograph** — a single, bounded moment captured and closed. **Imparfait** is a **video playing in the background** — ongoing, without a visible start or end point within the sentence. When you narrate the past in French, you're constantly deciding: am I showing a photo (something that happened and finished) or playing background footage (a state, habit, or ongoing scene)? This is the exact same choice you already make instinctively in Spanish between pretérito and imperfecto.

---

## 8. Common Mistakes

**Spanish speakers:** genuinely, your biggest risk here is small — mostly just needing exposure to French forms. Watch for slight boundary differences: some contexts where Spanish leans one way and French leans the other due to idiomatic convention, which we'll refine with more listening practice later in the program.

**English speakers:** since English doesn't grammatically distinguish these aspects (both often translate as simple past "I ate/I was eating" without a hard rule), English speakers often default to passé composé for everything, or struggle to feel *why* imparfait exists at all — this is precisely where your Spanish gives you a real, measurable head start over an English-only learner.

---

## 9. Progressively Harder Examples

1. Il faisait beau. — "The weather was nice." (imparfait — ongoing state)
2. Il a fait beau hier. — "The weather was nice yesterday." (passé composé — bounded, single day)
3. Quand j'étais enfant, je jouais dehors tous les jours. — "When I was a child, I used to play outside every day."
4. Un jour, je suis tombé et je me suis cassé le bras. — "One day, I fell and broke my arm." (sequence of completed events)
5. Je ne savais pas que tu étais là, mais quand je t'ai vu, j'ai été surpris. — "I didn't know you were there, but when I saw you, I was surprised." — imparfait (ongoing lack of knowledge) + imparfait (ongoing state) + passé composé (completed seeing) + passé composé (completed reaction)
