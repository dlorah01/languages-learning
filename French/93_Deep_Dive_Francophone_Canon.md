# Lesson 93 — Deep Dive 13: A Guided Francophone Canon
### Phase 5: Mastery & Maintenance — Optional Deep Dive

**Competency axes trained:** 🌍 Cultural/literary competence (primary) · 📖 Reading · 📚 Lexicon · 🧱 Discourse
**Prerequisites:** Lesson 55 (reading ladder), Lesson 60 (close reading), Lesson 63 (Francophone variation), Lesson 69 (cultural literacy)
**Time investment:** One read, then an open-ended reading plan (one work per 3–6 weeks).

> **Note:** this lesson recommends authors and titles and describes them in my own words. It contains no excerpts from copyrighted works. Public-domain texts can be read in full from the sources in Section 8; recent works require purchase or a library.

---

## 1. Why a "Canon," and Why Francophone?

A canon is not a list of obligations; it is a **shared map**. Educated French speakers carry a common stock of references (authors, titles, characters, phrases). Reading some of these works gives you:

- **Cultural intuition:** you start recognizing allusions in conversation, press, and humor (Lessons 44, 69, 87).
- **Linguistic depth:** literary French shows how the language behaves at its most flexible: syntax, rhythm, vocabulary, register shifts.
- **Perspective:** *la Francophonie* is not only France. Over half of the world's French speakers live outside France, and some of the most innovative French is written in Africa, the Caribbean, Québec, the Maghreb, Belgium, Switzerland, and the Middle East.

**Mental model: a reading map, not a ladder to climb once.** You will move across it by interest and by difficulty, returning to authors as your French deepens.

## 2. How Difficulty Works in Literary French

Difficulty comes from different sources, so don't equate "classic" with "hard".

| Source of difficulty | Example features | Where it appears |
|---|---|---|
| **Syntax** | long periodic sentences, inversion, subordinate chains | nineteenth-century novels, Proust |
| **Tenses** | *passé simple*, *imparfait du subjonctif* | narration in most pre-1950 novels |
| **Lexicon** | rare, technical, or archaic words | Hugo, Zola, Flaubert |
| **Register** | slang, spoken French rendered in writing | Queneau, contemporary urban fiction |
| **Variation** | local vocabulary, language mixing, creole or African-language influence | Caribbean, African, Québécois writing |
| **Structure** | fragmented narration, shifting perspective | modern and postmodern novels |
| **Cultural knowledge** | history and allusions | most works |

**Pick by the kind of difficulty you want to work on**, not only by prestige.

French literary prizes are a good way to discover contemporary work.

| Prize | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Prix Goncourt** | most prestigious novel prize, awarded in November |
| **Prix Renaudot** | announced the same day as the Goncourt |

| **Grand Prix du roman de l'Académie française** | |

## 5. Strategies for Reading Long Works

### 5.1 Before you start
- **Choose a work you want to read**, not one you think you should.
- **Read the back cover and a short, spoiler-light introduction** to know the setting and historical context.
- **Sample the first 20 pages**: if more than about 1 unknown word in 20 blocks understanding, choose an easier book or use support (see 5.3).

### 5.2 While reading
| Technique | How |
|---|---|
| **Don't stop for every word** | underline or note, read on; look up only words that appear repeatedly or block the sentence |
| **Predict** | guess what happens next; check |
| **Track characters and timeline** | a one-page character map |
| **Read aloud occasionally** | a page a day trains rhythm and prosody |
| **Keep a reading journal** | date, pages, 5 new expressions, one sentence you liked, one question |
| **Notice register** | narration versus dialogue; formal versus slang |
| **Mark grammar of interest** | rare tenses, inversion, participle clauses (Lesson 85) |

### 5.3 Supports
- **Audiobook + text:** listen while following the text; it combines pronunciation and reading speed (Lesson 56).
- **Bilingual or annotated editions** (e.g., school editions with notes) for classics.
- **Spanish or English translation as a reference:** read a chapter in French first, then check the translation selectively (not before). It helps with cultural references and difficult passages.
- **Dictionary tools:** digital dictionaries with etymology (Lesson 84).

### 5.4 After reading
- Write a **200-word résumé** in French and a **200-word opinion**.
- Discuss with a partner or tutor; explain a passage you found beautiful.
- Pick one **expression bank** from the book (20 items) and add them to your spaced-repetition system.

## 6. A Vocabulary of Literary Discussion

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **un incipit** | the opening lines |
| **le narrateur / la narratrice** | the narrator |
| **la focalisation** (interne, externe, zéro) | the narrative perspective |
| **l'intrigue (f.), le dénouement** | plot, resolution |
| **un personnage principal / secondaire** | main / secondary character |
| **le registre** (comique, tragique, lyrique, satirique…) | tone/genre of effect |
| **le champ lexical** | group of related words in a text |
| **une métaphore, une comparaison, une antithèse** | figures of speech (Lesson 65) |
| **le style indirect libre** | free indirect speech |
| **l'ironie (f.)** | irony |
| **le thème, le motif** | theme, recurring motif |
| **un roman, une nouvelle, un récit, un essai, un recueil** | novel, short story, narrative, essay, collection |
| **un/une romancier(-ière), un/une écrivain(e)** | novelist, writer (feminine forms: *une écrivaine* is widely used; see Lesson 76 on feminization) |
| **la critique, la quatrième de couverture** | review, back cover |
| **Ce roman m'a marqué(e) / m'a plu / m'a ennuyé(e).** | personal reaction phrases |
| **Je n'ai pas réussi à entrer dans le livre.** | "I couldn't get into the book" |

**Useful frames for oral discussion:** *Ce qui m'a frappé(e), c'est…; Le narrateur adopte un ton…; L'auteur joue avec…; Ce passage montre que…; Ce que j'ai moins aimé, c'est…*

## 7. Making Choices: Three Suggested Reading Paths

### Path A — "Accessible and enjoyable" (build the habit)
1. *Le Petit Nicolas* or a Maigret novel
2. *L'Étranger* (Camus)
3. *Une si longue lettre* (Bâ)
4. *Chanson douce* (Slimani) or *Ru* (Thúy)
5. *Stupeur et tremblements* (Nothomb)

### Path B — "Cultural core" (shared references)
1. *Fables* (La Fontaine; selected)
2. *Le Tartuffe* (Molière)
3. *Candide* (Voltaire)
4. A Maupassant short-story collection
5. *Madame Bovary* (Flaubert) or *Germinal* (Zola)
6. *L'Étranger* (Camus), then *Meursault, contre-enquête* (Daoud) for a dialogue between works

### Path C — "Francophone horizons" (variation and voices)
1. *Bonheur d'occasion* (Roy) or *Les Belles-sœurs* (Tremblay)
2. *Une si longue lettre* (Bâ) and *Les Bouts de bois de Dieu* (Sembène)
3. *Mémoires de porc-épic* (Mabanckou)
4. *Cahier d'un retour au pays natal* (Césaire), with annotations
5. *Texaco* (Chamoiseau) as a long-term project
6. *Léon l'Africain* (Maalouf)

You can also combine paths by theme: *colonialism and memory*, *city life*, *migration*, *women's voices*, *humor*, *crime fiction*.

## 9. Common Mistakes

**Spanish speakers:**
- Relying on false friends in literary vocabulary (*librairie*, *actuellement*, *éventuellement*, *sensible*).
- Reading too slowly out of perfectionism; context-based inference is a skill (Lesson 55).
- Choosing a famous but very dense work too early (Proust, Hugo) and abandoning reading.
- Skipping the *passé simple*: it is the narrative tense of many novels. Learn to recognize it (Lesson 85).

**English speakers:**
- Over-using translation instead of letting meaning build in French.

**Both:**
- Reading only translations of Francophone works (you lose the language).
- Treating the canon as exclusively France-centered.
- Not keeping a record: without a journal, vocabulary and ideas fade.
- Quitting after 30 pages. Give a book 50 pages; then decide.

## 10. A Twelve-Week Reading Plan (Example)

| Weeks | Activity |
|---|---|
| **1–3** | Read one short accessible work (Path A, item 1–3). Journal 10 minutes per session. Finish with a 200-word summary. |
| **4–6** | Read a medium work (Path B or C). Add audiobook support. Discuss it with a partner. |
| **7–9** | Read a Francophone work from a region you know little about (Section 3). Compare dialects and vocabulary with what you have heard in Lesson 63 and Lesson 88. |
| **10–11** | Read a short text from a classic (Maupassant story, a fable set, or a scene of Molière). Perform a close reading (Lesson 60). |
| **12** | Reflection: write 300 words in French on how your reading has changed; choose your next book. |

## 11. Progressively Harder Practice

1. **Choose (15 min):** pick two books from Section 3 and justify your choice in French (3 sentences each).
2. **Back-cover test:** read three back covers aloud and summarize each in one sentence.
3. **First-page drill:** read the first page of a book; list 10 words you can guess from context.
4. **Journal:** after each session write the date, pages, five expressions, one question.
5. **Free indirect speech:** find an example in a short story (Maupassant, public domain) and explain how you recognize it.
6. **Review:** write a 250-word review of a short work.
7. **Dialogue between works:** read the opening of *L'Étranger* (Camus) and of *Meursault, contre-enquête* (Daoud) and write 150 words comparing the narrators' voices (when you have both).
