# Lesson 11 — Possessive and Demonstrative Determiners
### Phase 1: Core System (A1 → A2)

**Competency axes trained:** 🧩 Morphosyntax (primary) · 📚 Lexicon (secondary)
**Prerequisite:** Lesson 8 (articles and gender) — this lesson extends the same agreement logic to two new determiner types
**Time investment:** One thorough read + daily drills this week

---

## 1. Why These Two Determiner Types Together (Intuitive Explanation)

Possessives ("my, your, his...") and demonstratives ("this, that...") both follow the **exact same rule** you learned in Lesson 8 for articles: **they agree with the gender/number of the noun they accompany, not with the possessor or the "distance" implied.** This single insight prevents a very common learner error, so we're teaching both together to reinforce that one underlying pattern.

---

## 2. Possessive Determiners: Agreement With the OWNED Noun, Not the Owner

This is the single biggest trap for Spanish speakers, so read carefully:

| Owner | Masc. sg. | Fem. sg. | Plural (both genders) |
|---|---|---|---|
| my | mon | ma | mes |
| your (tu) | ton | ta | tes |
| his/her/its | son | sa | ses |
| our | notre | notre | nos |
| your (vous) | votre | votre | vos |
| their | leur | leur | leurs |

**The critical point:** *son/sa/ses* mean "his" AND "her" AND "its" — French does **not** distinguish the owner's gender the way English does (his vs. her). Instead, **son/sa** agree with the gender of the **thing owned**:

> **son livre** — "his book" OR "her book" (livre is masculine, so "son" regardless of who owns it)
> **sa maison** — "his house" OR "her house" (maison is feminine, so "sa" regardless of who owns it)

This is genuinely different from both Spanish (*su libro* is ambiguous too, but for a different reason — Spanish doesn't mark owner gender either, so this one's actually a good transfer!) and especially different from English, which marks the *owner's* gender (his/her) rather than the *object's*. If you're translating from English, this is where you'll make mistakes; if you're translating from Spanish, the ambiguity itself will feel familiar even if the specific mechanism differs.

**Special exception — the "vowel rule":** before a feminine noun starting with a vowel sound, French uses the masculine form (**mon, ton, son**) purely for pronunciation ease, echoing Lesson 5's liaison logic:
> **mon amie** (not "ma amie") — "my (female) friend" — avoids the awkward vowel clash

---

## 3. Demonstrative Determiners: "This/That"

| Gender/number | Form | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Masculine sg. | ce | ce livre |
| Masculine sg. before vowel | cet | cet ami |
| Feminine sg. | cette | cette maison |
| Plural (both genders) | ces | ces livres / ces maisons |

**Key simplification vs. Spanish:** French collapses "this" and "that" (Spanish este/ese/aquel — a three-way distance distinction!) into a **single form** — ce/cette/ces covers both "this" and "that" without distinguishing distance grammatically. If distance genuinely needs clarifying, French adds **-ci** (here/this) or **-là** (there/that) as a suffix:

> **ce livre-ci** — "this book (here)"
> **ce livre-là** — "that book (there)"

But in ordinary conversation, plain **ce livre** covers both meanings and context resolves any ambiguity — a genuine simplification compared to Spanish's three-tier system.

---

## 4. Mental Model: Determiners as a Family That All Follow the Same Agreement Law

Since Lesson 8, you've been building one underlying law: **whatever precedes a French noun (article, possessive, demonstrative) must agree with that noun's gender and number** — never with anything else (not the speaker, not the owner, not the "distance"). Every new determiner type you learn from here on is just a new vocabulary set obeying a law you already know. This is the systems-thinking payoff: you're not learning new grammar, you're applying grammar you already have to new words.

---

## 5. Common Mistakes

**Spanish speakers:** occasionally over-applying Spanish's distance-based this/that/that-over-there three-way system, trying to find a French equivalent for "aquel" when none exists at the basic level (use -là if truly needed); forgetting the "mon amie" vowel exception since Spanish doesn't have an equivalent rule.

**English speakers:** using son/sa/ses based on the *owner's* gender instead of the *object's* gender — by far the most common and persistent error English speakers make with this topic, since it directly contradicts English's own system.

---

## 6. Progressively Harder Examples

1. C'est mon livre. — "This/that is my book."
2. Voici sa maison. — "Here's his/her house." (ambiguous without context — normal in French)
3. Mon amie habite ici, et cette maison est à elle. — "My friend lives here, and this house is hers."
4. Ces livres-ci sont à moi, ces livres-là sont à toi. — "These books (here) are mine, those books (there) are yours."
5. Leur famille et notre famille se connaissent bien. — "Their family and our family know each other well."

---

## 7. Collocations

- **à mon avis** — "in my opinion" (extremely high-frequency discourse opener)
- **de son côté** — "for his/her part / on his/her side"
- **c'est ça** — "that's right / that's it"
