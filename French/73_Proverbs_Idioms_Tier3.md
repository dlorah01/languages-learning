# Lesson 73 — Proverbs, Sayings, and Idioms: Tier 3

## 1. Why Proverbs and Advanced Idioms (Intuitive Explanation)

Proverbs (*proverbes*) and sayings (*dictons*) are **compressed wisdom**: one line that sums up a situation and often ends the discussion. Advanced idioms (*expressions idiomatiques*) are **vivid shortcuts** that carry attitude and humor. Using them well signals cultural belonging; using them badly signals the opposite.

**Good news:** many French proverbs have close Spanish equivalents (both inherit Latin and medieval European tradition), so you can learn them by **bridge**.

**Key rules for natural use:**
1. **Use sparingly.** One per conversation is plenty; a string of them sounds like a quote book.
2. **Often use half a proverb.** Natives cut them: *Petit à petit...* (the listener completes it).
3. **Introduce lightly:** *Comme on dit, ... / Comme dit le proverbe, ... / Le vieil adage dit que...*
4. **Adapt tone to context:** proverbs can sound preachy. A smile or a light voice helps.
5. **Never explain a proverb you've just used**; it kills the effect.

## 3. Proverbs by Theme

### Perseverance and effort
- **C'est en forgeant qu'on devient forgeron.** — Practice makes perfect.
- **Qui ne tente rien n'a rien.** — Nothing ventured, nothing gained.
- **Les petits ruisseaux font les grandes rivières.** — Small things add up.
- **Il n'est pas de sot métier.** — Every honest job has dignity.

### Patience and timing
- **Tout vient à point à qui sait attendre.** — Everything comes to him who waits.
- **À chaque jour suffit sa peine.** — Don't borrow tomorrow's troubles.
- **Il ne faut pas vendre la peau de l'ours avant de l'avoir tué.** — Don't count your chickens before they hatch.
- **Pierre qui roule n'amasse pas mousse.** — Someone who keeps moving doesn't build up anything (can also mean: stay free of burdens).

### Caution and consequences
- **Chat échaudé craint l'eau froide.** — Once bitten, twice shy.
- **Il n'y a pas de fumée sans feu.** — No smoke without fire.
- **Un malheur n'arrive jamais seul.** — Troubles come in threes.
- **Qui va à la chasse perd sa place.** — If you leave, you lose your spot.
- **Quand le chat n'est pas là, les souris dansent.** — When the cat's away, the mice will play.

### People and character
- **Tel père, tel fils.** — Like father, like son.
- **Qui se ressemble s'assemble.** — Birds of a feather flock together.
- **Les chiens ne font pas des chats.** — The apple doesn't fall far from the tree.
- **Chacun voit midi à sa porte.** — Everyone sees things from their own perspective.
- **Les absents ont toujours tort.** — Those who aren't there get blamed.

### Money, work, and life
- **L'argent ne fait pas le bonheur.** — Money doesn't buy happiness.
- **Les bons comptes font les bons amis.** — Clear accounts make good friends.
- **Ventre affamé n'a point d'oreilles.** — A hungry person doesn't listen.
- **L'appétit vient en mangeant.** — The more you do, the more you want.
- **Autre temps, autres mœurs.** — Times change, so do customs.
- **À l'impossible nul n'est tenu.** — No one can be expected to do the impossible.
- **Pas de nouvelles, bonnes nouvelles.** — No news is good news.

### Warning formulas
- **À bon entendeur, salut !** — A word to the wise (a warning or veiled threat).
- **Qui sème le vent récolte la tempête.** (above)

## 5. How Proverbs Appear in Real Language

### Typical patterns
1. **Full quote with intro:** *Comme on dit, qui sème le vent récolte la tempête.*
2. **Half quote:** *Petit à petit...* / *Qui ne tente rien...*
3. **Modified (playful) quote:** *Chat échaudé... et toujours pas prudent.* Speakers love twisting proverbs for humor and headlines.
4. **Narrative comment:** *Il a tout perdu, mais bon, à quelque chose malheur est bon.*

### Mini-dialogue

> — *J'ai raté mon train, et en plus il pleut.*
> — *Un malheur n'arrive jamais seul...*
> — *Oui, mais bon, après la pluie, le beau temps.*
> — *Exactement. Et puis, qui ne tente rien n'a rien : on prendra le prochain.*

Notice: the proverbs *answer* each other; they are conversational moves, not decorations.

## 6. Mental Model: Wisdom Tokens

Each proverb is a **token** that transmits an entire stance (patience, caution, effort) in five words. You hand it over; the other person recognizes it instantly. Because tokens are shared cultural currency, you can only "pay" with a token the other person knows. Learn the common ones first, and watch how often natives use them before trying your own.

## 7. Spanish and English Bridges (and Traps)

- **Shared images:** *Rome wasn't built in a day / Roma no se hizo en un día / Rome ne s'est pas faite en un jour* are the same in three languages. Others differ in image but not in idea: *To kill two birds with one stone* = *faire d'une pierre deux coups* (one stone, two blows).
- **False friend:** *tomber à pic* does **not** mean "to fall off a cliff"; it means "to arrive at the perfect time."
- **Caution:** do not translate Spanish idioms literally. *Estar en las nubes* works (*être dans les nuages*), but *No tener pelos en la lengua* does not map to *ne pas avoir de poils sur la langue*: say *ne pas mâcher ses mots*.
- **English trap:** *It's raining cats and dogs* → *Il pleut des cordes*; never *des chats et des chiens*.

---

## 8. Common Mistakes

**Spanish speakers:** literal translation of Spanish proverbs; mixing parts of two proverbs; forgetting definite articles and prepositions in the fixed phrase (*à quelque chose malheur est bon* has no *le*).

**English speakers:** adding English articles or changing word order (*Better late than never* → *Mieux vaut tard que jamais*, not *Mieux est tard...*); over-using idioms to sound fluent.

**Both:** using a proverb in the wrong register; explaining it after saying it; using too many; choosing one with a heavy tone in a light conversation; forgetting the fixed form (idioms rarely allow synonym substitution: *jeter l'éponge*, not *lancer la serviette*).

## 9. Progressively Harder Practice

1. **Match and bridge (15 min):** write the Spanish equivalent next to ten French proverbs from Section 2, from memory.
2. **Personal sentences:** for each of 10 idioms from Section 4, write one sentence about your own life.
3. **Dialogue writing:** write a 10-line dialogue between two friends that naturally includes four proverbs and three idioms.
4. **Half-quote drill:** your partner says the first half of a proverb; you complete it (and vice versa).
5. **Spot the twist:** find a newspaper headline that twists a proverb (try searching *proverbe détourné*). Explain the joke.
6. **Spontaneous use:** over a week, use two proverbs and three idioms in real conversation. Note the reactions and how natural they felt.
