# Lesson 59 — Pronunciation Polish: Prosody, Rhythm, and Accent Reduction
### Phase 4: Native Naturalness (B2 → C1)

**Competency axes trained:** 🔊 Phonology (primary) · 🗣️ Productive fluency · 👂 Listening decoding
**Prerequisite:** Lessons 1, 4, 5 (sounds, nasals, liaison), Lesson 45 and 56 (listening)
**Time investment:** One read, then 10 minutes of recorded practice per day for two weeks.

---

## 1. Why Sounds Are Not Enough (Intuitive Explanation)

You can pronounce every French phoneme correctly and still sound "foreign." What listeners hear first is **music**: rhythm, stress, and intonation (prosody). A speaker with a few imperfect vowels but excellent rhythm is perceived as far more natural than the reverse.

French prosody works very differently from English and, more subtly, from Spanish. This lesson targets the habits of a **Spanish-speaking learner with strong English**: transfer from Spanish (vowels, consonants, syllable habits) and from English (stress, vowel reduction, intonation).

---

## 2. The Three Pillars of French Prosody

### 2.1 Rhythm: Syllables Are Even, Groups Carry the Beat
Spanish and French are both **syllable-timed** (each syllable gets roughly equal duration), unlike English (stress-timed). That helps you. The French difference:

- French has **no word stress**. Instead, the **last syllable of a rhythmic group** is lengthened and slightly raised.
- A **rhythmic group** (*groupe rythmique*) is a meaningful chunk of 3–7 syllables, typically one idea:

> *Je voudrais / un café / s'il vous plaît.* → 3 groups; the last syllable of each (*-drais, -fé, -plaît*) is longer.

Compare with Spanish, where stress falls on a **specific syllable inside each word** (*CAfé, voLUMen*). In French you must **stop stressing words** and start **lengthening the group's end**.

### 2.2 Intonation: Shapes That Carry Meaning

| Sentence type | Melody | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Statement | falls at the end | Il vient demain. ↘ |
| Yes/no question | rises at the end | Il vient demain ? ↗ |
| Wh-question | falls (or high start, then falls) | Quand est-ce qu'il vient ? ↘ |
| Non-final group (more to come) | **rises** (continuation) | Il vient demain, ↗ mais il repart lundi. ↘ |
| Enumeration | rise, rise, rise, fall | du pain, ↗ du fromage, ↗ et du vin. ↘ |
| Surprise/incredulity | high rise | Il est parti ?! ⤴ |
| Emphasis | extra stress on the *first* syllable of the word (*accent d'insistance*) | C'est **im**-possible ! |

**Key point:** the **continuation rise** is the signature of fluent French. If your non-final groups fall like English statements, you sound "choppy."

### 2.3 Emphatic Stress (*Accent d'Insistance*)
French can add emphasis on the **first syllable** (usually of a word starting with a consonant): *C'est **for**midable ! C'est **ab**solument faux !* This is the only place French looks like it has word stress, and it is expressive, not lexical.

## 3. Spanish-Speaker Pronunciation Targets

Order of priority (by impact on comprehension):

| Problem | Why it happens | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **French /y/** (*tu, rue*) pronounced as /u/ or /i/ | Spanish has no /y/ | Say [i], keep tongue forward, **round the lips** like [u]. Minimal pairs: *tu/tout, rue/roue, dessus/dessous* |
| **Front rounded vowels /ø/, /œ/** (*peu, peur*) | Absent in Spanish | Say [e], round lips → [ø]; say [ɛ], round → [œ]. Pairs: *peu/peur*, *deux/dur* |
| **Nasal vowels** (*bon, bain, blanc*) | Spanish nasalizes lightly or adds [n] | **No [n] or [m] after the nasal vowel.** Lower the soft palate, no consonant. (Lesson 4) |
| **French /ʁ/** | Spanish has a trill/tap | Uvular friction at the back of the throat; start from gargling a soft [k]/[ɡ] |
| **Mid vowel pairs /e/ vs /ɛ/ and /o/ vs /ɔ/** | Spanish has one /e/ and one /o/ | Learn open vs. closed: *mes/mais*, *côte/cotte*; open in closed syllables (*sel, port*) |
| **/b/ vs /v/** | Spanish merges them | Teeth on lower lip for /v/: *beau/veau*, *bien/vient* |
| **/z/ between vowels** | Spanish uses [s] | *maison* [mɛzɔ̃] vs *poisson* [pwasɔ̃] |
| **/ʒ/ and /ʃ/** (*je, chat*) | Spanish /x/ and /tʃ/ | *je* is a voiced [ʒ], softer than Spanish *j* |
| **Added *e-* before s + consonant** | Spanish *escuela, España* | French *école, Espagne* (the sound is genuinely [e], but check *spécial, station*: no extra vowel) |
| **Pronouncing final consonants or adding vowels** | Spanish vowel endings | Cut cleanly: *petit* [pəti], *grand* [ɡʁɑ̃], *plus* (depends on meaning) |
| **Silent *h*** | Spanish *h* is also silent, so low risk | But French has "aspirated h" blocking liaison: *les héros* vs *les hommes* |

### English-speaker traps you may share
- **Vowel reduction:** English turns unstressed vowels into schwa. French keeps vowels full and pure. Say [a] as [a], not [ə].
- **Diphthongs:** English *say* [seɪ], *go* [ɡoʊ]. French *sait* [sɛ], *go* [ɡo]. No glide.
- **Aspiration:** English /p t k/ puff air at the start of words; French and Spanish do not. Your Spanish already gets this right.
- **Stress-timed reflex:** keep every syllable even.

## 4. The Schwa (*e muet*): When to Drop, When to Keep

| Situation | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| End of word | drops | *table* [tabl] |
| After one consonant in mid-phrase | often drops | *samedi* [samdi], *je ne sais pas* [ʒnəsepa] / [ʃɛpa] |
| After two consonants | usually pronounced | *vendredi* [vɑ̃dʁədi], *gouvernement* [ɡuvɛʁnəmɑ̃] |
| First syllable, one consonant | optional | *le petit* [lə pəti] / [lpti] |
| Pausing and emphasis | pronounced | *Je ne veux pas !* [ʒə nə vø pa] |

**Rule of three consonants:** drop a schwa only if it does not create **three consecutive consonant sounds**. *Vendredi*: *d-r-d* would be impossible, so keep it.

---

## 5. Liaison and Enchaînement Refresher (Lesson 5)

- **Mandatory:** determiner + noun (*les amis* [lezami]), pronoun + verb (*ils ont* [ilzɔ̃]), after *très, plus* (in some uses).
- **Forbidden:** after *et*, before aspirated *h*, after singular nouns.
- **Optional / literary:** after plural nouns, after verbs. Over-liaising sounds stilted.
- **Enchaînement:** final consonant sounds attach to the next vowel, so *il est ici* sounds like [i-lɛ-ti-si].

**Listening effect:** because of enchaînement, word boundaries are inaudible. **Chunks, not words, are the unit of listening** (Lesson 45).

---

## 6. Mental Model: A Conveyor Belt of Even Beads

Picture French speech as a **string of even beads (syllables)**, divided into groups by small knots at the end of each rhythmic group. Within a group the beads flow smoothly with no accent; the last bead is a little longer and carries the pitch movement (rising if more is coming, falling if finished). English is a bumpy road with potholes (stressed syllables); Spanish is a road with a bump on a different syllable in each word. French is a **smooth belt with periodic bumps at group ends**.

## 7. A Daily Fourteen-Day Routine (10 Minutes)

| Step | Time | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2 min | **Warm up:** tongue twisters or minimal pairs (see below) |
| 2 | 4 min | **Shadow** 30–40 seconds of a native clip, matching rhythm and melody (not just sounds) |
| 3 | 2 min | **Record yourself** reading the same text, then listen: where does your melody differ? |
| 4 | 2 min | **Mark and repeat:** pick one issue (a vowel, a group end) and drill it 10 times |

**Rule:** work on **one** feature per day. Perfection of everything at once is impossible.

### Minimal pairs to drill
- /y/ vs /u/: *tu/tout, rue/roue, sur/sourd, lu/loup*
- /ø/ vs /o/: *peu/peau, deux/dos, veut/veau*
- /e/ vs /ɛ/: *thé/terre, (j')ai/(je) sais, mes/mais*
- /ɑ̃/ vs /ɔ̃/ vs /ɛ̃/: *blanc/blond/blême/brin, pend/pont/pain*
- /ʃ/ vs /ʒ/: *chat/jas, chou/joue*
- /b/ vs /v/: *bon/vont, beau/veau*

### Tongue twisters (*virelangues*)
- *Les chaussettes de l'archiduchesse sont-elles sèches, archi-sèches ?*
- *Un chasseur sachant chasser doit savoir chasser sans son chien.*
- *Trois gros rats gris dans trois gros trous ronds.*
- *Je veux et j'exige d'exquises excuses.*

## 8. Self-Diagnosis: Recording Checklist

Record 60 seconds of free speech and check:

- [ ] Do my non-final groups rise, with the final one falling?
- [ ] Do I stress words instead of lengthening group ends?
- [ ] Is my /y/ clearly different from /u/ and /i/?
- [ ] Do my nasal vowels contain no extra [n] or [m]?
- [ ] Do I drop schwa where natives do (*je ne sais pas*, *samedi*)?
- [ ] Are my liaisons correct, with no *et* + liaison?
- [ ] Is my /ʁ/ uvular, not trilled?

Share the recording with a tutor or native speaker occasionally: external feedback catches what you cannot hear.

---

## 9. Common Mistakes

**Spanish speakers:** stressing a syllable inside every word; /u/ for /y/; adding [n] after nasal vowels; trilled /r/; merging /b/ and /v/; saying [s] for [z] in *maison*; pronouncing final silent letters.

**English speakers:** reducing vowels to schwa; diphthongizing; strong stress contrasts; aspirating /p t k/; English retroflex /r/; falling intonation on every group.

**Both:** speaking word-by-word with pauses instead of in rhythmic groups; over-pronouncing every *e muet*; ignoring melody; practicing sounds in isolation without sentences.

## 10. Progressively Harder Practice

1. **Phrases (3–4 syllables):** *Ça va ? Pas mal. Et toi ?* Clear rise on questions, falls on statements.
2. **Sentences with continuation rises:** *Quand je suis arrivé, il y avait déjà du monde.* (rise on *arrivé*, fall on *monde*)
3. **Lists:** *Il faut du pain, du fromage, des fruits et du vin.* (three rises, then fall)
4. **Schwa and liaison combos:** *Je ne vous ai pas encore vus.* [ʒnəvuzɛpazɑ̃kɔʁvy]
5. **Paragraph shadowing:** one minute of a news broadcast (RFI *Journal en français facile*), then a minute of fast conversation (Lesson 56).
