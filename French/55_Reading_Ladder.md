# Lesson 55 — Reading: From Graded Readers to Your First Real French Texts
### Phase 4: Native Naturalness (B2 → C1)

**Competency axes trained:** 📖 Reading (primary) · 📚 Lexicon · 🌍 Cultural/literary competence
**Prerequisite:** Lesson 42 (complex sentence architecture), Lesson 49 (collocations), Lesson 53 (building your own lexicon)
**Time investment:** Ongoing. Aim for 20–30 minutes of reading per day, starting this week.

---

## 1. Why Reading Is Your Best Lever Right Now (Intuitive Explanation)

At this stage, **reading is the most efficient way to acquire vocabulary, collocations, and sentence patterns at scale**, for three reasons:

1. **You control the speed.** Unlike listening (Lesson 45), you can pause, reread, and notice.
2. **Written French carries spelling, gender, and collocations visibly**, which is exactly where Spanish and English speakers most need exposure (silent letters, agreement, prepositions).
3. **Reading builds the "internal French voice."** The more French you read, the more French sentence rhythms become your default inner narration, a direct step toward thinking in French.

Research on second-language acquisition consistently finds that learners who read **a lot of comprehensible material** gain vocabulary and grammar intuition faster than learners who study only rules and word lists. The key phrase is *comprehensible*: if you understand about **95–98% of the words**, you can infer the rest without stopping.

---

## 2. The Two Reading Modes You Need

| Mode | Goal | Text difficulty | Technique |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Intensive reading** | analyze and learn from a short text | slightly above your level (90–95% known) | read slowly, annotate, look up, reread, shadow aloud |
| **Extensive reading** | volume and fluency, enjoyment | at or slightly below your level (97–99% known) | read fast, **do not** look up every word, aim for the story |

**Balance:** 20–30% intensive, 70–80% extensive. Many learners over-do intensive reading and never build speed.

---

## 3. The Reading Ladder

### Level 1: Graded readers (B1)
- **Series:** *Lectures CLE en français facile* (CLE International) and similar FLE readers (Hachette FLE, Didier). They use controlled vocabulary and often include glossaries and audio.
- **Why:** you finish whole books with real plots, which builds confidence and the habit of continuous reading.
- **Expected difficulty:** comfortable. You should understand almost everything.

### Level 2: Simple authentic texts (B1+/B2)
- ***Le Petit Nicolas* (Goscinny and Sempé):** short, funny stories narrated by a child. Simple syntax, lots of spoken-style French, very good for idioms and humor (Lesson 44).
- ***Le Petit Prince* (Saint-Exupéry):** short sentences, slow pace, widely available, and many learners already know the story. Excellent for rhythm and imparfait/passé composé in narration (Lesson 13).
- **Graphic novels and comics:** *Astérix*, *Tintin*, *Persepolis* (Marjane Satrapi). The images carry context, and dialogue shows natural spoken French. (*Astérix* is full of wordplay, a good humor workout.)
- **Why:** real French written for French readers, but with strong support (images, short chapters).
- **Expected difficulty:** B1+ to B2, a few unknown words per page, easily guessable.

### Level 3: News and magazines (B2)
- **Learner-friendly news:** *1jour1actu* (explains current events for young readers, clear vocabulary), RFI *Savoirs* and *Journal en français facile* (text and audio together), TV5Monde *Apprendre le français* (articles with exercises).
- **Mainstream press:** *Le Monde*, *Le Figaro*, *Libération*, *Le Parisien*, *Ouest-France*. Start with **short news items** (*faits divers*, *brèves*) and sports/culture, then move to editorials (*tribunes*) and long reads.
- **Why:** exposes you to formal written register (Lesson 33), connectors, passive constructions (Lesson 31), and current vocabulary.
- **Expected difficulty:** B2. Expect many unknown words in political or economic articles. Use the **"read first, look up the 10 most repeated words"** approach.

### Level 4: Contemporary novels and non-fiction (B2/C1)
- **Accessible modern fiction:** short novels by authors such as Marc Levy or Guillaume Musso (popular, plot-driven), Anna Gavalda (short stories), Delphine de Vigan, and Amélie Nothomb (short, punchy novels, often witty).
- **Non-fiction and essays:** popular science and history in French, plus French Wikipedia on topics you know well.
- **Why:** sustained narrative, richer vocabulary, and the full range of tenses (including the *passé simple*, the literary past tense you will begin to meet in books).
- **Expected difficulty:** C1 with effort. Choose books you would enjoy in English or Spanish.

### Level 5: Classic and literary French (C1/C2)
- **Short classics:** Camus (*L'Étranger*), Maupassant (short stories), Saint-Exupéry, Marguerite Duras, Annie Ernaux (short, precise prose).
- **Later:** Modiano, Flaubert, Proust (long-term goal), and eventually poetry and philosophy.
- **Why:** style, register, and cultural depth. These authors shape how educated French speakers think about language.
- **Expected difficulty:** C1–C2. Expect the *passé simple* and *imparfait du subjonctif* (see below).

## 4. The Literary Past Tense: Recognition Only

Written narrative (novels, history) uses the **passé simple** where spoken French uses the *passé composé*:

| Spoken/neutral | Literary |
|---|---|
| il **a dit** | il **dit** |
| elle **est arrivée** | elle **arriva** |
| ils **ont fini** | ils **finirent** |
| il **a eu** | il **eut** |
| il **a été** | il **fut** |

**You do not need to produce the passé simple in speech or everyday writing.** You only need to **recognize** the third person forms (*-a, -it, -ut, -èrent, -irent, -urent, -ent*) when reading fiction. The imparfait is still used for background description, so the logic from Lesson 13 (photograph vs. video) remains.

## 5. How to Read Intensively: A Five-Step Method

For a 200–400 word passage (an article paragraph, a chapter page):

1. **First pass, no dictionary.** Read for gist. Summarize in one sentence **in French**.
2. **Second pass, mark.** Underline (a) unknown words, (b) collocations that surprised you, (c) grammar structures you would not produce yourself.
3. **Look up selectively.** Only words that recur or block meaning. Always capture the **word with its gender, its collocations, and the sentence** (Lesson 53 template).
4. **Third pass, read aloud.** Shadow the rhythm (Lesson 5: liaison, enchaînement). This links reading to pronunciation and listening.
5. **Retrieve tomorrow.** Cover the text and **retell it aloud** from memory in 5–6 sentences, reusing five new items. (Active recall, the most effective step.)

---

## 6. How to Read Extensively: Habits That Work

- **The 5-finger rule:** open a page. If you meet **more than five unknown words** on it, the book is too hard for extensive reading. Move it to your intensive pile or choose an easier one.
- **Do not stop for every word.** Guess from context, keep going, and let repeated words teach themselves.
- **Read what you love.** Genre and interest drive consistency, and consistency drives results.
- **Keep a short "mining" log**, not a long vocabulary list: 3–5 items per session (a collocation, an idiom, a connector), reviewed with spaced repetition (days 2, 7, 21).
- **Pair reading with audiobooks.** Following the printed text while listening to a French audiobook trains the reading-listening link (Lesson 45) and fixes pronunciation of unfamiliar words.

---

## 7. Mental Model: Reading as Training Your Internal Autocomplete

Every time you read a natural French sentence, your brain records **which words predict which** (*prendre* → *décision*, *il faut que* → subjunctive, *dans* + future time). After enough exposure, your internal "autocomplete" starts suggesting the right collocation or structure **before** you consciously decide. This is the mechanism behind "native-like intuition" and "knowing what sounds natural," your stated final objective. Volume of comprehensible input is what trains it.

---

## 8. Common Mistakes

**Spanish speakers:** trusting cognates too much when reading (*actual*, *éventuellement*, *librairie* ≠ library, *assister à* ≠ assist); skipping unknown small words (prepositions and pronouns) that carry grammar; reading only for plot and missing the register.

**English speakers:** guessing words from English cognates and being wrong (*sensible*, *actuellement*, *journée*); getting discouraged by long sentences with subordination (Lesson 42) and abandoning the text.

**Both:** the "dictionary trap": looking up every word, losing the thread, and burning out. Also **choosing texts that are too hard** (starting with Proust) instead of building volume at the right level.

---

## 9. Progressively Harder Practice

1. **This week:** read one short *1jour1actu* article daily (5 minutes), summarizing it in one French sentence.
2. **Next two weeks:** read 10–15 pages daily of a graded reader or *Le Petit Nicolas*. Mine 3 items per day.
3. **Month 2:** read a news article from *Le Monde* or *Le Figaro* three times a week (intensive method), and a comic or novel extensively on other days.
4. **Month 3+:** start a contemporary novel. Set a rule: **never abandon a book before page 30**, and then keep it only if you enjoy it.

---

## 10. Speaking and Writing Follow-Up

- **Summarize aloud:** after each reading session, summarize in 4–6 sentences without looking, using at least two collocations from the text.
- **Opinion exercise:** give your opinion about the text in 5 sentences using argumentative connectors (Lesson 33) and the subjunctive after opinion/emotion verbs (Lessons 26 and 54).
- **Shadowing:** read the best paragraph aloud twice, imitating French rhythm.
