# Lesson 27 — Relative Clauses: Qui, Que, Dont, Où
### Phase 2: Expansion (A2 → B1)

**Competency axes trained:** 🧩 Morphosyntax (primary) · 🔗 Discourse competence
**Prerequisite:** Lesson 24 (object pronoun system) — relative pronouns follow similar logic
**Time investment:** One thorough read + sentence-combining drills

---

## 1. Why Relative Clauses Are a Discourse Superpower (Intuitive Explanation)

Relative clauses let you combine two sentences into one, richer sentence — "the man who called" instead of "the man. He called." This is one of the biggest jumps from "beginner sentences" to "natural-sounding French," because native speakers constantly embed clauses rather than stacking short, choppy statements.

---

## 2. Qui — Subject of the Relative Clause

Use **qui** when the noun being described is the **subject** of the following verb:

> L'homme **qui** parle est mon professeur. — "The man who is speaking is my teacher." (homme is the subject of "parle")

Compare Spanish "que" (which covers both subject and object roles) — French **splits** this into qui (subject) and que (object), a genuine structural difference worth internalizing now.

---

## 3. Que — Object of the Relative Clause

Use **que** when the noun being described is the **direct object** of the following verb:

> Le livre **que** je lis est intéressant. — "The book that I'm reading is interesting." (livre is the object of "lis" — "je" is the subject)

**Diagnostic trick:** if a subject noun/pronoun immediately follows the relative pronoun, use **que**; if a verb immediately follows, use **qui**. (L'homme qui parle = pronoun+verb → qui. Le livre que je lis = pronoun+subject+verb → que.)

---

## 4. Dont — Replacing "de + noun" (Direct Payoff of Lesson 24's "En")

**Dont** replaces a noun introduced by **de** within the relative clause — structurally, this is the relative-clause version of the pronoun "en" from Lesson 24:

> Le livre **dont** je parle est excellent. — "The book I'm talking about is excellent." (je parle **de** ce livre → dont)
> La femme **dont** le fils est médecin... — "The woman whose son is a doctor..." (le fils **de** cette femme → dont — this is how French expresses "whose"!)

**Critical insight:** French has **no separate word for "whose"** — dont covers this function precisely because possession is expressed via "de" (Lesson 14), and dont is simply the relative pronoun standing in for that "de" relationship.

---

## 5. Où — Place and Time

**Où** replaces a location or time expression:

> La ville **où** j'habite est belle. — "The city where I live is beautiful."
> Le jour **où** je suis né... — "The day when I was born..." (où covers "when" for time expressions too — a genuine extension beyond its literal "where" meaning)

---

## 6. Mental Model: Relative Pronouns as the Same "Door" System From Lesson 24, Now for Clauses

Recall Lesson 24's model: pronouns replace nouns based on which grammatical "door" (no preposition, à, de) the noun entered through. Relative pronouns follow the **exact same logic**, just linking two clauses instead of replacing a noun in a simple sentence: qui/que = no preposition (subject/object roles), dont = the "de-door," où = location/time. You're not learning a new system — you're applying Lesson 24's system to a new sentence-combining context.

---

## 7. Common Mistakes

**Spanish speakers:** using "que" for both subject and object roles by direct transfer from Spanish's single "que," missing the qui/que split entirely; forgetting "dont" exists and trying to build "whose" or "about which" clauses with awkward workarounds instead.

**English speakers:** omitting the relative pronoun entirely, since English frequently drops it ("the book I'm reading" — no "that"), while French **never** allows this omission — que/qui/dont/où must always be present.

---

## 8. Progressively Harder Examples

1. J'ai un ami qui parle français. — "I have a friend who speaks French."
2. C'est le film que nous avons vu hier. — "That's the film we saw yesterday."
3. Voici la maison dont je t'ai parlé. — "Here's the house I told you about."
4. C'est la personne dont le frère travaille avec moi. — "That's the person whose brother works with me."
5. Je me souviens du jour où nous nous sommes rencontrés. — "I remember the day we met."
