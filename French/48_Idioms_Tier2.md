# Lesson 48 — Idioms Tier 2: Intermediate Frequency and Domain-Specific Expressions
### Phase 4: Native Naturalness (B2 → C1)

**Competency axes trained:** 📚 Lexicon (primary) · 🎭 Pragmatics/Register · 🌍 Cultural competence
**Prerequisite:** Lesson 34 (Tier 1 idioms), Lesson 47 (full register command)
**Time investment:** Reference + drill lesson. Learn 4–5 idioms per day, not all at once.

---

## 1. From Tier 1 to Tier 2 (Intuitive Explanation)

Tier 1 idioms (Lesson 34) are the ones you cannot avoid: *ça marche, tant pis, sans blague*. Tier 2 idioms are less frequent individually, but an educated native speaker uses dozens of them every week, and they carry **imagery** (animals, food, body parts, weather) that makes speech vivid. Recognizing them is what lets you follow a conversation, a podcast, or a newspaper column without hitting a wall of "I know every word but not the meaning."

**Selection principle for this lesson:** these are idioms that educated speakers actually use in ordinary speech and writing, not the "folkloric" ones that appear in tourist phrasebooks and that natives rarely say.

## 2. Everyday Life and States

| Idiom | Literal image | Meaning | Register |
|---|---|---|---|
| Il pleut des cordes. | "It's raining ropes." | It's pouring. | neutral |
| Avoir le cafard | "to have the cockroach" | to feel down, gloomy | neutral/casual |
| Être dans la lune | "to be in the moon" | to be daydreaming, spaced out | neutral |
| Tomber dans les pommes | "to fall in the apples" | to faint | casual |
| Être au bout du rouleau | "to be at the end of the roll" | to be exhausted, at the end of one's strength | casual |
| Avoir du pain sur la planche | "to have bread on the board" | to have plenty of work ahead | neutral |
| Faire la grasse matinée | "to do the fat morning" | to sleep in | neutral |
| Se mettre sur son trente et un | "to put oneself on one's thirty-one" | to dress up | neutral |

**Spanish bridge:** *estar en la luna* maps almost exactly onto *être dans la lune*. For rain, Spanish *llover a cántaros* has the same "extreme quantity" logic, but the image differs (pitchers vs. ropes), a good reminder that the idiom's image is arbitrary, and only the meaning transfers.

## 3. Social Situations and Relationships

| Idiom | Literal image | Meaning | Register |
|---|---|---|---|
| Avoir un coup de foudre | "to have a lightning strike" | love at first sight | neutral |
| Poser un lapin à quelqu'un | "to put a rabbit on someone" | to stand someone up | casual |
| Casser les pieds à quelqu'un | "to break someone's feet" | to annoy someone | casual |
| Mettre son grain de sel | "to put in one's grain of salt" | to butt in uninvited with an opinion | casual |
| Avoir le cœur sur la main | "to have the heart on the hand" | to be generous, open-hearted | neutral |
| Raconter des salades | "to tell salads" | to tell tall tales, spin lies | casual |
| Se moquer de quelqu'un | "to mock someone" | (not an idiom, but note: *faire marcher quelqu'un* = to pull someone's leg) | neutral |

**Spanish bridge:** *poser un lapin* corresponds to *dejar plantado* or *dar plantón*. The French image (rabbit) has no Spanish equivalent, so this is a pure "learn as a chunk" idiom.

## 4. Money, Effort, and Difficulty

| Idiom | Literal image | Meaning | Register |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coûter les yeux de la tête | "to cost the eyes of the head" | to cost a fortune | neutral |
| Ce n'est pas la mer à boire. | "It's not the sea to drink." | It's not that hard / not such a big deal. | neutral |
| Faire d'une pierre deux coups | "to make two blows with one stone" | to kill two birds with one stone | neutral |
| Mettre la main à la pâte | "to put the hand in the dough" | to pitch in, get hands-on | neutral |
| C'est la fin des haricots. | "It's the end of the beans." | It's all over / we're done for. | casual |
| Revenons à nos moutons. | "Let's go back to our sheep." | Let's get back to the subject. | neutral/literary |

**Spanish bridge:** *costar un ojo de la cara* and *faire d'une pierre deux coups* (*matar dos pájaros de un tiro*) both have near-equivalents, so lean on your Spanish for the meaning, but remember the French wording is fixed (you cannot say "tuer deux oiseaux").

## 5. Speech and Communication

| Idiom | Literal image | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Appeler un chat un chat | "to call a cat a cat" | to call things by their name, speak frankly (Spanish: *llamar al pan pan y al vino vino*) |
| Avoir un chat dans la gorge | "to have a cat in the throat" | to have a frog in one's throat |
| Quand les poules auront des dents | "when hens have teeth" | when pigs fly / never |
| Ne pas avoir la langue dans sa poche | "not to have one's tongue in one's pocket" | to be outspoken, never at a loss for words |

## 6. Mental Model: Idioms as Compressed Cultural Images

Every idiom is a tiny story that a community agreed to compress into a fixed phrase. You do not decode it word by word; you store the **whole image together with its meaning**, exactly as you store a word. A useful memory technique (from the active-recall principles of this program): when you meet a new idiom, **visualize the literal image vividly for two seconds** (a cat in your throat, a rabbit placed on someone), then attach the meaning. The more absurd the image, the stronger the memory trace.

Two rules of thumb keep idiom use natural:
- **Fixed form:** you cannot swap words. *Avoir un chat dans la gorge* does not become "avoir un chien dans la gorge," and tampering with the form turns the idiom into nonsense.
- **Register fit:** most of the idioms above are neutral-to-casual. In a formal email (Lesson 32) or academic essay (Lesson 33), prefer plain wording, and save idioms for speech, narrative, and opinion writing.

---

## 7. Common Mistakes

**Spanish speakers:** assuming that because *estar en la luna* and *être dans la lune* match, other idioms will also match. Most do not, and false assumptions produce sentences that are grammatical but meaningless to a French listener. Also avoid "translating up" a Spanish idiom into French word for word.

**English speakers:** using English idioms with French words ("il pleut des chats et des chiens"), which is instantly recognizable as a calque and sounds comical. Learn the French image instead.

**Both groups:** overusing idioms. Native speakers use them sparingly; stacking three in one sentence sounds forced.

## 8. Progressively Harder Examples

1. Il pleut des cordes, donc on reste à la maison. — "It's pouring, so we're staying home."
2. Elle a posé un lapin à son collègue, et il a le cafard depuis. — "She stood her colleague up, and he has been feeling down ever since."
3. Ce n'est pas la mer à boire: en mettant tous la main à la pâte, on finit avant midi. — "It's not that hard: if we all pitch in, we'll finish before noon." (two idioms plus a gerund from Lesson 42)
4. Il a toujours son grain de sel à donner, ce qui casse les pieds à tout le monde. — "He always has an opinion to chip in, which annoys everybody."
5. J'ai du pain sur la planche cette semaine, alors revenons à nos moutons: où en est le budget? — "I have a lot on my plate this week, so let's get back to the point: where do we stand on the budget?"

## 9. Speaking Practice

1. **Image drill:** pick 5 idioms, say each aloud, describe the literal image in French in one short sentence ("Un chat dans la gorge: il y a un chat qui ne veut pas sortir!"), then say the real meaning.
2. **Situation drill:** for each of these situations, choose one idiom that fits and say a full sentence: (a) a friend didn't show up to dinner, (b) it's raining hard, (c) you are very tired at the end of a project, (d) an item costs far too much.

## 10. Mini Quiz (active recall)

3. Why is it a mistake to say "il pleut des chats et des chiens"?

5. What register do most Tier 2 idioms belong to, and where should you avoid them?
