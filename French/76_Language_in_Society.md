# Lesson 76 — Language in Society: Anglicisms, Language Policy, and Inclusive Writing

## 1. Why Learn About Debates About the Language (Intuitive Explanation)

In France and across the Francophone world, **language is a public topic**. Newspaper columns, school curricula, ministers, academies, and dinner conversations debate which words are acceptable, whether English is "invading," how to write about women and men, and what counts as "good French." If you want native-like intuition, you need to understand not only the language but **how speakers feel about it**: what they consider elegant, vulgar, snobbish, outdated, or politically charged.

This lesson is **descriptive, not prescriptive**: it maps the positions so that you can read, listen, and take part in these conversations with accuracy and tact. On contested topics, it presents the strongest arguments on each side and leaves your opinion to you.

> **Note:** policies, laws, and recommendations on these topics change. Verify current details with up-to-date sources.

## 2. Who "Controls" French? (Institutions and Their Limits)

| Institution | Role | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| **L'Académie française** (founded 1635) | 40 members ("les Immortels"); maintains the dictionary and issues recommendations | advisory; does not legally control usage |
| **Dictionaries** (*Le Robert*, *Larousse*) | record usage; add new words yearly | descriptive, published by companies |
| **La DGLFLF** (ministry body) and **la Commission d'enrichissement de la langue française** | propose French terms for new concepts; terms published in the *Journal officiel* | mandatory only for public administration |
| **L'Office québécois de la langue française (OQLF)** | language policy and terminology in Québec | applies in Québec |
| **L'Organisation internationale de la Francophonie (OIF)** | promotes French and cooperation among French-speaking countries | cultural and political, not linguistic regulation |
| **Schools and examiners** | transmit norms through teaching | |

**Key insight:** unlike what many learners imagine, **no institution can force people to speak a certain way**. The Académie's role is moral and symbolic; usage is shaped by millions of speakers. Spanish speakers will recognize a parallel with the **RAE** (Real Academia Española), which also issues norms through a network of academies. English has no central academy.

### Legal framework in France
- **Constitution, article 2 (1992):** *« La langue de la République est le français. »*
- **Loi Toubon (1994):** requires French in certain contexts: consumer information, employment contracts and workplace documents, public advertising, some public services. It was designed to protect users and workers, not to police private conversation.
- **Regional languages** (Breton, Basque, Alsatian, Occitan, Corsican, Catalan and others): recognized as part of the national heritage (constitution, article 75-1, 2008); their teaching has been debated and legislated, with ongoing disagreements about the extent of support.

### Québec
The **Charte de la langue française** (*Loi 101*, 1977) makes French the official language of Québec in government, education, and business signage. It reflects a historical context in which French-speaking Québécois sought to protect their language in a majority-English North America.

## 3. Anglicisms (*Anglicismes*) and *Franglais*

### Why English enters French
Technology, business, entertainment, music, sport, and science. This is a long, two-way history: **English itself borrowed thousands of words from French** after 1066 (*government, justice, beef, mutton, restaurant*), and French borrowed from English since the 18th century (*sport, week-end, tunnel, budget*).

### Types of anglicisms
| Type | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct borrowing, widely accepted** | *un week-end, un weekend, un parking, un sandwich, un clown, le football, un budget, un tunnel* | in all dictionaries |
| **Technology and business English** | *le marketing, un manager, un meeting, un deadline, un feedback, un brainstorming, un workshop, le cloud, une start-up* | very common at work |
| **Calques (literal translations)** | *faire du sens* (from "make sense"; standard: *avoir du sens*), *adresser un problème* (standard: *aborder / traiter un problème*) | often criticized |
| **Pseudo-anglicisms (faux anglicismes)** | words that look English but are French inventions | see below |
| **Semantic borrowings** | *réaliser* (to realize) in the English sense; *supporter* (to support); *éventuellement* | often criticized |

### Pseudo-anglicisms: English-looking words French invented
| French word | Meaning | English (differs) |
|---|---|---|
| **un smoking** | dinner jacket / tuxedo | "smoking jacket" |
| **un pressing** | dry cleaner's | "pressing" (urgent) |
| **un footing** | a jog | "footing" (basis) |
| **un baby-foot** | table football | — |
| **un brushing** | blow-dry | "brushing" |
| **un relooking** | makeover | — |
| **un zapping** | channel-surfing | — |
| **un lifting** | facelift | — |
| **un slip** | underpants | "slip" (to slide) |
| **un tennis** | tennis shoe (in some uses) / tennis | — |
| **un flipper** | pinball machine | — |
| **un camping-car** | RV/motorhome | — |
| **un talkie-walkie** | walkie-talkie | "walkie-talkie" |
| **un parking** | car park (not "parking" as an action) | "parking lot" |

> These illustrate how borrowed material is reshaped. Watch out in conversation with English speakers: they may not recognize them.

### Official French alternatives (recommended terms)
| English | Official French recommendation | Common usage |
|---|---|---|
| e-mail | **courriel** (also *mél.*) | *mail* and *e-mail* are widespread in France; *courriel* is standard in Québec |
| hashtag | **mot-dièse** | *hashtag* is dominant |
| start-up | **jeune pousse** | *start-up* dominates |
| cloud | **informatique en nuage** | *le cloud* |
| streaming | **diffusion en continu** | *le streaming* |
| feedback | **retour** (*retour d'information*) | both |
| podcast | **balado / baladodiffusion** (Québec) | *podcast* in France |
| shopping | **magasinage** (Québec), *achats* | *faire du shopping* |
| week-end | **fin de semaine** (Québec) | *week-end* in France |
| software | **logiciel** | universal |
| computer science | **informatique** | universal |
| home office | **télétravail** | universal |

**Pattern:** several French terms won (*logiciel, ordinateur, informatique, télétravail, covoiturage*), several did not (*mot-dièse, jeune pousse* remain official but rare). Usage decides.

### Attitudes toward anglicisms
| Position | Typical argument |
|---|---|
| **Purist / protective** | "Excessive borrowing impoverishes French and creates social divides for those who don't speak English." |
| **Pragmatic / open** | "Borrowing is normal in every language; French has always absorbed foreign words." |
| **Professional reality** | "In many sectors (tech, finance, science) English terms are the working vocabulary." |
| **Critical of snobbery** | "Using English words can be a social marker or a way of sounding modern or exclusive." |

**Your practical approach:** in formal documents and public-facing writing, prefer the French term when a well-established one exists (*logiciel, courriel, ordinateur*); in daily speech and technical work, follow what your interlocutors use.

## 4. Terms Related to Language Judgment

| French | English / meaning |
|---|---|
| **le bon usage** | "correct" usage (also the title of Grevisse's grammar) |
| **un barbarisme** | an incorrect word form (e.g., *infractus* for *infarctus*) |
| **un solécisme** | a grammar error |
| **un pléonasme** | redundancy (*monter en haut*) |
| **un néologisme** | a new word |
| **un emprunt** | a borrowing |
| **le franglais** | French mixed with English (the word is often critical) |
| **la langue de bois** | "wooden language": rigid, evasive political or corporate jargon |
| **le jargon** | specialized language, often with negative tone |
| **le langage soutenu / courant / familier / populaire / argotique / vulgaire** | register levels (Lesson 47) |
| **prescriptif / descriptif** | prescriptive / descriptive approaches |
| **la norme** | the accepted standard |

**Familiar debates among natives:** *voire même*, *au jour d'aujourd'hui* (redundant but widespread), *malgré que* (criticized but widely used), *la grande majorité des gens sont / est*, *se rappeler de* vs. *se rappeler* (traditionally *se rappeler quelque chose*), *je suis de retour* vs. *je suis retourné*. Prescriptive grammars (and many teachers) mark these as errors; descriptive linguists observe they are widespread. As a learner, in formal writing choose the traditional form, and you will rarely be wrong.

## 5. Gender in French: Feminization of Titles and Inclusive Writing

French has two grammatical genders, and the traditional rule is that **the masculine form also serves as the general (generic) form**: *Les étudiants sont arrivés* can include women and men. This grammatical feature is at the center of a public debate.

### 5.1 Feminization of job titles (largely settled)
Official bodies, including the Académie française in a 2019 report, accepted the feminization of professional titles. Common forms:

| Title | Feminine |
|---|---|
| le ministre | **la ministre** |
| le directeur | **la directrice** |
| le professeur | **la professeure** (also *la professeur*) |
| le président | **la présidente** |
| l'auteur | **l'autrice** (also *l'auteure*) |
| le docteur | **la docteure / la doctoresse** |
| le maire | **la maire** (also *la mairesse*) |
| l'ingénieur | **l'ingénieure** (more common in Québec) |

**Regional note:** Québec and Switzerland adopted feminization earlier and more systematically than France. Some women prefer the masculine title (*Madame le Directeur*) for personal or traditional reasons, and many insist on the feminine. In practice: **use the form the person uses for themselves.**

### 5.2 "Inclusive writing" (*écriture inclusive*): a contested topic
The term covers several practices. Here is a neutral overview.

| Practice | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Double forms (*doublets*)** | *les étudiantes et les étudiants* | widely accepted in speeches and formal writing |
| **Neutral / epicene wording** | *le corps enseignant, les personnes concernées, l'équipe* | avoids marking gender; usually uncontroversial |
| **Midpoint (*point médian*)** | *les étudiant·e·s* | common in some universities, associations, and activist writing; controversial |
| **Other typographic forms** | *étudiant-e-s, étudiant(e)s, étudiant.e.s* | variations |
| **Neopronouns / neutral forms** | *iel* (il + elle), *toustes*, *celleux* | recent; some dictionaries have recorded *iel*; strongly debated |
| **Feminization of titles** | see above | broadly accepted |
| **Agreement by proximity** | *les hommes et les femmes sont belles* | historical practice, sometimes proposed again |

### 5.3 The arguments (strongest versions)

**Arguments often made in favor of inclusive writing:**
- Language shapes representation: generic masculine can make women less visible, and some studies suggest it affects mental images.
- The rule *« le masculin l'emporte sur le féminin »* was formalized by grammarians in the 17th century; earlier French used agreement by proximity and more feminine titles, so the current rule is a historical choice, not an eternal law.
- Languages evolve as societies change; new forms help those who feel excluded by traditional ones.

**Arguments often made against, or for caution:**
- Typographic forms (like the midpoint) make texts harder to read and to read aloud, which affects learners, people with dyslexia, and visually impaired readers using screen readers.
- The generic masculine is considered by many linguists to be a grammatical, not a social, category.
- Language changes through use, not by decree, and prescribed changes may produce backlash.
- Priority should be given to concrete equality measures over typography.

**Institutional positions:** the Académie française has expressed strong opposition to inclusive writing (notably in 2017 and later statements), the French government has issued guidance restricting its use in official texts and in education, and legislative proposals on this topic have been debated. Other institutions (universities, companies, local governments, Québec and Swiss administrations) use inclusive formulations to varying degrees. Check current regulations for the context you work in.

**Spanish bridge:** you know a parallel debate in Spanish (*todos / todas / todes / tod@s / todxs*, with the RAE's positions). The French discussion involves some different tools (the midpoint, *iel*), but the arguments will feel familiar.

### 5.4 A practical stance for a learner
1. **Recognize all forms** when you read; you will meet them in newspapers, academic work, and social media.
2. **Be accurate with feminization of titles** and use the form a person prefers.
3. **Use doublets and neutral wording** as safe, widely understood options when you want to include everyone (*Chers collègues, chères collègues, / Chères et chers collègues*; *le personnel*, *l'équipe*).
4. **Follow the norms of your context** (institution, publication, client). When unsure, ask or look at documents from the same organization.
5. **Avoid assuming a person's views** from the forms they use; the topic is sensitive, and listening before commenting is wise.

## 6. Language Attitudes: How French Speakers Judge Speech

| Feature | Social perception |
|---|---|
| **Standard Parisian / "neutral" accent** | often treated as unmarked, "correct" |
| **Regional accents** | warmth and identity; some historical stigma, now increasingly valued |
| **Québécois, Belgian, Swiss, African French** | often appreciated, sometimes subject to stereotyping (Lesson 63) |
| **Verlan and slang** | youth identity, urban culture, now partly mainstream |
| **Liaison errors (*cuir*, *velours*) and "pataquès"** | stigmatized by prescriptive speakers (*les petits-enfants [z]*) |
| **Subjunctive and literary tenses in speech** | educated or formal, can seem stiff |
| **"Tu" vs. "vous"** | social positioning (Lesson 16) |
| **Foreign accent** | usually accepted; being understood matters more |

**Perspective for you:** a Colombian Spanish speaker with a slight accent in French is common and fully acceptable. Native-like fluency does not require erasing your accent, but control of rhythm and clarity (Lesson 59) helps comprehension.

## 7. Mental Model: A River With Banks

Language is a river: **usage is the water** (always flowing), while **institutions, dictionaries, and grammars are the banks** (shaping and slowing the flow, never fully containing it). Debates about anglicisms or inclusive writing are people pushing the banks in different directions, while the water keeps finding its path. Knowing who stands where on the bank helps you read the landscape.

## 8. Common Mistakes

**Spanish speakers:** translating the RAE/Académie parallel too literally; using Spanish-influenced calques (*realizar* for *réaliser* in all senses, *aplicar* for *postuler*), and mixing up false friends (*une librairie*, *un exit*); assuming the Académie rules usage the way the RAE influences Spanish media.

**English speakers:** using English words in French as if they were universally understood (some are, some are pseudo-anglicisms); calquing English phrasal verbs or idioms (*faire du sens*, *prendre place* ≠ take place, *adresser un problème*); assuming French has the same openness to borrowings in every context.

**Both:** taking sides on contested topics too fast in a professional setting; using *iel* or the midpoint without knowing the audience; mocking others' choices; confusing normative statements ("it is wrong") with descriptive ones ("it is widespread").

## 9. Progressively Harder Practice

1. **Anglicism audit:** read a newspaper page and list all English words. Classify them as: accepted, business jargon, pseudo-anglicism, or calque.
2. **Alternatives table:** make a table for 10 anglicisms with the official French term and what people actually say.
3. **Titles drill:** write the feminine and masculine forms for 15 job titles and use five in sentences.
4. **Two-perspective summary (200 words):** read one article in favor and one critical of inclusive writing (from different outlets). Summarize each side's strongest argument in French, without taking a side, using reporting verbs (Lesson 74).
5. **Neutral wording exercise:** rewrite five sentences using epicene formulations (*les étudiants → la communauté étudiante*; *tous les employés → l'ensemble du personnel*).
6. **Conversation practice:** discuss with a tutor "Faut-il défendre la langue française ?" Prepare three arguments for and three against the proposition using concession (*certes... mais*).
7. **Listen:** one episode of a French radio program about language (e.g., a linguistics segment on *France Culture* or *franceinfo*) and summarize it.
