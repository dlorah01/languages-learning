# Lesson 63 — The Francophone World: Accents, Regional Vocabulary, and Variation
### Phase 5: Mastery & Maintenance (C1 → C2+)

**Competency axes trained:** 👂 Listening decoding · 🌍 Cultural/literary competence · 📚 Lexicon
**Prerequisite:** Lesson 2 (history of French), Lesson 47 (register), Lesson 56 (immersion ladder), Lesson 59 (prosody)
**Time investment:** One read, then regular exposure to at least two non-Parisian varieties.

---

## 1. Why This Matters (Intuitive Explanation)

French is spoken by roughly **300 million people** on five continents, and a majority of daily speakers now live **outside France**, mostly in Africa. If you only train your ear on Parisian-style standard French, you will be surprised in a Montréal café, a Brussels office, a Geneva train, or an Abidjan market.

**Good news:** the varieties share the same grammar core and most vocabulary. The differences are mostly in **accent, a few hundred vocabulary items, some idioms, and some pragmatic habits**. Your goal is not to speak every variety, but to **understand them all** and to **choose consciously** which one you produce.

## 2. The Map at a Glance

| Region | Approx. role | Distinctive features |
|---|---|---|
| **France (north, "standard")** | reference model in teaching | the "neutral" accent of media |
| **France (south)** | strong regional accent | pronounced final *e*, different vowels |
| **Québec (Canada)** | North American French | affrication, vowel laxing, English contact, sacres |
| **Belgium** | European variety | *septante/nonante*, unique vocabulary |
| **Switzerland (Romandie)** | European variety | *septante/huitante/nonante*, calm rhythm |
| **West and Central Africa** | the largest French-speaking population | local rhythm, local vocabulary, Nouchi (Côte d'Ivoire), contact with African languages |
| **Maghreb (Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia)** | French as second language and code-switching | Arabic/Berber loanwords, frequent mixing |
| **Caribbean and Indian Ocean** | French plus Creole | Creole influence, Haitian and Antillean French |

---

## 3. France: Regional Accents Inside One Country

- **Standard/neutral accent (television, radio):** the model for textbooks and most media.
- **Southern accent (Marseille, Toulouse, Provence):** the final *e* is often pronounced (*une fille* → [ynə fijə]), nasal vowels lengthen with a trace of [ŋ] (*pain* → [pɛ̃ŋ]), and the rhythm is more "musical." Marcel Pagnol's films showcase it.
- **Northern accents (Lille, Ch'ti):** vocabulary and rhythm differences, famously portrayed in *Bienvenue chez les Ch'tis*.
- **Paris/Île-de-France urban speech:** fast, reduced, and rich in slang and *verlan* (*meuf* = femme, *ouf* = fou, *relou* = lourd).
- **Alsace and Brittany:** substrate influence from German and Breton, subtle prosody.

**Training tip:** in fast speech, a southern speaker will sound like they are "adding" vowels, a Parisian like they are "swallowing" them. Both are valid.

## 4. Québec French

### Pronunciation
| Feature | Example | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Affrication of /t/, /d/ before /i, y/** | *tu* → [tsy], *dire* → [dziʁ] | most recognizable trait |
| **Vowel laxing in closed syllables** | *petite* → [pətɪt], *jupe* → [ʒʏp] | short vowels become "lax" |
| **Diphthongization of long vowels** | *père* → [pɛɑ̯ʁ], *pâte* → [pɑʊ̯t] | the long vowels glide |
| **Nasal vowel shift** | *vin* and *vent* vowel qualities differ from France | |
| **Different rhythm** | more stress-like, with less phrase-final lengthening | |

### Vocabulary (Québec ≠ France)
| Québec | France | English |
|---|---|---|
| **un char** / **une auto** | une voiture | car |
| **un dépanneur (dép)** | une supérette, une épicerie | convenience store |
| **magasiner** | faire les magasins | to shop |
| **la fin de semaine** | le week-end | weekend |
| **déjeuner / dîner / souper** | petit-déjeuner / déjeuner / dîner | breakfast / lunch / dinner |
| **mon chum / ma blonde** | mon copain / ma copine | boyfriend / girlfriend |
| **un courriel** | un e-mail / un mail | email (official in France too) |
| **bienvenue** | de rien | you're welcome |
| **tiguidou** | parfait, super | great, all good |
| **pantoute** | pas du tout | not at all |

### Grammar and pragmatics
- **Question particle -tu:** *Tu viens-tu ?* "Are you coming?"
- **Tu** is used far more readily, even with strangers, than in France (Lesson 16 note: Québec is more egalitarian in tone).
- **Sacres** (religious swear words: *tabarnak, câlisse, crisse*) are strong expressions. Recognize them; avoid producing them unless you are fully immersed.
- **Language policy:** a strong effort to use French terms instead of English (*courriel, magasiner, fin de semaine*), supported by the Office québécois de la langue française.

**Anglicisms to recognize:** some English-influenced expressions exist (*checker* = to check, *être game* = to be up for it), though the Québec tradition resists English borrowings compared to some other varieties.

## 5. Belgium and Switzerland

### Numbers (a clean win for learners)
| Standard France | Belgium / Switzerland |
|---|---|
| soixante-dix (70) | **septante** |
| quatre-vingts (80) | quatre-vingts (Belgium) / **huitante** or **octante** (some Swiss cantons) |
| quatre-vingt-dix (90) | **nonante** |

Your Spanish intuition (*setenta, noventa*) matches the logical system.

### Meals
Like Québec: **déjeuner** (breakfast), **dîner** (lunch), **souper** (dinner), a significant difference from France.

### Belgian vocabulary
| Belgium | France | English |
|---|---|---|
| **un GSM** | un portable | mobile phone |
| **une drache** | une averse | heavy shower |
| **septante / nonante** | soixante-dix / quatre-vingt-dix | 70 / 90 |
| **savoir** (+ infinitive) for ability | pouvoir | *Je ne sais pas venir* = I can't come |
| **s'il vous plaît / une fois** | | *Viens une fois !* adds friendliness |
| **blinquer** | briller | to shine |
| **une chique** | un bonbon | candy |

### Swiss vocabulary
| Switzerland | France | English |
|---|---|---|
| **un natel** | un portable | mobile phone |
| **un cornet** | un sac en papier/plastique | bag |
| **poutzer** | nettoyer | to clean |
| **une panosse** | une serpillière | floor cloth |
| **déjeuner / dîner / souper** | (as Québec) | meals |
| **huitante** | quatre-vingts | eighty (Vaud, Valais, Fribourg) |

**Accent:** Swiss French is often described as calm and slightly slow, with a characteristic lengthening of syllables. Belgian French is close to standard French in pronunciation, with some differences in vowel length and intonation.

## 6. Africa (The Fast-Growing Center of the Francophone World)

- **Sub-Saharan French** shows strong local variation: Senegal, Côte d'Ivoire, Cameroon, DR Congo, Mali, Gabon, Madagascar. Rhythm, intonation, and vocabulary are influenced by local languages (Wolof, Bambara, Lingala, Malagasy...).
- **Common features:** syllable-timed rhythm, clear consonants, sometimes different treatment of nasal vowels and /y/, local expressions that spread through music, football, and social media.
- **Nouchi** (Côte d'Ivoire): urban slang mixing French and local languages. Example: *enjailler* = to have fun; *gbê*; *go* (girlfriend).
- **Senegalese contributions:** *teranga* (Wolof: hospitality), *toubab* (foreigner/white person, Wolof origin).
- **Maghreb:** French coexists with Arabic and Berber, creating frequent **code-switching** within a sentence. Loanwords widely understood in France: *toubib* (doctor), *bled* (remote place), *kif-kif* (the same), *maboul* (crazy), *kiffer* (to love, from Arabic *kayf*).
- **Africa and the future:** projections suggest the majority of French speakers will be African by mid-century, so African varieties will increasingly shape global French.

**Learner advice:** for listening, use RFI Afrique, African radio and television, music (Congolese rumba, Ivorian *coupé-décalé*, Senegalese hip-hop, Malian music), and films by African directors. This is both cultural and linguistic enrichment.

---

## 7. Caribbean and Indian Ocean French

- **Haiti:** French and Haitian Creole coexist; Haitian authors and musicians are important voices.
- **Guadeloupe, Martinique (Antilles):** French is official; Creole influences intonation and vocabulary.
- **Réunion, Mauritius, Seychelles:** French and Creole in contact.

**Note:** Creoles are separate languages with their own grammar, not "broken French." Recognition and respect matter.

## 8. Recognize vs. Produce: A Strategy

| Category | Strategy |
|---|---|
| **Accents** | Aim to **understand all**. Choose **one** target to produce (the "neutral" French of France, or Québec/Belgian/Swiss if your life is there) |
| **Vocabulary** | **Recognize** regionalisms. Produce them only if you live in or interact mostly with that region |
| **Numbers (*septante, nonante*)** | Easy to recognize; you can use them in Belgium/Switzerland; use *soixante-dix/quatre-vingt-dix* in France |
| **Slang (verlan, Nouchi, sacres)** | Recognize. Use sparingly and only when you are confident of the social context |
| **Tu/vous** | Adjust to local norms: Québec is more *tu*-friendly than France |

**Rule of thumb:** mixing varieties in the same conversation can sound odd (*char* in Paris, *voiture* in Montréal), but nobody will misunderstand you. Local speakers generally appreciate that you make the effort.

---

## 9. Mental Model: One Language, Many Dialects of One Family

Think of French like the Spanish you already know: Colombian, Mexican, Argentine, and Spanish (Spain) Spanish share grammar and most vocabulary but differ in accent, pronouns (*vos/tú/vosotros*), and everyday words (*carro/coche/auto*, *celular/móvil*). You already navigate that variation effortlessly. French works the same way, with the extra twist that for centuries France's norm was promoted by schools and institutions (the Académie française), so the differences are often labelled as "regional" when they are simply different histories.

---

## 10. Common Mistakes

**Spanish speakers:** assuming African or Québec French is "wrong" or "less correct" (it is not); expecting every accent to sound like the one in the textbook; ignoring that Spanish and French speakers also share parallels (the *septante/nonante* logic echoes *setenta/noventa*).

**English speakers:** mistaking Québec French for "English-accented French" (it is an independent, centuries-old variety with its own internal logic).

**Both:** letting one bad experience with a fast accent discourage you; confusing a regionalism for an error in a text; using Québec swearing or Nouchi expressions without knowing their weight.

## 11. Progressively Harder Practice

1. **Listen and compare:** play 60 seconds each from a French (Paris), Québécois, Belgian, and Senegalese speaker on the same topic (a news story or a recipe). Note three differences.
2. **Vocabulary mapping:** make a three-column table (France / Québec / Belgium-Switzerland) of 10 everyday words.
3. **Transcribe** 30 seconds of Québécois speech and mark the affricated *t/d*.
4. **Watch** one film or series from a non-French francophone region with French subtitles.
5. **Interview** (in writing or aloud) a francophone from another country about their everyday vocabulary and idioms.
