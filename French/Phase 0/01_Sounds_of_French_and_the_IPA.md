# Lesson 1 — The Sound System of French & the IPA
### Phase 0: Foundations

**Competency axes trained:** 🔊 Phonology (primary) · 👂 Listening (secondary)
**Prerequisite:** None — this is lesson zero of the sound system
**Time investment:** Read once for the model, then return daily for 5–10 min of practice for the next week

---

## 1. Why We Start Here (Intuitive Explanation)

Most courses start with "Bonjour, je m'appelle..." and grammar. We're starting with **sound** instead, for a specific reason tied to your profile.

You already have excellent grammatical intuition available to transfer from Spanish (gender, mood, subjunctive — all familiar concepts). What you do **not** have is a nervous system trained to hear and produce French sounds. And here's the trap: **if you learn vocabulary with the wrong sounds attached from day one, you will spend years unlearning bad pronunciation habits.** It's much cheaper to build the sound system first and hang vocabulary on top of it, than to build vocabulary first and retrofit pronunciation later.

Think of it like this: Spanish and English gave you a *keyboard* with certain keys on it. French needs several keys that simply don't exist on your current keyboard — nasal vowels, the French "u," the uvular "r." You're not just learning new words; you're **installing new keys**.

---

## 2. Technical Explanation: What Makes French Phonology Different

French, Spanish, and English are all "Indo-European," but they sit in very different phonological families in practice:

| Feature | Spanish | English | French |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vowel count | 5 pure vowels | ~11–12 vowels | ~12–16 vowels (incl. 4 nasal) |
| Syllable timing | Syllable-timed (even rhythm) | Stress-timed (uneven rhythm) | Syllable-timed but with final-syllable stress grouping |
| Nasal vowels | None | None (nasal *consonants* only) | 4 phonemic nasal vowels |
| The "r" sound | Alveolar trill/tap [r]/[ɾ] | Postalveolar approximant [ɹ] | Uvular fricative [ʁ] |
| Word-final consonants | Usually pronounced | Usually pronounced | Usually **silent** (unless liaison) |
| Liaison/linking | Minimal | Minimal | Systematic and rule-governed |

The single biggest mental shift: **French spelling is not phonetic the way Spanish is.** In Spanish, what you see is almost always what you say. In French, spelling reflects a much older stage of the language (roughly 1000+ years of sound change without corresponding spelling reform) — so you need the IPA as your real map, not the letters.

---

## 3. The IPA: Your New Map

The International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) gives one symbol per sound, regardless of spelling. This matters enormously in French because the same sound can be spelled many ways, and the same letters can represent different sounds depending on context.

**Example — the sound [o] (like "o" in Spanish "sol," roughly):**
Can be spelled: `eau` (beau), `au` (chaud), `ô` (hôtel), `o` (mot), `os` (gros — silent s)

**Example — the letter combination "en":**
Can represent: nasal vowel [ɑ̃] (in "enfant") OR the sequence [ɛn] (in "chien" — no wait, that's different)... You see the problem already. Spelling lies. IPA doesn't.

From today, every new word you learn should come with its IPA transcription. I will always provide it.

## 4. The French Vowel System (the part that matters most for you)

### 4.1 Oral vowels (12)

| IPA | Example word | Closest Spanish sound | Closest English sound | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [i] | si | Spanish *i* (sí) | "ee" in *see* | Nearly identical to Spanish |
| [e] | été | Spanish *e* (but tenser) | no equivalent | Close-mid, tense — don't diphthongize like English "ay" |
| [ɛ] | même | between Spanish e/a | "e" in *bed* | Open-mid |
| [a] | patte | Spanish *a* | "a" in *cat* (fronted) | Front [a], not back |
| [ɑ] | pâte | — | "a" in *father* | Back [a] — many French speakers merge this with [a] now |
| [y] | tu | **NEW SOUND** | **NEW SOUND** | Round your lips as for [u], but say [i]. This is THE sound that outs a foreign accent instantly |
| [ø] | peu | **NEW SOUND** | — | Round lips as for [o], say [e] |
| [œ] | sœur | **NEW SOUND** | "ur" in *fur* (roughly) | Round lips as for [ɔ], say [ɛ] |
| [u] | tout | Spanish *u* (sopa) | "oo" in *food* | Close to Spanish, but more rounded |
| [o] | mot | Spanish *o* (but tenser) | no equivalent | Don't diphthongize like English "oh" |
| [ɔ] | homme | between Spanish o/a | "o" in *pot* (UK) | Open-mid |
| [ə] | le | — | "a" in *sofa* (schwa) | The famous "mute e" — often dropped in speech |

### 4.2 Nasal vowels (4) — the sounds Spanish has zero preparation for

This is the single biggest phonological gulf between Spanish and French. Spanish nasalizes vowels slightly before "n/m" but never as an independent, meaning-changing sound. In French, nasal vowels are **phonemic** — changing oral to nasal changes the word's meaning entirely.

| IPA | Example | Spelling patterns | Mental model |
|---|---|---|---|
| [ɑ̃] | dans, temps, enfant | an, am, en, em | Say Spanish "a," let air escape through your nose *at the same time* as your mouth, don't pronounce the n/m as a consonant |
| [ɛ̃] | vin, pain, fin | in, ain, ein, un (some accents) | Say Spanish "e," same nasal technique |
| [ɔ̃] | bon, nom, pont | on, om | Say Spanish "o," same nasal technique |
| [œ̃] | un, parfum | un, um | Increasingly merged into [ɛ̃] in Paris French, but still distinct in careful/southern speech |

**The core technique (do this now, out loud):** Say the Spanish word "**san**" but stop your tongue from touching the roof of your mouth for the "n." Let the vowel itself go nasal, and just... let the n trail off into nothing. That "ghost n" feeling is nasal vowel production. It will feel wrong for about two weeks. That's normal — you're building new neuromuscular pathways.

## 5. Consonants Worth Special Attention

| IPA | Example | Note for Spanish/English speakers |
|---|---|---|
| [ʁ] | rouge, Paris | The French "r" — a uvular sound made at the back of the throat, like gently gargling. NOT the Spanish tap/trill, NOT the English "r." This is the #2 accent-marker after [y]. |
| [ʒ] | je, rouge | Like English "s" in "measure," or the "y" in some Argentinian/Uruguayan Spanish ("yo" → "sho"). Spanish speakers from Río de la Plata have a huge advantage here! |
| [ʃ] | chat | Like English "sh," or Spanish "ch" but without the "t" onset (Spanish ch = [tʃ], French ch = [ʃ]) |
| [ɲ] | montagne | Identical to Spanish "ñ" — easy win for you |
| [w] | oui | Identical to Spanish "hue-" (bueno) — easy win |

## 6. Mental Model: The "French Mouth" vs. the "Spanish Mouth"

Picture two different default mouth postures:

- **Spanish mouth**: relaxed, vowels are short and crisp, lips move minimally, jaw does most of the work.
- **French mouth**: lips are *active* — rounding and spreading constantly, especially for [y], [ø], [œ]. Tension is held more forward and in the lips than in Spanish. Think of French as spoken slightly "at the front of the face," almost pouty, while Spanish is spoken more centrally.

A useful physical exercise: practice saying "tu" [ty] by starting your lips in the position for whistling (rounded, as for [u]) and then, **without moving your lips**, try to say [i]. That tension — lips say one thing, tongue says another — is the essence of French vowel production, and it's why French vowels feel more "effortful" at first than Spanish ones.

## 7. Common Mistakes

### Mistakes Spanish speakers typically make:
- Pronouncing final consonants that should be silent (saying "petiT" fully instead of [pəti]) — Spanish always pronounces what's written.
- Replacing [y] with [u] (saying "tu" like Spanish "tú") — the #1 giveaway of a Spanish accent in French.
- Trilling or tapping the French "r" like Spanish "r/rr" instead of using the uvular [ʁ].
- Not nasalizing vowels — pronouncing the "n" as a full consonant instead of nasalizing the vowel (saying "bon" as "bo-n" instead of [bɔ̃]).
- Diphthongizing pure vowels — turning [o] into [ou] or [e] into [ei], a habit from Spanish where this doesn't happen, but ironically English speakers do this *more*.

### Mistakes English speakers typically make (for contrast — useful since you also think in English):
- Diphthongizing [e] and [o] heavily (English "day," "go" have built-in glides French doesn't).
- Aspirating consonants like French speakers don't (English "p," "t," "k" have a puff of air; French doesn't).
- Using the English approximant "r" [ɹ] instead of uvular [ʁ].
- Word-level stress instead of French's more even, phrase-final stress pattern.

## 8. Progressively Harder Examples (practice reading these aloud, using the IPA as your guide)

1. **tu** [ty] — "you" (informal)
2. **rue** [ʁy] — "street"
3. **du** [dy] — "of the" — contrast with **doux** [du] "soft" — minimal pair!
4. **sur** [syʁ] — "on" — contrast with **sourd** [suʁ] "deaf"
5. **un bon vin blanc** [œ̃ bɔ̃ vɛ̃ blɑ̃] — "a good white wine" — all four nasal vowels in one phrase (a classic French pronunciation drill!)
6. **je voudrais un peu de sucre** [ʒə vudʁɛ œ̃ pø də sykʁ] — "I would like a little sugar" — combines [ʒ], [ʁ], nasal [œ̃], [ø], [y] in one natural sentence

## 9. Collocations & Fixed Phrases to Anchor These Sounds

- **tout à fait** [tut‿a fɛ] — "absolutely / exactly" (note the liaison between *tout* and *à* — more on liaison in Lesson 5)
- **bien sûr** [bjɛ̃ syʁ] — "of course"
- **peu à peu** [pø a pø] — "little by little"
- **un peu plus** [œ̃ pø plys] — "a little more"

## 10. Speaking Practice (do this today)

**Shadowing exercise:** Search "un bon vin blanc prononciation" or any French pronunciation drill video. Listen to a native speaker say the phrase 5 times. On the 6th listen, speak simultaneously with them (this is "shadowing" — don't wait for pauses, overlap your voice with theirs). Record yourself once and just *notice* the difference — no need to judge yet, just build awareness.

**Minimal pairs drill (do out loud, 10x each):**
- tu / tout [ty] / [tu]
- du / doux [dy] / [du]
- sur / sourd [syʁ] / [suʁ]
- bon / banc [bɔ̃] / [bɑ̃]
