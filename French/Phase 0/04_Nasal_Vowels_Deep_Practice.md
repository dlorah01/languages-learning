## 1. Why This Gets Its Own Dedicated Lesson (Intuitive Explanation)

In Lesson 1, we introduced nasal vowels conceptually. But nasal vowels deserve a lesson entirely to themselves for a very practical reason: **they are the single highest-frequency phonological feature in French that Spanish gives you zero preparation for**, and they show up in extremely common words — numbers, articles, everyday verbs, basic vocabulary. You will use nasal vowels in nearly every sentence you speak in French, starting from your very first conversation. There is no "save this for later" option.

Compare this to, say, the uvular [ʁ] — also new, but you can mumble through it for months and still be understood. Nasal vowels are different: **if you don't nasalize, you often produce a completely different, real word**, not just an accented version of the right one. This is what linguists call *phonemic* contrast — the nasalization itself carries meaning.

## 3. Technical Explanation: What Is Actually Happening Physically

A nasal vowel is produced when the **soft palate (velum) lowers**, allowing air to resonate through the nasal cavity *at the same time* as the mouth shapes the vowel — instead of the velum staying raised (blocking the nose) as it does for oral vowels, then dropping sharply for a separate nasal consonant like Spanish "n."

**The critical difference from Spanish:** in Spanish, when you say "san," you produce a full oral vowel [a], THEN a separate, fully-formed nasal consonant [n] where your tongue tip touches the ridge behind your teeth. In French "sans" [sɑ̃], there is **no tongue-tip contact at all** — the vowel itself becomes nasal, and the "n" is not pronounced as a consonant. It's not "vowel + n," it's "one single nasalized vowel sound."

This is the #1 correction you'll need to make repeatedly: **stop your tongue from touching anywhere** when producing these sounds. If your tongue touches the roof of your mouth, you've reverted to Spanish-style pronunciation.

## 4. The Four Nasal Vowels — Full Reference Table

| IPA | Mouth shape (start from this oral vowel) | Example words | Common spellings |
|---|---|---|---|
| [ɑ̃] | Start from [a] (Spanish "a") | dans, temps, enfant, grand, français | an, am, en, em |
| [ɛ̃] | Start from [ɛ] (Spanish "e," more open) | vin, pain, fin, important, bien | in, im, ain, aim, ein, (un in Paris speech) |
| [ɔ̃] | Start from [ɔ] (between Spanish o/a) | bon, nom, pont, maison, oncle | on, om |
| [œ̃] | Start from [œ] (rounded [ɛ]) | un, parfum, lundi | un, um |

**Important nuance:** in most of contemporary Paris French, [œ̃] has largely merged into [ɛ̃] — many French speakers today pronounce "un" and "brun" almost identically to "vin"/"pain." You will still hear [œ̃] preserved in the south of France, in Belgium, and in careful/older speech. For your production, don't stress over perfecting a fourth distinct sound — but you should be able to *recognize* both.

## 5. The Step-by-Step Production Method

Do this exercise out loud, right now, slowly:

**Step 1 — Isolate the oral vowel.** Say Spanish "a" clearly: **[a]**. Hold it for 2 full seconds.

**Step 2 — Add nasal resonance without moving your tongue.** While still holding the [a] sound, imagine you have a stuffy nose and you're trying to "push" some of the sound up into your nasal cavity, without your tongue moving at all. It should feel like the sound splits between your mouth and your nose simultaneously. This is [ɑ̃].

**Step 3 — Never finish with a consonant "n."** The sound should simply fade out — no click of the tongue against the roof of the mouth at the end. If you hear yourself finishing with a clear "nn" sound, you've reverted to the Spanish pattern. Go back to Step 2.

**Step 4 — Repeat for each of the four nasal vowels**, starting from their oral base ([a] → [ɑ̃], [ɛ] → [ɛ̃], [ɔ] → [ɔ̃], [œ] → [œ̃]).

**A useful trick some learners find helpful:** pinch your nose gently while saying an oral vowel like [a] — nothing changes, because oral vowels don't use the nose. Now pinch your nose while saying the nasal vowel [ɑ̃] — you should feel the sound get physically blocked/muffled, because it *was* resonating through the nose. If pinching your nose doesn't change the sound at all, you're not actually nasalizing yet.

---

## 6. Mental Model: The Nasal Vowel as a "Vowel With the Door Left Open"

Picture your mouth and nose as two rooms connected by a door (the soft palate/velum). For oral vowels, that door is **firmly shut** — all the sound stays in the "mouth room." For nasal vowels, the door is **left slightly ajar** — sound leaks into the "nose room" too, and you hear both rooms' resonance blended together. It's not a separate sound tacked onto the vowel; it's the *same* vowel, produced with the door open instead of closed.

---

## 7. Common Mistakes

### Mistakes Spanish speakers typically make:
- Producing a full oral vowel followed by a full consonant "n" (the exact Spanish pattern), instead of a single nasalized vowel — by far the most common error.
- Under-nasalizing [ɔ̃] and [ɑ̃] specifically, because Spanish's own mild pre-nasal vowel coloring (a subtle, non-phonemic effect before n/m in Spanish) is *just* enough to make learners think they're "already doing it," when French requires a much stronger, sustained nasal resonance.
- Confusing [ɑ̃] and [ɔ̃] because both are "back" nasal vowels — practice the minimal pair **dans/dont** [dɑ̃]/[dɔ̃] specifically to separate these.

### Mistakes English speakers typically make (relevant since you also think in English):
- Nasalizing vowels *too weakly*, because English has zero phonemic nasal vowels — English speakers often produce a vague, unclear nasal quality rather than the strong, deliberate French nasalization.
- Confusing [ɛ̃] with English "ang" sounds (as in "bang"), producing something closer to a nasalized [æ] rather than the correct [ɛ̃].

## 8. Progressively Harder Examples

**Level 1 — single nasal vowel, isolated:**
1. **bon** [bɔ̃] — "good"
2. **vin** [vɛ̃] — "wine"
3. **dans** [dɑ̃] — "in"
4. **un** [œ̃] — "a/one"

**Level 2 — nasal vowel in a short phrase:**
5. **un bon vin** [œ̃ bɔ̃ vɛ̃] — "a good wine"
6. **cinq enfants** [sɛ̃k ɑ̃fɑ̃] — "five children" (note: two different nasal vowels in one short phrase!)

**Level 3 — all four nasal vowels in one sentence (the classic drill from Lesson 1, now with full understanding):**
7. **Un bon vin blanc.** [œ̃ bɔ̃ vɛ̃ blɑ̃] — "A good white wine." — contains [œ̃], [ɔ̃], [ɛ̃], [ɑ̃] in sequence

**Level 4 — natural sentence density:**
8. **Mon oncle habite en France depuis longtemps.** [mɔ̃n‿ɔ̃kl abit ɑ̃ fʁɑ̃s dəpɥi lɔ̃tɑ̃] — "My uncle has lived in France for a long time." — five nasal vowels in one natural sentence

## 9. Collocations Anchored to Nasal Vowels

- **bien sûr** [bjɛ̃ syʁ] — "of course"
- **en fait** [ɑ̃ fɛt] — "in fact / actually" (extremely high-frequency discourse marker — you'll use this constantly once we reach natural spoken French)
- **de temps en temps** [də tɑ̃z‿ɑ̃ tɑ̃] — "from time to time"
- **combien** [kɔ̃bjɛ̃] — "how much/many" — note it contains two different nasal vowels

## 10. Speaking Practice

**The nose-pinch test (do this daily this week):** Say each of the Level 1 words above while gently pinching your nose. You should feel/hear a clear difference (muffling) compared to saying an oral-vowel word like "beau" with your nose pinched (no difference). This gives you immediate physical feedback on whether you're truly nasalizing.

**Shadowing target:** search for "les voyelles nasales" pronunciation videos, or any French phonetics teaching channel. Shadow a native speaker doing the classic **"un bon vin blanc"** drill 10 times, focusing purely on not letting your tongue touch the roof of your mouth.

**Recording exercise:** record yourself saying the Level 4 sentence about your uncle. Listen back specifically for any place where you hear a clear "n" consonant click — that's your signal for where you're still defaulting to Spanish pronunciation.
