# Lesson 53 — Collocations II: Adverb + Adjective and Intensifiers
### Phase 5: Vocabulary as a System | Module 3 of 8

---

## 0. Where this fits
Lesson 52 covered the two biggest collocation families (verb + noun, adjective + noun). This lesson covers the family that gives your English **tone, precision, and strength control**: the adverbs that modify adjectives (and other adverbs and verbs) to say *how much*.

- *very* tired → **utterly** exhausted
- *very* worried → **deeply** concerned
- *very* unlikely → **highly** unlikely
- *very* cold → **bitterly** cold

Spanish has a small, flexible set (*muy, bastante, demasiado, totalmente, realmente, sumamente*) that works with almost any adjective. English has dozens, and each one is **picky about its partners**. Learners who stay with *very* and *really* sound correct but flat. Learners who use a random strong adverb sound odd (*"highly cold," "deeply tall"*).

This lesson gives you three things:
1. The **map** (types and strength levels of intensifiers).
2. The **grammar** (gradable vs. ungradable adjectives, which decides which intensifier you can use).
3. The **collocations** (which adverb goes with which adjective), organized so that you can retrieve them.

Lesson 54 (Synonym Precision) builds on this: choosing between *angry, furious, irate* depends partly on which intensifier each takes.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
Think of an **audio mixer** with a volume knob for every adjective. English speakers do not just turn the knob up (*very*), they **choose a different knob** depending on what they are describing.

- Emotions have a **heart knob**: *deeply, bitterly, genuinely, painfully, profoundly.*
- Probability has a **likelihood knob**: *highly, extremely, very, fairly, remotely.*
- Completeness has a **fullness knob**: *fully, completely, totally, entirely, thoroughly, utterly.*
- Temperature and weather have a **sensory knob**: *bitterly cold, scorching hot, pouring wet.*
- Judgments have an **opinion knob**: *perfectly reasonable, wildly inaccurate, grossly unfair.*

And not every adjective has a volume knob at all. You can turn *tired* up (*very tired*) but you cannot turn *exhausted* up with *very*. *Exhausted* is already at maximum; you can only emphasize it: *absolutely exhausted, completely exhausted.* This is the single most useful grammar idea in the lesson (Section 2.2).

So the learning goals are:
> **(1) Know whether the adjective is gradable. (2) Pick the intensifier that belongs to that adjective's "family." (3) Control the strength from weak to strong.**

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 Types of degree adverbs
Linguists divide degree adverbs into **amplifiers** (turn it up) and **downtoners** (turn it down).

| Group | Subtype | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Amplifiers** | **Maximizers** | the highest point | *absolutely, completely, entirely, totally, utterly, perfectly, fully, thoroughly, wholly* |
| | **Boosters** | very high, but not absolute | *very, really, extremely, highly, deeply, awfully, terribly, incredibly, remarkably, particularly, exceptionally* |
| **Downtoners** | **Approximators** | almost | *almost, nearly, virtually, practically, all but* |
| | **Compromisers** | moderate | *fairly, quite (UK), pretty, rather, somewhat, reasonably* |
| | **Diminishers** | below average | *slightly, a bit, a little, somewhat, mildly, partly, kind of, sort of* |
| | **Minimizers** | almost none | *hardly, barely, scarcely, not at all, in the least, remotely* |

### 2.2 The key grammar: gradable vs. ungradable adjectives
This is the rule that causes the most errors.

| Type | Meaning | Examples | Which intensifiers? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gradable** | exist on a scale (more or less) | *good, cold, tired, big, happy, difficult, expensive, interesting* | boosters (*very, really, extremely, highly, deeply*), compromisers, diminishers. **Not** maximizers like *absolutely, utterly*. |
| **Extreme (strong) adjectives** | already at the top of a scale | *exhausted, terrible, fantastic, enormous, freezing, delighted, furious, awful, brilliant, hilarious, gorgeous, starving, huge, tiny* | maximizers: *absolutely, utterly, completely, totally, simply, really*. **Not** *very* (✗ *very exhausted*). |
| **Classifying (absolute) adjectives** | yes/no categories | *dead, unique, impossible, perfect, correct, married, pregnant, true, false, essential, final, daily, wooden* | *completely, absolutely, totally, entirely, almost, nearly, virtually.* **Not** *very* or *more* (with rare exceptions). |

**The basic pattern**

| ✅ Natural | ❌ Unnatural |
|---|---|
| very tired | absolutely tired |
| absolutely exhausted | very exhausted |
| very good | absolutely good |
| absolutely fantastic | very fantastic |
| very cold | utterly cold |
| absolutely freezing | very freezing |
| very difficult | totally difficult |
| completely impossible | very impossible |
| very surprised | very astonished |
| utterly astonished | |
| extremely angry | absolutely angry |
| absolutely furious | very furious |
| really good / really exhausted | (**really** works with both) |

> **Why this rule matters:** *really* is the safe bridge. *Really* works with gradable *and* extreme adjectives (*really tired, really exhausted, really good, really fantastic*). *Absolutely* and *completely* work only with extreme and classifying adjectives. *Very* works only with gradable ones.

**Quick test:** Can you say "a little [adjective]"? If yes, it is gradable (*a little tired* ✅, *a little exhausted* ✗).

**Exceptions and flexibility**
- *Quite* in British English can act as a compromiser (*quite good* = fairly good) with gradable adjectives, and as a maximizer with extreme or classifying ones (*quite amazing*, *quite impossible* = completely). See Section 6.
- *Pretty* (US) works as a compromiser with gradable adjectives (*pretty good*).
- *Fairly* is mild; it does not combine with extreme adjectives.
- In very informal speech, people say *very exhausted, very impossible* with a humorous effect. As a learner, prefer the standard rule.
- *Absolutely* alone can also be a full answer: "Do you agree?" "Absolutely."

### 2.4 Downtoners and softeners (precision at the lower end)
These matter as much as boosters. Natives soften constantly (politeness, hedging).

| Word | Strength | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **a little / a bit** | slight | Informal-neutral: *a bit tired*. Can be negative: *a bit rude*. |
| **slightly / mildly / marginally** | slight | Formal-neutral: *slightly higher*, *mildly surprised* |
| **somewhat** | moderate-low | Formal: *somewhat unexpected* |
| **fairly** | moderate | Mild, usually positive-neutral: *fairly good* |
| **reasonably** | moderate+ | Positive-neutral: *reasonably priced* |
| **pretty (US)** | moderate+ | Informal: *pretty good* = better than "fairly" |
| **rather** | moderate+ | Often unexpected/negative-ish: *rather surprising*, *rather expensive*, UK-leaning |
| **quite (UK)** | moderate | *quite good*; with extreme/absolute = maximizer |
| **kind of / sort of** | hedge | Informal, vague: *kind of tired* |
| **relatively / comparatively** | compared to others | *relatively cheap* |
| **hardly / barely / scarcely** | almost none | Negative meaning: *hardly noticeable*; **never use with "not"** |
| **not very / not particularly / not at all** | negative | *not very good*, *not particularly useful* |
| **nowhere near** | far from | *nowhere near finished* |
| **less than** | below | *less than ideal* |

**Strength ladder for a gradable adjective (hot/cold/good/difficult)**
`not at all < hardly < slightly/a bit < somewhat < fairly < reasonably < quite (UK) < pretty (US) < rather < very < really < extremely < incredibly`
Then for extreme adjectives: `absolutely / utterly / completely / totally / simply`

### 2.5 Intensifiers with verbs, nouns, comparatives, and other adverbs
Intensifiers are not only for adjectives.

| Modifies | Pattern | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Verbs** | *deeply regret, strongly recommend, completely forget, firmly believe, greatly appreciate, seriously consider, totally agree, fully understand, strongly suggest, wholeheartedly support, bitterly resent, utterly despise* | Fixed verb–adverb collocations. Position: before the verb, or after the object (*I appreciate it greatly*). |
| **Other adverbs** | *extremely well, incredibly quickly, painfully slowly, remarkably easily, perfectly clearly, fairly often, quite often* | |
| **Comparatives** | *much better, far worse, a lot easier, slightly higher, considerably smaller, a great deal more, no better, even faster, still bigger* | **Never *very* + comparative** (✗ *very better*); use *much, far, a lot, even, still, slightly, a bit.* |
| **Superlatives** | *by far the best, easily the cheapest, much the largest, quite the best (UK), the very best* | |
| **Too / enough** | *far too expensive, much too late, a bit too loud, not nearly enough, way too long (informal)* | *Too* = more than acceptable (negative). |
| **Nouns** | *a complete disaster, a total waste, an absolute nightmare, sheer luck, pure fantasy, utter nonsense, a real shame, a perfect stranger, a great deal, a serious problem* | Adjective-type intensifiers before nouns. |
| **Past participles** | *deeply moved, highly respected, widely used, fully booked, heavily criticized, badly damaged, well known* | Often the strongest collocations. |
| **Prepositional phrases** | *completely out of control, entirely up to you, absolutely in favor of, perfectly at home, utterly at a loss, deeply in debt, fast asleep* | |

### 2.6 "So / such / too / enough / very much / a lot"
These small words cause large errors.

| Word | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **very** + adj/adv | normal degree | *very good, very quickly* |
| **so** + adj/adv (no noun) | emphasis, result clause, exclamation | *It's so cold! It was so cold that we left.* |
| **such (a/an)** + (adj) + noun | emphasis with noun | *It's such a cold day! Such nice people!* |
| **too** + adj/adv | more than acceptable | *too expensive, too fast* (negative result) |
| **adj + enough** | sufficient | *tall enough, good enough* (post-position!) |
| **enough + noun** | sufficient quantity | *enough time, enough money* |
| **very much / a lot** | with verbs | *Thank you very much. I like it a lot.* |
| **much** | with comparatives/negatives/questions | *much better; I don't like it much; Is it much different?* |
| **really** | informal general | *really good, I really like it* |
| **quite a / quite an** | surprising degree (UK/US) | *quite a surprise, quite a few (= many)* |
| **rather a** | UK | *rather a shame* |
| **such a lot / so much / so many** | quantity | *so much time; so many people* |
| **too much / too many** | excess | *too much noise; too many people* |

### 2.7 Position of intensifiers
- **Before the adjective:** *very tired, deeply concerned, absolutely fantastic.*
- **Before a participle used as an adjective:** *highly paid, fully booked.*
- **Before verbs (mid-position):** *I deeply regret that…, I strongly recommend…, I completely forgot…*
- **End position for some:** *I appreciate it greatly. / I love it very much. / He tried hard.*
- **Not between verb and object** (✗ *I like very much football*) → ✅ *I like football very much.* / *I really like football.*
- **"Enough" after adjective** (✗ *enough big*) → ✅ *big enough.*
- **Intensifiers don't pile up** (✗ *very really extremely good*).

## 3. Why Native Speakers Do This
Three reasons.

1. **Precision and expressivity.** *"I'm deeply grateful"* says something different from *"I'm very grateful."* The first carries feeling, the second only degree. Natives pick the adverb that adds a *shade* of meaning, not just volume.
2. **Collocational habit.** Over time, certain adverb–adjective pairs become **so familiar** that they behave as single units (*bitterly cold, fast asleep, blissfully unaware*). The brain retrieves the pair, not the parts (Lesson 51).
3. **Social tuning.** Intensifiers do social work. *Absolutely brilliant!* is warmth and enthusiasm. *Slightly disappointing* is polite criticism. *Not entirely convinced* is polite disagreement. The strength you choose communicates attitude, status, and politeness. A learner who only uses *very* has a narrow expressive range, even with perfect grammar.

Also: **intensifiers decay.** Words like *terribly, awfully, dreadfully, really, totally, literally, super, mega* started as strong, then weakened through overuse. Natives constantly invent new intensifiers (*super, so, hella, mad, dead, proper, well, wicked*) to refresh the effect. Stay with the stable ones.

## 4. When to Use / When Not To

**Use**
- Strong, specific collocations in writing and speeches (*deeply concerned, highly unlikely, widely accepted*) instead of repeating *very*.
- Downtoners to **soften** criticism and requests: *a bit late, slightly concerned, not entirely convinced, somewhat disappointing.*
- Maximizers with extreme adjectives for **emotional warmth:** *absolutely delighted, utterly delicious.*
- *Really* as your safe default in speech.

**Don't**
- Don't use *very* with extreme or classifying adjectives (✗ *very amazing, very impossible, very unique*).
- Don't use *absolutely/utterly/totally* with ordinary gradable adjectives (✗ *absolutely good, totally big*).
- Don't use *highly* with feelings or physical qualities (✗ *highly cold, highly sad*).
- Don't use *very* + comparative (✗ *very better, very more*).
- Don't use *hardly* with *not* (✗ *I don't hardly know*).
- Don't use *much* before an adjective (✗ *much tired, much good*).
- Don't stack: *very, very, very* or *really really extremely* sounds childish in writing.
- Don't use *literally* as a pure intensifier in formal writing; it means "in a literal sense." In casual speech it is common but often criticized.
- **Avoid** slang intensifiers (*mad, hella, wicked, proper, well, dead*) unless you know the region and register.

## 5. Frequency & Register

**Frequency (spoken + written, approximate order)**
1. *very, really, so, too, quite, pretty, enough*
2. *absolutely, completely, totally, extremely, fairly, rather, a bit*
3. *highly, deeply, fully, entirely, particularly, especially*
4. *utterly, remarkably, incredibly, exceptionally, bitterly, painfully, profoundly, thoroughly*

**Register**
| Register | Intensifiers |
|---|---|
| **Informal** | *really, super, so, pretty, kind of, sort of, a bit, dead (UK), totally, awfully, terribly, way (way too)* |
| **Neutral** | *very, extremely, quite, fairly, absolutely, completely, particularly, rather* |
| **Formal / academic** | *highly, deeply, profoundly, considerably, substantially, significantly, markedly, remarkably, exceptionally, entirely, wholly, fully, somewhat, relatively, marginally, appreciably* |
| **Spoken emphasis** | *absolutely!, definitely!, totally!, exactly!* |

**Academic writing tip:** avoid *very* and *really* in formal prose; use a precise adjective or a collocation: *very big* → *considerable / substantial / significant*; *very small* → *minimal / negligible / slight*; *very important* → *crucial / essential / of great importance*; *very good* → *excellent / sound / strong*; *very bad* → *poor / severe / serious*. This is the **"replace very + adjective with one stronger word"** habit.

## 6. American vs. British Notes

| Item | US | UK |
|---|---|---|
| **quite + gradable adj** | rare, usually "very" (*quite good* = pretty good, or fully; ambiguous) | *quite good* = moderately good (less than "good"). *Quite* = "to a moderate degree." |
| **quite + extreme/absolute adj** | *quite impossible* = completely | same: *quite amazing*, *quite right* = completely |
| **pretty + adj** | very common (*pretty good*) | common, informal |
| **fairly** | rarer, a bit formal | common (*fairly good* = rather less than "good") |
| **rather** | formal / literary | common, mild and slightly formal (*rather good*, *rather expensive*) |
| **really** | everywhere, informal | everywhere |
| **proper(ly)** | rare | UK informal intensifier: *proper cold, a proper mess, proper good* |
| **dead** | rare | UK informal: *dead good, dead tired, dead funny* |
| **mad** | NYC / young US: *mad cold* | rare |
| **wicked** | New England / US slang: *wicked cold* | UK/Irish slang sometimes |
| **hella** | California slang | rare |
| **well + adj** | rare (*well good* is UK slang) | UK slang: *well good, well annoyed* |
| **so (as in "so tired")** | very common | common |
| **awfully / terribly** | dated | still common in polite speech (*awfully sorry, terribly kind*) |
| **jolly** | rare | dated UK: *jolly good* |
| **blooming / bloody** | rare | UK mild/strong: *bloody awful* (strong; use cautiously) |
| **ever so** | rare | UK: *ever so grateful* |
| **I couldn't care less** vs. **I could care less** | US (illogical but common) | UK: *couldn't* |

**Warning for "quite":** British *quite* can mean either "somewhat" or "completely," depending on the adjective and stress. If a British boss says *"It's quite good,"* it may be mild praise (not enthusiasm!). In US English, *"It's pretty good"* is generally positive.

**Ambiguity with "fairly" and "rather":**
- *"The film was fairly good."* = acceptable but not great.
- *"The film was rather good."* = better than expected (UK).

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

### 7.1 Gradable vs. extreme
- ❌ *very exhausted / very fantastic / very terrible / very delicious (in formal)* → ✅ *absolutely exhausted / fantastic / terrible; really exhausted; extremely tired.*
- ❌ *absolutely tired / absolutely nice / completely big* → ✅ *very tired / very nice / really big.*
- ❌ *very unique / more unique* → ✅ *unique / truly unique / quite unique (UK)*. (*Unique* is absolute.)
- ❌ *very impossible / very perfect* → ✅ *completely impossible / absolutely perfect.*

### 7.2 *Muy / mucho / bastante / demasiado / tan / tanto*
| Spanish | ❌ Error | ✅ English |
|---|---|---|
| *muy cansado* | much tired | **very** tired |
| *mucho más fácil* | very easier / much more easy | **much** easier / **a lot** easier |
| *muy mejor* | very better | **much** better / **far** better |
| *mucho* (with verbs) | I like very football | I like football **a lot** / I **really** like football |
| *bastante* (= quite) | I'm bastante tired | I'm **pretty / quite / fairly** tired |
| *bastante* (= enough) | I have bastante money | I have **enough** money |
| *demasiado* | very expensive (when you mean "too") | **too** expensive |
| *demasiado* + noun | too much people | **too many** people / **too much** noise |
| *tan… que* | so… that (✅) / such… that | **so** tired **that**… |
| *tanto/a(s)* | so much / so many (✅) | **so much** time / **so many** people |
| *tan* + noun | so a nice day | **such a** nice day |
| *muy bueno* | very good (✅) | very good / really good / great |
| *muchísimo* | very very much | **a great deal / tremendously / enormously** |
| *sumamente* | sumamente important | **extremely / highly** important |
| *totalmente de acuerdo* | totally agree (✅) | **completely / totally** agree, or **I couldn't agree more** |
| *realmente* | realmente (✗) | **really / truly** |
| *absolutamente* | absolutamente | **absolutely** |
| *casi* | almost (✅) / nearly | almost / nearly (with *all, every*, verbs) |
| *apenas* | scarcely/hardly (✅) | **hardly/barely** (not "apenas" used as "just") |

### 7.3 Positional errors
- ❌ *I like very much music.* → ✅ *I like music very much.* / *I really like music.*
- ❌ *She is enough tall.* → ✅ *She is tall enough.*
- ❌ *It's too much expensive.* → ✅ *It's too expensive.*
- ❌ *very much tired* → ✅ *very tired.*
- ❌ *I very like it.* → ✅ *I like it very much / a lot.*
- ❌ *It is very much interesting.* → ✅ *It is very interesting.*

### 7.4 *Very* + comparative or superlative
- ❌ *very bigger / very more important / very better.* → ✅ *much bigger / far more important / a lot better.*
- ❌ *the very best* is fine ✅ (emphatic, *very* = precise). But ❌ *the very most.*

### 7.5 *Hardly* and negative doubles
- ❌ *I don't hardly know him.* → ✅ *I hardly know him.*
- ❌ *I can't hardly wait* (colloquial in US; avoid) → ✅ *I can hardly wait.*
- ❌ *hardly ever not* → ✅ *hardly ever.*
- ❌ *hardly + not* patterns.

### 7.6 Wrong collocation (translation of *muy* with the wrong booster)
- ❌ *highly worried / highly sad* → ✅ *deeply worried / deeply sad.*
- ❌ *deeply recommended* → ✅ *highly recommended.*
- ❌ *highly cold / very cold* → ✅ *bitterly cold* (or *very cold*).
- ❌ *completely hot* → ✅ *boiling hot / scorching hot.*
- ❌ *strongly tired* → ✅ *very / extremely tired.*
- ❌ *deeply tall* → ✅ *very tall.*
- ❌ *a strong sleep* → ✅ *a deep sleep.*
- ❌ *deeply asleep* is fine ✅, but ✅ *fast asleep* is the more natural pair.
- ❌ *heavily recommended* → ✅ *highly recommended.*
- ❌ *a strongly disappointment* → ✅ *a bitter disappointment* (adj) / *bitterly disappointed* (adv).

### 7.7 Other
- ❌ *I am very agree* → ✅ *I completely agree / I agree.* (*Agree* is a verb; *very* can't modify it.)
- ❌ *I am very in love* → ✅ *I'm deeply / madly in love.*
- ❌ *It is very good, no?* → ✅ *It's really good, isn't it?* (tag)
- ❌ *so much* + adjective: *so much tired* → ✅ *so tired.*
- ❌ *such tired* → ✅ *so tired.* (*such* requires a noun)
- ❌ *a so big house* → ✅ *such a big house / a really big house.*
- ❌ *actually* for *actualmente*; *actually* = in fact (false friend): ✅ *currently*.
- ❌ *literally* overuse: *I'm literally dying* (in careful writing) → ✅ *I'm exhausted.*
- ❌ *too much good* → ✅ *too good / very good.*
- ❌ *enough good* → ✅ *good enough.*
- ❌ *really much* → ✅ *really / a lot.*
- ❌ *I appreciate very much your help* → ✅ *I really appreciate your help.* / *I appreciate your help very much.*

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ **"Very is the safe intensifier for everything."** — It's safe grammatically but weak stylistically, and it is wrong with extreme or classifying adjectives.
- ❌ **"Absolutely is just a stronger very."** — It's not; it belongs to a different class (maximizers) and has different partners.
- ❌ **"Intensifier = emphasis only."** — They also carry **meaning** and **attitude** (*bitterly* = negative emotion; *pleasantly* = positive).
- ❌ **"Quite means very."** — In UK English, it usually means "moderately." In US English it varies. Check context and stress.
- ❌ **"Strong adverb = formal."** — *Awfully, terribly, dreadfully, super, totally* are informal, even though they sound dramatic.
- ❌ **"Any adverb can strengthen any adjective."** — Collocational preference is real: *highly unlikely* but not *highly sad*.
- ❌ **"Using many intensifiers makes my English more expressive."** — The reverse: overuse deflates their effect. Reserve strong words for strong moments.
- ❌ **"Downtoners are weak language."** — Softeners are essential politeness tools in English, especially in business and academic contexts (*a little concerned, not entirely convinced*).
- ❌ **"Hardly is the same as hard."** — *Hard* = with effort/difficult; *hardly* = almost not (*hardly worked* = barely worked).
- ❌ **"Enough is placed before the adjective, like 'bastante'."** — *Enough* follows adjectives and adverbs, precedes nouns.

## 9. Pronunciation Note
1. **Stress the intensifier for emphasis.** In neutral speech, the stress falls on the adjective or noun (*very TIRED*); in emphatic speech, the intensifier takes extra stress and a rise: *"It was SO good!" "That's ABsolutely ridiculous!" "I'm REALLY sorry."*
2. **Word stress in long intensifiers**
   - ab-so-**LUTE**-ly /ˌæbsəˈluːtli/ (stress on 3rd syllable; also secondary on 1st)
   - ex-**TREME**-ly /ɪkˈstriːmli/
   - in-**CRED**-i-bly /ɪnˈkrɛdəbli/
   - **TER**-ri-bly /ˈtɛrəbli/
   - **UT**-ter-ly /ˈʌɾərli/ (US flap)
   - com-**PLETE**-ly /kəmˈpliːtli/
   - **TO**-tal-ly /ˈtoʊɾəli/
   - **HIGH**-ly /ˈhaɪli/
   - **DEEP**-ly /ˈdiːpli/
   - **BIT**-ter-ly /ˈbɪɾərli/
   - **PAIN**-ful-ly /ˈpeɪnfəli/
   - **THOR**-ough-ly /ˈθɜːroʊli/ (US) / /ˈθʌrəli/ (UK)
   - re-**MARK**-a-bly /rɪˈmɑːrkəbli/
   - **EN**-tire-ly /ɪnˈtaɪərli/ (stress on 2nd syllable: en-**TIRE**-ly)
3. **Spanish-speaker trouble spots**
   - *-ly* endings: /li/, not /laɪ/. Keep it short: *really* /ˈriːli/ or /ˈrɪəli/, not "real-lie."
   - *quite* /kwaɪt/ vs. *quiet* /ˈkwaɪət/ (two syllables) vs. *quit* /kwɪt/: a common minimal-pair trap.
   - *really* /ˈrɪəli/ (US /ˈrɪli/ casual): the /r/ is not rolled.
   - *very* /ˈvɛri/ (not /ˈbɛri/): voiced labiodental /v/.
   - *extremely* has a consonant cluster /kstr/: say /ɪkˈstriːmli/, not "es-treme-ly."
   - *absolutely* contains /bs/: /ˈæbsəˌluːtli/.
   - *particularly* /pərˈtɪkjələrli/: five syllables; stress on *-tic-*.
   - *pretty* /ˈprɪɾi/ (US flap). *Pretty* ≠ *petty*.
   - *fairly* /ˈfɛrli/. *Rather* /ˈræðər/ (US) /ˈrɑːðə/ (UK).
   - *enough* /ɪˈnʌf/ (not "enuf" as /e/). Stress on 2nd syllable.
   - *too* /tuː/ vs. *two* /tuː/ vs. *to* /tə/: unstressed *to* reduces; stressed *too* is long.
   - *so* /soʊ/, *such* /sʌtʃ/ (not /sutʃ/).
4. **Intonation for "quite" and "pretty" (UK/US).** In UK speech, *quite* with a **falling** tone and stress on *quite* ("It's QUITE good") is mild; stress on the adjective ("It's quite GOOD") is a bit warmer. With extreme adjectives, *"It's quite AMAZing"* means very. Listen for this.
5. **Sarcasm and understatement.** *"Not bad"* and *"Not exactly cheap"* are understatements; the **intonation** (slow, low) tells you the speaker means the opposite. Understatement is a major feature of British humor.
6. **Chunk pronunciation.** Say *fast asleep*, *bitterly cold*, *highly unlikely* as single rhythmic units, not as two separate words. Shadow native clips.

## 10. Memory Technique

### 10.1 The three buckets (Gradable / Extreme / Absolute)

|---|---|---|
| *very, really, extremely, fairly, a bit* | *absolutely, utterly, completely, simply, really* | *completely, absolutely, entirely, almost, virtually* |
| good, tired, cold, big, difficult | fantastic, exhausted, freezing, enormous, impossible* | dead, unique, perfect, essential, married |

(*Impossible* is both extreme and absolute; it takes *absolutely/completely.*)

### 10.2 The thermometer (strength ladder)
Draw a vertical thermometer. Fill it from bottom to top with downtoners → compromisers → boosters → maximizers. Put your own examples next to each level. This makes strength **visual**.

### 10.3 Partner families (the "knob" system)
Group intensifiers by the **kind of adjective** they like:
- **Feeling knob:** *deeply, bitterly, genuinely, painfully, profoundly, sincerely, terribly, desperately*
- **Likelihood/quality knob:** *highly, extremely, remarkably, exceptionally, reasonably*
- **Completeness knob:** *fully, completely, totally, entirely, thoroughly, utterly, wholly*
- **Sensory/weather knob:** *bitterly cold, scorching hot, pitch black, bone dry, crystal clear*
- **Opinion/judgment knob:** *perfectly reasonable, wildly inaccurate, grossly unfair, hopelessly romantic, hopelessly lost*
- **Surprise/emotion on events:** *pleasantly surprised, unpleasantly surprised, genuinely shocked, bitterly disappointed*

When you meet a new adjective, ask: **"Which knob does it belong to?"**

### 10.4 Chunk cards (cloze)
- Front: *The chances of success are ___ unlikely.* → **highly**
- Front: *I'm ___ grateful for your help.* → **deeply / extremely / very**
- Front: *It was ___ cold outside.* → **bitterly**
- Front: *The baby was ___ asleep.* → **fast**
- Front: *It is ___ obvious that…* → **painfully / perfectly / abundantly**
- Front: *The house was ___ black.* → **pitch**
- Front: *I ___ agree.* → **completely / totally / fully**
- Front: *It's ___ the best option.* → **by far**

### 10.5 Substitution drill
Take *very + adjective* sentences from your own writing and replace with a collocation:
- *very worried* → deeply concerned
- *very tired* → utterly exhausted / worn out
- *very good* → exceptionally good / excellent
- *very bad* → appallingly bad / dreadful
- *very important* → crucially important / vital
- *very cold* → bitterly cold / freezing
- *very different* → radically different / entirely different
- *very small* → tiny / minuscule
- *very sure* → absolutely sure / fully convinced
- *very quickly* → in no time / at lightning speed / rapidly
- *very happy* → absolutely delighted / overjoyed
- *very surprised* → astonished / taken aback / pleasantly surprised

Aim: **replace one "very" per paragraph per day**.

### 10.6 Spaced retrieval
Review cards on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30 (Lesson 51). Mix gradable/extreme items to practice **discrimination** (interleaving).

### 10.7 The 5-second check
Before using an intensifier, ask:
1. Is the adjective **gradable, extreme, or absolute**? (*a little ___?* test)
2. Which **knob** (feeling, likelihood, completeness, sensory)?
3. Does it match the **register**?
4. Am I **overusing** it?

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1: Gradable + very/extremely; extreme + absolutely.** Memorize this as a rule: *very cold / absolutely freezing*, *very tired / absolutely exhausted*, *very good / absolutely brilliant.*

> **Pattern 2: *Really* is the bridge.** It works with both gradable and extreme adjectives, and in both speech and informal writing.

> **Pattern 3: *Highly* = probability, quality, skill, regard.** *highly unlikely, highly effective, highly skilled, highly recommended, highly respected.* Never with raw feelings.

> **Pattern 4: *Deeply* = feeling or conviction.** *deeply concerned, deeply moved, deeply regret, deeply committed, deeply rooted.*

> **Pattern 5: *Fully/completely/entirely/totally* = completion or agreement.** *fully aware, completely different, entirely my fault, totally agree.*

> **Pattern 6: Fixed adverb–adjective pairs act as units.** *wide awake, fast asleep, bitterly cold, blissfully unaware, pitch black, bone dry, crystal clear, razor sharp, brand new, dead serious.* Treat them as single vocabulary items.

> **Pattern 7: Comparatives take *much/far/a lot/even/still/slightly,* never *very*.** *much better, far worse, even more interesting, slightly cheaper, a lot easier.*

> **Pattern 8: *So* + adj/adv; *such (a)* + (adj) + noun.** *so tired / such a tiring day.* The result clause uses *that*: *so tired that I fell asleep.*

> **Pattern 9: *Too* is negative; *enough* follows the adjective.** *too hot (to drink), hot enough (to cook).* *Too…to* and *enough…to* connect to infinitive structures (Lesson 7+).

> **Pattern 10: Softening with negatives.** *not very, not particularly, not entirely, not quite, not exactly, not all that, not especially, not altogether.* *Not entirely convinced* is far more polite than *wrong.*

> **Pattern 11: Understatement.** *not bad* (= good), *a bit of a problem* (= serious problem), *rather unfortunate* (= bad), *not the best idea* (= terrible idea). Common in British English.

> **Pattern 12: Adverb + participle as pre-modifier.** *a highly paid job, a fully booked hotel, a widely used method, a deeply flawed argument, a heavily armed guard.* Hyphenation: *a well-known author* (adv + participle) but *a highly respected author* (-ly adverbs are not hyphenated).

> **Connection to Lesson 52:** adjective + noun collocations (*heavy rain*) and adverb + adjective collocations (*bitterly cold*) work together in descriptions: *a bitterly cold wind, heavy rain, a deeply troubling report.*

> **Connection to Lesson 19 (quantifiers):** *quite a few, a good many, a great deal of* are quantity intensifiers.

> **Connection to Lesson 20 (pronouns and reflexives):** emphatic reflexives (*I did it myself*) are another kind of emphasis; compare with degree adverbs.

> **Connection to Lesson 23 (inversion/emphasis):** negative adverbs trigger inversion (*Never have I felt so…; Hardly had he arrived when…; Not only was it expensive, but…*). Emphasis can come from structure as well as from intensifiers.

> **Connection to Lesson 24 (cleft sentences):** *What I really need is…; It was absolutely essential that…* use intensifiers inside cleft structures.

> **Connection to Lesson 28 (discourse markers) and Phase 6:** *honestly, frankly, seriously, genuinely, literally, basically, essentially* are adverbs that operate at sentence level, closely related to intensifiers.

> **Connection to Lesson 54 (synonym precision):** synonyms differ in which intensifiers they accept: *angry* (very/extremely) vs. *furious* (absolutely/utterly).

## 12. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1 — Basic**
- It's **very** cold today.
- I'm **really** tired.
- The food was **really** good.
- She's **quite** tall.
- It's **too** expensive.

**Level 2 — Everyday**
- I'm **absolutely** exhausted.
- The weather was **absolutely** terrible.
- It's **a bit** expensive, but **pretty** good quality.
- He was **fast** asleep.
- I'm **completely** sure.

**Level 3 — Work and study**
- It's **highly** unlikely that we'll finish by Friday.
- I'm **deeply** grateful for your support.
- The results were **remarkably** consistent.
- The proposal is **fairly** realistic but **slightly** over budget.
- I **strongly** recommend that you read the report.

**Level 4 — Analytical**
- The evidence is **overwhelmingly** in favor of the new policy.
- The two studies reached **vastly** different conclusions.
- The argument is **fundamentally** flawed.
- I'm **not entirely** convinced by the data.
- The project is **nowhere near** completion.

**Level 5 — Native-like**
- "The decision was **bitterly** opposed by **a handful of** members, but it was **overwhelmingly** approved."
- "She was **blissfully** unaware that the entire office was **fully** informed."
- "It's **perfectly** reasonable to be **slightly** apprehensive, though the odds are **vastly** better than you think."
- "The room was **pitch** black and **eerily** quiet, and he felt **painfully** alone."
- "I was **pleasantly** surprised by how **remarkably** well the team **worked together**, given how **utterly** exhausted everyone was."

**Understatement and softening (polite English)**
- *"That's not the best idea."* (= bad idea)
- *"I'm a little concerned about the timeline."* (= worried)
- *"It's slightly higher than we expected."* (= much higher)
- *"I'm afraid I don't entirely agree."* (= disagree)
- *"The service was less than ideal."* (= poor)
- *"We're not quite there yet."* (= not finished)

**Mini-dialogue (read aloud with natural stress)**
> **A:** How was the trip?
> **B:** Absolutely fantastic! The weather was perfect, the hotel was fully booked but the staff were incredibly helpful, and the food was really good.
> **A:** Was it expensive?
> **B:** A bit pricey, but not unreasonably so. Honestly, it was well worth it.

**Mini Quiz**
1. Gradable: on a scale (*good, tired*); extreme: already at the top (*fantastic, exhausted*); classifying: yes/no categories (*unique, dead, perfect, married*).
2. *Exhausted* is already extreme; *very* needs gradable adjectives. Say *absolutely/completely exhausted* or *very tired.*
3. *Really* works with both gradable and extreme adjectives (and in informal contexts).
4. *Highly unlikely, deeply concerned, bitterly cold.*
5. Comparatives take *much/far/a lot/even,* not *very*: *much better, far better, a lot better.*
6. *Enough* follows adjectives: *tall enough* ✅; *enough tall* ✗.
7. *Hard* = with effort/difficult; *hardly* = almost not (negative meaning).
8. UK *quite good* = moderately good; US *pretty good* usually = quite positive (better than mild).
9. It softens disagreement, making it polite and less direct.
10. Repetition becomes dull and vague; precise collocations carry more meaning and sound more natural.
11. ab-so-**LUTE**-ly; ex-**TREME**-ly; en-**TIRE**-ly.
12. *So* modifies an adjective/adverb alone (*so tired*); *such* needs a noun phrase (*such a tiring day*).
