# Lesson 55 — Register Ladders: Formal ↔ Neutral ↔ Informal ↔ Slang
### Phase 5: Vocabulary as a System | Module 5 of 8

---

## 0. Where this fits
Lesson 54 sorted near-synonyms by meaning, intensity, collocation, and connotation. One dimension kept returning: **register**, the level of formality. Lesson 55 gives register its own lesson, because it is the dimension where fluent Spanish speakers make their most *socially* costly mistakes. A wrong collocation makes you sound foreign. A wrong register can make you sound rude, cold, childish, or pompous.

The aim is a **dial**, not a switch. By the end you should be able to take one idea (a request, an apology, a refusal, a disagreement) and say it at four or five levels, then choose the level that fits the person, the place, and the medium.

This lesson also prepares three later ones: Lesson 56 (academic register and nominalization), Lesson 57 (professional register), and Phase 6 (natural spoken English).

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
Register is a **dress code for language**. You own several outfits:

| Outfit | Language equivalent | Where you wear it |
|---|---|---|
| Tuxedo / formal suit | formal register: *I would be most grateful if you could…* | legal letters, formal complaints, speeches, academic papers |
| Business casual | neutral register: *Could you send me the report?* | most emails, meetings, service encounters, news |
| Jeans and a T-shirt | informal register: *Can you send me the report?* | friends, family, close colleagues, texts |
| Pyjamas / gym clothes | very informal / slang: *Shoot me that report.* | best friends, in-group jokes |

Nobody is "wrong" in jeans. They are wrong *at a funeral*. Likewise, *Hey dude, gimme the report* is perfect language in the wrong room.

Native speakers change outfits dozens of times a day without noticing: a formal tone with a bank clerk, a neutral tone with a colleague, a relaxed tone with a friend, a baby-talk tone with a child. This is called **style-shifting**, and it is a core part of being fluent. A learner who has only one outfit sounds either too stiff (textbook English) or too casual (TV-series English).

> **Rule of thumb:** *When in doubt, go one step more formal than the other person, then follow their lead.* Moving down is easy; recovering from being too casual is hard.

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 What register is
**Register** is the variety of language used in a particular situation. Linguists (following Halliday) describe it with three variables:

| Variable | Question | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Field** | What is going on? (topic/activity) | law, medicine, football, cooking, gossip |
| **Tenor** | Who is talking to whom? (relationship, power, distance) | boss–employee, friends, stranger–stranger, teacher–student |
| **Mode** | Through what channel? (spoken/written, planned/spontaneous) | text message, email, speech, essay, phone call |

Change any one and the language changes. A professor writing a paper (academic field, distant tenor, written mode) and the same professor chatting at a barbecue (different field, close tenor, spoken mode) sound like different people.

### 2.2 The scale of formality
Many models exist. A practical one for learners has five levels:

| Level | Label | Typical features | Example (asking for help) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | **Very formal / frozen** | fixed, legal, ceremonial; passive; Latinate; no contractions | *Assistance would be greatly appreciated.* |
| 4 | **Formal** | complete sentences, careful vocabulary, polite distance | *I would be grateful if you could assist me.* |
| 3 | **Neutral / consultative** | standard grammar, common words, some contractions | *Could you help me, please?* |
| 2 | **Informal / casual** | contractions, phrasal verbs, ellipsis, fillers | *Can you give me a hand?* |
| 1 | **Intimate / slang** | shortcuts, in-group slang, swearing, incomplete sentences | *Gimme a sec, mate—help me out?* |

Real speech slides along this line; the boundaries blur. What matters is **relative** position: is this more or less formal than the situation calls for?

### 2.3 How register shows up in language

| Feature | Formal | Neutral | Informal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Vocabulary** | Latinate: *obtain, require, inform, commence* | common: *get, need, tell, start* | phrasal/slang: *pick up, gotta, fill in, kick off* |
| **Verbs** | single-word verbs | mix | phrasal verbs (Phase 4) |
| **Contractions** | avoided (*cannot, do not*) | common (*can't, don't*) | always, plus reductions (*gonna, wanna, dunno*) |
| **Voice** | frequent passive, impersonal (*It is recommended that…*) | active, some passive | active, personal (*I think we should…*) |
| **Sentence structure** | long, subordinate, nominalized (*the implementation of the policy*) | medium | short, fragments, run-ons |
| **Pronouns** | *one*, *the author*, avoids *you* | *you*, *we* | *you*, *ya*, *y'all* (US South), *mate* |
| **Mood** | subjunctive in set phrases (*I suggest that he be present*) | indicative | indicative |
| **Politeness** | indirect, hedged (*I wonder whether you might…*) | modal (*Could you…?*) | direct (*Can you…? / Do it.*) |
| **Discourse markers** | *furthermore, nevertheless, in addition, however* | *also, but, so, anyway* | *plus, though, like, you know, I mean* |
| **Punctuation / form** | full punctuation; semicolons | standard | dashes, ellipses, emoji, no capitals in texts |
| **Openings/closings** | *Dear Sir or Madam… Yours faithfully* | *Hello Ana… Best regards* | *Hi!… Cheers / Talk soon / xx* |
| **Tentativeness** | *It would appear that…* | *It seems that…* | *Looks like…* |

### 2.4 The "Latin vs. phrasal" shortcut (Lesson 54 revisited)
The single most useful trick for shifting register is **swapping a phrasal or Germanic verb for a Latinate verb** (up) or the reverse (down).

| Informal (phrasal/Germanic) | Neutral | Formal (Latinate) |
|---|---|---|
| *find out* | *discover* | *ascertain* |
| *look into* | *investigate* | *examine* |
| *put off* | *delay* | *postpone / defer* |
| *get* | *receive* | *obtain / acquire* |
| *ask for* | *request* | *solicit* |
| *show up* | *arrive* | *attend* |
| *call off* | *cancel* | *discontinue* |
| *go on* | *continue* | *proceed* |
| *come up with* | *think of* | *devise* |
| *set up* | *establish* | *institute* |
| *carry out* | *do* | *conduct / execute* |
| *get rid of* | *remove* | *dispose of* |
| *talk about* | *discuss* | *address* |
| *look at* | *consider* | *review* |
| *tell* | *inform* | *notify / advise* |
| *need* | *require* | *necessitate* |
| *help* | *assist* | *facilitate* |
| *end* | *finish* | *terminate / conclude* |
| *buy* | *purchase* | *procure* |

Caution: formal is not always better. Going all the way up produces **officialese** (*Prior to the commencement of the proceedings, participants are requested to refrain from…*).

### 2.5 Politeness and directness: the second axis
Register has a second dimension: **how direct** you are. Spanish speakers, used to the *tú/usted* system, often treat politeness as a matter of choosing the pronoun. English has no such switch. Instead, politeness is built through:

1. **Modals and past forms** (*can → could → would → might*): more distance = more polite.
2. **Questions instead of commands** (*Could you…?* not *Send me…*).
3. **Hedges** (*a bit, a little, sort of, perhaps, I was wondering, if possible*).
4. **Indirectness** (*It's a bit cold in here* = please close the window).
5. **Apology and thanks** (*Sorry to bother you… Thanks so much…*).
6. **Past tense for distance** (*I was wondering if…*, *I wanted to ask…*, *Did you want something?*).
7. **Understatement** (*not ideal, slightly disappointing, I'm not sure it works*).

Politeness is **cumulative**. Layering several devices (*I was wondering if you might possibly be able to…*) is more polite than one (*Could you…?*). Overlayering can sound sarcastic or insincere.

**The modal politeness ladder (requests)**
`Give me… < Can you…? < Could you…? < Would you…? < Would you mind…-ing? < I was wondering if you could… < I would be grateful if you could… < I would be most grateful if you might be able to…`

### 2.6 Positive vs. negative politeness
- **Positive politeness** builds closeness: *Hey, great to see you! Mind giving me a hand?* (friends, informal)
- **Negative politeness** avoids imposing: *Sorry to trouble you; would it be possible to…?* (strangers, superiors)

English-speaking cultures vary. In US business culture, warmth and informality come early (first names, *Hi Laura*), so US English can sound friendlier but still require care with requests. In UK business culture, indirectness and understatement are stronger. In many workplaces worldwide, global English is more formal and literal. Calibrate to the audience.

### 2.7 Written vs. spoken
Mode matters as much as formality.
- **Written** language tends to be denser (more nouns, longer sentences, fewer fillers).
- **Spoken** language is more fragmented, uses fillers (*well, you know, I mean*), repetitions, and ellipsis.
- Texts and chat are **written speech**: informal register, written mode.
- Presentations are **spoken writing**: formal-ish register, spoken mode, prepared.

## 3. Why Native Speakers Do This
Because register is **social information**. A listener hears your word choices and instantly infers: how well you know me, how much respect you give me, whether you are in a hurry, whether you belong to my group, how educated you are, how serious the situation is. Language carries these signals automatically.

That explains two things you may have noticed:

1. **Natives forgive grammar errors more than register errors.** A foreign accent or a dropped *-s* is "foreign." *Hey, give me the file* to a senior client is "rude."
2. **Register violations are sometimes done on purpose**, for humor or distance: *"Would you be so kind as to pass the ketchup, my good man?"* is funny because it is too formal for the situation. If you cannot tell when it's intentional, you will miss jokes.

For the learner, this means register competence is not "extra." It is part of **pragmatic competence** (knowing what to say, to whom, when), which employers and interviewers weigh heavily.

## 4. When to Use / When Not To

| Situation | Suggested level |
|---|---|
| Job application, cover letter, formal complaint, legal text | 4–5 (formal) |
| First email to a professor, client, or manager you don't know | 3–4 (neutral-formal) |
| Day-to-day email to colleagues | 3 (neutral) |
| Meetings (depending on culture) | 2–3 |
| Chatting with friends, texting | 1–2 |
| Interviews | 3, polished; avoid slang; contractions are fine |
| Academic essays | 4 (formal-neutral); no contractions, limited phrasal verbs, hedged claims (Lesson 56) |
| Presentations | 3–4, spoken style, signposting |
| Customer service | 3, warm and clear |
| Social media | varies; match the platform |

**Don't**
- Don't use slang with people you've just met or with authority.
- Don't use *Dear Sir/Madam* in an email to a friend.
- Don't use *I demand* or imperatives in requests (*Send me…*) unless you're in a hierarchy that expects them.
- Don't overload with hedges when clarity matters (safety, emergencies).
- Don't copy a native's slang if you don't know its connotations or region (*mate* is fine in the UK/Australia; unusual in US business).
- Don't use swear words, even mild ones, unless your interlocutor does and you are sure of the context. Swear words carry very different weights in different varieties.
- Don't treat formal English as "better." Excessively formal English in a casual setting sounds cold or sarcastic.

## 5. Frequency & Register
For a B2+/C1 learner the highest-value zone is **levels 2–4**: informal-neutral to formal. Slang (level 1) is largely for **recognition**, not production, until you are confident.

**Priority list (learn these ladders first):**
1. Requests (asking for help, favors, permission)
2. Apologies
3. Refusals and declining invitations
4. Disagreement and giving criticism
5. Thanks and compliments
6. Greetings, openings, closings (emails and calls)
7. Giving advice and suggestions
8. Complaining
9. Bad news and delaying
10. Agreement and acceptance

**Frequency note:** neutral register is the most common in real life. The ability to **sound neutral and warm** (not just correct) is the most valuable register skill.

## 6. American vs. British Notes
| Feature | US | UK |
|---|---|---|
| **Overall tone** | direct, friendly, quicker to first names | more indirect, understatement, hedging |
| **Email openings** | *Hi Laura,* / *Hello Laura,* | *Dear Laura,* / *Hi Laura,* |
| **Formal closings** | *Sincerely, / Best regards,* | *Yours sincerely (named) / Yours faithfully (Dear Sir/Madam)* |
| **Neutral closings** | *Best, / Thanks, / Regards,* | *Best wishes, / Kind regards,* |
| **Informal closings** | *Talk soon, / Cheers (rare), / Take care,* | *Cheers, / Speak soon, / All the best,* |
| **Titles** | *Mr./Ms./Dr.* (period) | *Mr/Mrs/Ms/Dr* (no period) |
| **Gender-neutral** | *Ms.* | *Ms* |
| **Requests** | *Could you…? / Can you…?* | *Would you mind…? / I wonder if…* |
| **Thanks** | *Thanks a lot, I appreciate it* | *Cheers, ta (very informal), much appreciated* |
| **Apologies** | *My bad* (slang), *Sorry about that* | *Sorry, ever so sorry, apologies* |
| **Agreeing** | *Totally, for sure, absolutely* | *Quite, indeed, I quite agree, fair enough* |
| **Slang for friend** | *dude, bro, buddy, man* | *mate, pal, bloke (man), lad* |
| **Money** | *bucks* | *quid* |
| **Phone** | *I'll give you a call* | *I'll give you a ring/bell* |
| **Mild exclamations** | *Wow, geez, darn* | *Blimey, crikey, bother* |
| **Swearing weight** | milder for *ass, damn, hell*; strong for *f-word* | *bloody* mild-to-medium; stronger terms common in casual speech but offensive formally |
| **Politeness formula** | *please/thank you* required | *please/thank you/sorry* constantly (even to bump into someone) |

**Practical advice:** adopt a neutral, widely understood style as your base, and change surface features (greetings, closings, spelling) to match your audience.

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

### 7.1 Directness errors (translating *quiero*, *dame*, *necesito*)
| ❌ Too direct | ✅ Natural neutral | ✅ More polite |
|---|---|---|
| *I want a coffee.* | *Could I have a coffee, please?* | *I'd like a coffee, please.* |
| *Give me the report.* | *Could you send me the report?* | *I was wondering if you could send me the report.* |
| *I need the data today.* | *Could I get the data today?* | *Would it be possible to have the data by end of day?* |
| *Tell me your name.* | *What's your name?* | *May I ask your name?* |
| *Open the window.* | *Could you open the window?* | *Would you mind opening the window?* |
| *You must come.* | *You should come.* / *You really should come.* | *It would be great if you could join us.* |
| *You are wrong.* | *I don't think that's right.* | *I'm not sure I agree.* |
| *I don't like your idea.* | *I'm not sure that'll work.* | *I have a few reservations about it.* |

*Why:* Spanish permits direct imperatives and *quiero* in service contexts (*Quiero un café*), softened by tone and *por favor*. English requires **modal/conditional softening**. *I want a coffee* sounds like a demand from a child.

### 7.2 Over-formality (translating *usted* everywhere)
| ❌ Stiff | ✅ Natural |
|---|---|
| *Dear Sir, I am writing to inform you that I will be late.* (to a coworker) | *Hi Mark, I'll be a bit late today.* |
| *I would like to request your esteemed assistance.* | *Could you help me with this?* |
| *Kindly do the needful.* | *Could you take care of this, please?* |
| *Please revert back to me.* | *Please get back to me.* |
| *I hereby confirm that…* (casual) | *Just confirming that…* |
| *Good day, sir.* (everyday) | *Hi / Hello.* |
| *Do you have the kindness to…?* | *Would you mind…?* |
| *I remain at your entire disposal.* | *Let me know if you need anything.* |
| *Without further ado, I proceed to…* | *Let's get started.* |

*Why:* Spanish business writing uses a more ceremonial style (*Estimado señor… Le saluda atentamente*). Direct translations produce archaic English. Also watch for **"Indian-English-style" and other international formulas** (*do the needful, revert back, kindly…*) that sound odd to US/UK ears.

### 7.3 Wrong salutation and sign-off
- ❌ *Dear friend, I hope this email finds you well.* to a close colleague → ✅ *Hi Sam,*
- ❌ *Hey Professor,* → ✅ *Dear Professor Smith,* / *Hello Professor Smith,*
- ❌ *Hi Mr. Smith, what's up?* → ✅ *Dear Mr. Smith, I hope you are well.*
- ❌ *Sincerely yours,* to a friend → ✅ *Best,* / *Talk soon,*
- ❌ *Atte.* (abbreviation of *atentamente*) → ✅ *Sincerely* or *Best regards.*
- ❌ *Greetings* alone (sounds like a form letter) → ✅ *Hello / Hi.*
- ❌ *Hope you are fine.* → ✅ *I hope you're well.* (*Fine* is a response to *How are you?*; use *well*.)
- ❌ *I hope this message finds you in good health.* (excessively formal) → ✅ *I hope you're doing well.*

### 7.4 Wrong titles and names
- English commonly uses **first names** quickly, even with senior colleagues in US/Australian settings. Calling everyone *Mr./Ms. Surname* may sound distant. Follow the other person's lead.
- ❌ *Teacher* as a vocative (*Teacher, I have a question*) → ✅ *Excuse me, Ms. Lopez* / *Professor…*
- ❌ *Boss* as address → ✅ first name or *Sir/Ma'am* in service/military contexts.
- ❌ *Sir* or *Madam* overused (sounds servile or sarcastic in everyday contexts).

### 7.5 Slang misuse
- Using *dude, bro, mate, mad, wicked* with strangers or in writing.
- Mixing varieties: *mate* with *awesome* with *innit*.
- Using slang words with the wrong register meaning: *pissed* (US: angry; UK: drunk), *fanny* (UK: rude), *rubber* (UK: eraser; US: condom), *randy*, *knocked up*. Avoid unless confident.
- Using the **f-word** as a filler because you hear it in series/films. In real life it's much stronger and can end careers.

### 7.6 Transferring Spanish politeness patterns
- ❌ *Excuse me, I can't understand* (too blunt) → ✅ *Sorry, could you repeat that?* / *Sorry, I didn't catch that.*
- ❌ *No, thank you* repeated stiffly → ✅ *I'm good, thanks / I'll pass, thanks.*
- ❌ *Of course yes!* → ✅ *Of course!* / *Yes, definitely.*
- ❌ *With pleasure* in every reply → ✅ *Sure / No problem / Happy to / My pleasure* (mix by register)
- ❌ *Thank you for your attention* at the end of every casual talk → fine in presentations; odd in chat.
- ❌ *Please* stacked in every sentence → in English, *please* is used once, usually at the end.

### 7.7 Phone and customer-service mistakes
- ❌ *Who are you?* → ✅ *Who's calling, please?* / *May I ask who's calling?*
- ❌ *I want to speak with Sr. Smith.* → ✅ *Could I speak to Mr. Smith, please?*
- ❌ *Wait a moment.* → ✅ *Could you hold on a moment, please?* / *Just a moment.*
- ❌ *I will transfer you.* (ok) → ✅ *I'll put you through.* / *Let me transfer you.*
- ❌ *I'm calling to complaint.* → ✅ *I'm calling to make a complaint / about a problem.*

### 7.8 "Gringo English" myths
- ❌ *Native speakers hate formal English.* — They use it when needed and respect it.
- ❌ *Contractions are always informal.* — *Don't, can't, I'm* are standard in neutral writing; avoid only in very formal and academic texts.
- ❌ *Using "whom" makes you sound smart.* — Only in formal contexts; in speech, *who* is normal (*Who did you see?*).
- ❌ *Ending a sentence with a preposition is wrong.* — It's natural in neutral/informal English (*Who are you talking to?*), though formal writing may prefer *To whom are you speaking?*

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ **"Formal = better English."** — Formal is *appropriate* in some contexts. A formal tone to a friend is distant or humorous.
- ❌ **"Informal English is bad English."** — It is correct for its context; most of everyday communication uses it.
- ❌ **"I can pick up register just from series and films."** — Media mostly teach informal and sometimes unrepresentative speech. Formal registers need reading and deliberate practice.
- ❌ **"Politeness = adding *please*."** — Politeness in English is mostly grammar (modals, hedging, past forms) and tone.
- ❌ **"Native speakers don't care about my register errors because I'm a learner."** — Some forgive, many silently judge. In interviews, emails, and customer interactions, register affects impressions.
- ❌ **"If I use a lot of phrasal verbs, I'll sound native."** — In formal writing, they can sound too casual.
- ❌ **"Contractions are 'wrong' in writing."** — Only in very formal/academic writing.
- ❌ **"English has no formal/informal 'you.'"** — It doesn't have *tú/usted*, but it has other markers: titles, first names, modals, vocabulary.
- ❌ **"Slang is only for young people."** — All ages use slang, but different slang; it's tied to groups and regions.
- ❌ **"Register is the same in speech and writing."** — Spoken formal English differs from written formal English (more signposting, repetition).

## 9. Pronunciation Note
Register also shows up in **sound**.

1. **Formal speech: careful articulation.** Full vowels, no elision, more pauses, slower pace: *I would like to/ ask/ you something.* /aɪ wʊd laɪk tuː ɑːsk juː ˈsʌmθɪŋ/.
2. **Informal speech: reductions and links.**
   - *want to* → "wanna", *going to* → "gonna", *got to* → "gotta", *kind of* → "kinda", *don't know* → "dunno", *give me* → "gimme", *let me* → "lemme", *what do you* → "whaddaya", *did you* → "didja", *could have* → "coulda".
   - Learn to **understand** all of these. Produce them selectively (informal settings only). Avoid writing them in formal contexts.
3. **Politeness intonation.**
   - **Falling tone** on requests can sound abrupt or commanding: *Could you CLOSE the window.* (↘)
   - **Rising or fall-rise tone** sounds softer, friendlier: *Could you close the WINdow?* (↗) / *Could you close the window, PLEASE?*
   - Adding **a smile in the voice** (higher pitch, wider range) conveys warmth. A flat tone with polite words can sound sarcastic or cold.
4. **Stress for emphasis changes register.** *I'm SORRY.* (sincere, strong), *I'm so sorry.* (warm), *Sorry.* (casual), *I do apologize.* (formal). Match stress to meaning.
5. **Sarcasm and understatement.** *"Thanks a LOT."* with falling tone and elongation = annoyed. *"That's... interesting."* with pauses = doubtful. *"Not bad."* with low pitch = good.
6. **Tag questions as softeners.** *It's a bit cold, isn't it?* (rising: genuine question; falling: seeking agreement.)
7. **Formal pronunciation of key words.** *Kindly /ˈkaɪndli/, assistance /əˈsɪstəns/, apologize /əˈpɑlədʒaɪz/, regret /rɪˈɡrɛt/, reservations /ˌrɛzərˈveɪʃənz/, appreciate /əˈpriːʃieɪt/, sincerely /sɪnˈsɪrli/, convenience /kənˈviːniəns/.*
8. **Spanish-speaker traps**
   - Don't add a vowel before /s/ clusters: *sorry, school, special, speak* (not "es-"). Especially in *sorry for the... / ‘scuse me.*
   - *Thank you* /θæŋk juː/ with /θ/ (not /t/ or /s/). Spanish speakers often say "sank you" or "tank you," which can sound careless in formal contexts.
   - Keep final consonants: *request* /rɪˈkwɛst/, *suggest* /səɡˈdʒɛst/.
   - Intonation: Spanish questions often rise at the end; English *wh-* questions normally fall (*What time is it?* ↘) and yes/no questions rise (*Is it ready?* ↗). Wrong intonation can sound abrupt or uncertain.

## 10. Memory Technique

### 10.1 Build a "Register Ladder" card
For each function, write five rungs. Practice moving up and down.

> **Function: ask for help**
> 5. *I would be most grateful for any assistance you could provide.*
> 4. *I was wondering if you could help me with this.*
> 3. *Could you help me with this, please?*
> 2. *Can you give me a hand?*
> 1. *Help me out here, will you?* (very close)

Say each rung aloud with the right tone. The **motor memory** helps retrieval.

### 10.2 The Four-Version Rewrite
Take any short message and rewrite it as:
1. **Text to a friend** (informal)
2. **Message to a colleague** (neutral)
3. **Email to a client** (neutral-formal)
4. **Formal letter** (formal)

Do one per day for a week. This builds flexibility.

### 10.3 The "Who's listening?" check
Before speaking or writing, ask three questions:
1. **Who** is the audience? (relationship, power)
2. **What** is the medium? (text, email, speech)
3. **What** is at stake? (risk, importance, politeness)

Then pick the rung.

### 10.4 Model sentences to memorize (the "politeness toolkit")
- *Could you possibly…?*
- *I was wondering if…*
- *Would it be possible to…?*
- *Would you mind -ing…?*
- *I'm afraid that…*
- *I'm sorry to say that…*
- *With all due respect, …*
- *If I may…*
- *Just to clarify…*
- *Not to be rude, but… (warning: this often precedes something rude)*
- *Sorry to bother you, but…*
- *Thanks in advance.*
- *I really appreciate it.*
- *Let me know if that works for you.*

### 10.5 Chunk labels in your notebook
Add a **register tag** (F/N/I/S) to every chunk. Review by tag: "Today I'll practice only F and N chunks."

### 10.6 Observe and collect
Collect real examples from emails you receive, talks, interviews, and films. Copy an email opening and closing into your notebook, labeling its register. Over time you'll have a library of "real" models.

### 10.7 Shadowing at different registers
Find a formal speech (e.g., a university lecture or a business talk) and a casual podcast. Shadow 30 seconds from each. Notice differences in rhythm, vocabulary, and intonation.

### 10.8 Spaced retrieval
- Front: *Make this polite: "Give me the file."* → *Could you send me the file, please?*
- Front: *Formal version of "find out".* → *ascertain*
- Front: *Neutral version of "kick off".* → *start*
- Front: *Polite way to disagree.* → *I see your point, but I'm not sure I agree.*
Review on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30.

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1: Distance = politeness.** Past tense, modals, conditional, and indirect questions create distance: *Can you → Could you → Would you → I was wondering if you would…*

> **Pattern 2: Informal = shorter, formal = longer.** Informal English cuts (contractions, ellipsis); formal English expands (full forms, nominalizations).

> **Pattern 3: Phrasal verb ↔ Latinate verb.** Swap to move up or down (*find out ↔ ascertain*). Formal also prefers passive and impersonal constructions.

> **Pattern 4: Soften bad news with a buffer.** *Thanks for your proposal. Unfortunately, we won't be able to…* (positive opening + negative + positive close or alternative). Never open with *No*.

> **Pattern 5: Sandwich feedback.** *Positive – constructive – positive.* *I really like the structure. One thing I'd change is… Overall, it's a strong draft.*

> **Pattern 6: Hedge opinions.** *I think, it seems, perhaps, I'd say, in my view, it could be argued.* Strong claims without hedges sound arrogant in neutral/formal contexts (Lesson 56).

> **Pattern 7: Small talk precedes business.** In many English-speaking contexts, a brief friendly opening (*How are you? How was your weekend?*) comes before requests. Skipping it can feel abrupt.

> **Pattern 8: Mirror the other person.** If they write *Hi Laura*, reply *Hi*. If they use first names, follow. If they say *Dear Professor*, keep formal. Mirroring is the safest strategy.

> **Pattern 9: Email structure by register.** Informal: greeting + 1–2 lines + sign-off. Neutral: greeting + purpose + details + closing line + sign-off. Formal: salutation + reference/purpose + detailed paragraphs + formal close.

> **Pattern 10: Tone markers in writing.** Exclamation marks (friendly), emojis (informal), capital letters (shouting), full stops in short replies (*Fine.* = cold). Be aware of how these are read.

> **Connection to Lesson 54:** register is one of the six dimensions of synonym choice.

> **Connection to Lesson 53:** intensifier choice also tracks register (*really* informal; *extremely* neutral; *considerably, markedly* formal).

> **Connection to Lessons 14–17 (modals):** politeness relies on modal distance (*can/could/would/might*), a direct application of your modals work.

> **Connection to Lesson 22 (reported speech):** reporting verbs signal stance and register (*said, mentioned, stated, informed*).

> **Connection to Lesson 28 (discourse markers):** markers also have registers (*furthermore* formal; *plus* informal; *anyway* spoken).

> **Connection to Lesson 56 (next):** academic register depends on nominalization, hedging, and impersonal style.

### 12.2 One message, four registers

**Message:** "I can't come to the meeting tomorrow because I'm ill. Can someone take notes?"

- **Text to a friend:** *Can't make the meeting tomorrow, feeling rough. Can someone take notes?*
- **Message to a colleague:** *Hi all, I'm unwell and won't be able to join tomorrow's meeting. Could someone take notes for me?*
- **Email to a manager:** *Dear Ms. Taylor, I'm afraid I'm unwell and will be unable to attend tomorrow's meeting. Would it be possible for a colleague to take notes? I will review them upon my return. Best regards, Camila*
- **Formal letter:** *Dear Ms. Taylor, I regret to inform you that, owing to illness, I shall be unable to attend the meeting scheduled for tomorrow. I would be grateful if the minutes could be circulated. Yours sincerely, Camila García*

### 12.3 Email: wrong vs. right
> ❌ *"Hey boss, I want the day off tomorrow. Tell me if it's ok."*
> ✅ *"Hi Ms. Lee, I was wondering if I could take tomorrow off. I've finished the report and Sam can cover for me. Let me know if that's okay. Thanks!"*

### 12.4 Advanced contrasts (same idea, different feel)
- *He died.* (neutral) / *He passed away.* (gentle, formal) / *He's no longer with us.* (euphemistic) / *He kicked the bucket.* (slang, joking)
- *She was fired.* / *She was let go.* / *She was made redundant.* (UK, no fault) / *She got the boot.*
- *He's drunk.* / *He's had a few.* / *He's tipsy.* / *He's wasted.* / *He's plastered.*
- *toilet / restroom (US) / bathroom (US) / loo (UK) / WC / lavatory (formal) / john (US slang)*
- *old person / senior / elderly / geriatric (medical) / old-timer / old fogey (rude)*
- *pregnant / expecting / with child (formal) / up the duff (UK slang)*
- *poor / low-income / disadvantaged / broke (informal) / skint (UK) / hard up*
- *money / funds / capital / cash / dough / bread (slang) / quid / bucks*

### 12.5 Native-like control
- "Would you mind if I jumped in here for a second?" (polite interruption, level 3–4 with informal phrasal verb)
- "I hate to be a pain, but would you have a minute?" (self-deprecating softener)
- "I'm not sure that's the best way forward, to be honest." (hedged disagreement)
- "That said, I do see where you're coming from." (acknowledging)
- "Sorry, I didn't catch that. Could you say it again?" (polite request to repeat)
- "No worries at all." (reassurance, informal-neutral)
- "I'd really appreciate it if you could keep me posted." (neutral-formal request)

## 17. Speaking Practice

**Drill 1 — Ladder recitation.** Say each rung aloud, changing your tone to match:
> "Give me a hand." → "Can you help me?" → "Could you help me, please?" → "I was wondering if you could help me." → "I would be most grateful for your assistance."

**Drill 2 — Soften it.** Say the polite version aloud:
- "Give me the report." → "Could you send me the report, please?"
- "You're wrong." → "I'm not sure I agree."
- "I don't understand." → "Sorry, I'm not sure I follow. Could you explain that again?"
- "Wait." → "Could you hold on a moment, please?"

**Drill 3 — Tone shift.** Say "Thanks" five ways: flat/cold, warm, sarcastic, formal (*Thank you very much*), enthusiastic (*Thanks so much!*). Notice how pitch, stress, and speed change.

**Drill 4 — Role-play: three audiences.** Ask to leave early to (a) a friend, (b) a colleague, (c) your boss. Record each. Compare vocabulary and tone.

**Drill 5 — Phone call.** Role-play: call a company to complain about a delayed order. Use: *Hello, I'm calling about…; I'm afraid there's a problem…; I'd appreciate it if…; Could you tell me what can be done?; Thank you for your help.*

**Drill 6 — Interrupting politely.** Practice: *Sorry to interrupt, but…; If I could just add something…; Can I jump in here?; Before we move on, I'd like to…; May I make a quick point?*

**Drill 7 — Role-play (feedback).**
Partner: "Here's my draft. What do you think?"
You: "I think the structure is really clear, and your examples are strong. One thing I might suggest is shortening the introduction a little. Overall, it's a good start."
