# Lesson 71 — Sounding Natural vs. Sounding Translated
### Phase 8: Listening & Speaking Fluency | Module 3 of 3 (Phase Capstone)

---

## 0. Where this fits
You can now hear fast speech (Lesson 69) and keep a conversation moving (Lesson 70). The last question of Phase 8 is the one advanced learners care about most:

> *"My English is correct. People understand me. But it still sounds like… translated Spanish. How do I fix that?"*

This lesson is the **capstone of the whole course so far**. It gathers what you learned in earlier phases (grammar, verbs, prepositions, phrasal verbs, vocabulary, spoken English, pronunciation) and turns it into a **diagnostic system**: you will learn to spot *why* a sentence sounds translated and how to rebuild it the way a native speaker would.

Connections:
- **Lessons 1–5:** the Translation Trap and the habit of thinking directly in English.
- **Phases 1–4:** grammar, key verbs, prepositions, phrasal verbs (the most common sources of "translated" sound).
- **Phase 5:** collocations, false friends, register, idioms.
- **Phase 6:** hedging, discourse words, small talk, turn-taking.
- **Phase 7:** rhythm, weak forms, and intonation (translated *sound*, not only translated words).
- **Lesson 70:** the real-time tools you need to apply this live.
- **Phase 9:** the same ideas in writing.

> **Core idea:** "Sounding translated" is almost never one big mistake. It is a **pile of small choices** that follow Spanish habits: the word, the pairing, the structure, the politeness level, the rhythm. Fix them at the right level and the whole effect disappears.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
Imagine two tailors. One takes a Spanish suit and **re-sews each piece to English measurements**: the sleeves are right, the length is right, but the suit still has a Spanish cut. The other **starts with an English pattern** and uses your measurements. The second suit fits and looks local.

A sentence is the same. A "translated" sentence starts from a Spanish pattern and swaps in English words. A "natural" sentence starts from an English pattern.

Look at this:
> ❌ "I have much interest in the position and I think that I would be a good option for the company."
> ✅ "I'm really interested in the role, and I think I'd be a good fit."

Nothing is *grammatically* terrible in the first sentence. But a native reader hears: *tengo mucho interés… una buena opción*. Each phrase is a **calque** (a word-for-word copy of a Spanish phrase).

A second image: **a path in a forest.** Native speakers walk well-worn paths: *a good fit, heavy rain, make a decision*. A translated sentence takes a shortcut through the trees. You arrive at the same place, but the walk looks odd.

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 Five levels at which a sentence can sound translated
Diagnose by level. Each level has a different fix.

| Level | What goes wrong | Example (translated → natural) | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Word** | wrong word, false friend, wrong register | *I assisted the meeting* → *I attended the meeting* | learn the real word |
| **2. Collocation** | right words, wrong pairing | *do a mistake* → *make a mistake* | learn chunks (Lesson 52–53) |
| **3. Grammar/structure** | Spanish word order, missing subject, wrong preposition | *Is important to say* → *It's important to say* | grammar patterns |
| **4. Pragmatics** | wrong politeness, directness, or speech act | *Give me the report* → *Could you send me the report?* | functional phrases |
| **5. Rhythm and discourse** | long sentences, no reductions, no discourse markers, flat melody | a perfect sentence read like a paragraph | Phases 6–7 |

Most "translated" feeling in advanced learners comes from **levels 2, 4 and 5**, not from grammar errors.

### 2.2 Level 1 — Word choice: false friends and Latinate words
**(a) False friends (a reminder from Phase 5)**
| Spanish | Looks like | Actually means | You want |
|---|---|---|---|
| *asistir* | assist | help | *attend* |
| *actualmente* | actually | currently | *currently / at the moment* |
| *constipado* | constipated | having a cold | *I have a cold* |
| *realizar* | realize | carry out | *carry out, complete, make* |
| *embarazada* | embarrassed | pregnant | *pregnant* |
| *eventualmente* | eventually | possibly, occasionally | *possibly / occasionally* |
| *sensible* | sensible | sensitive | *sensitive* |
| *carpeta* | carpet | folder | *folder* |
| *librería* | library | bookstore | *bookstore* |
| *éxito* | exit | success | *success* |
| *soportar* | support | bear, tolerate | *stand, put up with* |

**(b) Latinate vs. Germanic vocabulary (the biggest hidden source of "formal" Spanish-flavored English)**
Spanish speakers see cognates and reach for the **Latin-based, formal** English word. Native speakers in conversation often prefer the short **Germanic** word.

| Latinate (formal, bookish) | Everyday choice |
|---|---|
| obtain | get |
| require | need |
| commence / initiate | start / begin |
| purchase | buy |
| inquire | ask |
| assist | help |
| reside | live |
| sufficient | enough |
| attempt | try |
| demonstrate | show |
| inform | tell |
| terminate | end / stop |
| utilize | use |
| approximately | about / around |
| concerning | about |
| numerous | a lot of / many |
| however (in speech) | but |
| consequently | so |

Latinate words are fine in formal writing and academic speech (Phase 9). In conversation they sound stiff, as if you were reading from a brochure.

### 2.3 Level 2 — Collocations: the words that "live together"
This is the most important level for sounding natural. Spanish and English make different pairings.

| Translated | Natural | Why |
|---|---|---|
| do a mistake | **make** a mistake | *cometer un error* |
| make a favor | **do** a favor | |
| do a decision | **make** a decision | |
| make sport | **do** sports / **play** a sport | |
| have a doubt | **have a question** / **I'm not sure** | *tener una duda* |
| strong rain | **heavy** rain | |
| heavy wind | **strong** wind | |
| big/great problem | **big / major** problem | |
| a great quantity of | **a lot of / a large number of** | |
| realize a project | **carry out / complete** a project | |
| pass time | **spend** time | *pasar tiempo* |
| lose the bus | **miss** the bus | *perder el bus* |
| give a class / a conference | **teach** a class / **give** a talk | |
| win money | **earn / make** money | *ganar dinero* |

**How to learn collocations:** by chunk, not by word. Keep a collocation notebook and check with a collocations dictionary or a corpus (see Section 9).

### 2.4 Level 3 — Grammar and structure
Typical transfer patterns (many were covered in earlier lessons):

| Translated | Natural | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| *Is raining. / Is important.* | **It's** raining. / **It's** important. | English needs a subject (no pro-drop) |
| *Have many people here.* | **There are** many people here. | *hay* → *there is/are* |
| *I have 35 years.* | **I'm** 35. | age uses *be* |
| *I am agree.* | **I agree.** | *agree* is a verb |
| *It depends of.* | It depends **on**. | preposition |
| *I'm interested in to learn.* | I'm interested **in learning**. | gerund after preposition |
| *I want that you come.* | I want **you to come**. | no *that* + subjunctive |
| *My boss is a person very demanding.* | My boss is **very demanding**. | adjective before noun/no extra noun |
| *I go to the home.* | I'm going **home**. | no article/preposition |
| *I like very much the music.* | I like the music **very much** / I **really like** the music. | adverb placement |
| *In my opinion I think that…* | **I think** … / **In my opinion,** … | redundancy |
| *She told me that he has hunger.* | She said he's **hungry**. | *tener hambre* → *be hungry* |
| *More better* | **Better** / much better | |
| *The people that I talked* | The people **I talked to** | relative pronoun and preposition |
| *The thing that I like is…* | **What I like is…** | |

### 2.5 Level 4 — Pragmatics: directness, politeness, and speech acts
English requests, refusals, and opinions are usually **softer** than their literal Spanish equivalents.

| Situation | Translated (sounds blunt) | Natural |
|---|---|---|
| Ordering | *I want a coffee.* | **I'd like** a coffee. / **Could I have** a coffee? |
| Asking for help | *Give me a hand.* / *Help me.* | **Could you give me a hand?** |
| Requesting | *Send me the report.* | **Could you send me the report?** / **When you get a chance, could you send…** |
| Refusing | *No, I can't.* | **I'd love to, but I can't.** / **Unfortunately, I can't.** |
| Disagreeing | *You are wrong.* | **I'm not sure I agree.** / **I see it a bit differently.** |
| Asking a favor | *Can you lend me your car?* | **I was wondering if I could borrow your car.** |
| Complaining | *This is very bad.* | **I'm afraid there's a problem with…** |
| Ending emails | *Without more, I say goodbye.* | **Best regards,** / **Thanks again,** |
| Waiting for a reply | *I'm waiting for your news.* | **I look forward to hearing from you.** / **Let me know.** |
| Offering | *Do you want that I help you?* | **Do you want me to help?** / **Can I help?** |
| Thanks | *Thanks for your attention.* (after a talk) | **Thanks for listening.** |

**Directness cuts both ways.** Spanish often softens with diminutives (*un momentito*), polite imperatives, and repetition. English softens with **modal verbs, past forms, and hedges** (*could, would, I was wondering, a bit, kind of*). See Lesson 59.

Also watch **politeness inflation**: *Dear Sir/Madam, I would like to kindly request that you…* can sound old-fashioned or stiff. Match the register to the situation.

### 2.6 Level 5 — Rhythm and discourse
Some sentences sound translated because of **how they are delivered or connected**, not their words.

| Feature | Translated sound | Natural sound |
|---|---|---|
| Length | long sentences with many subordinate clauses | shorter sentences, often joined by *and, but, so* |
| Connectors | formal ones (*furthermore, moreover*) in speech | *also, plus, and another thing, on top of that* |
| Reductions | none | weak forms, contractions (Lessons 66–67) |
| Melody | flat, narrow | varied; fall-rise for "yes, but" (Lesson 68) |
| Fillers | Spanish fillers, long silences | *um, well, so* |
| Repetition | restating the same idea with synonyms | one clear statement, then an example |
| Intensifiers | *very, very, very* | *really, super, pretty, kind of, a bit* |
| Openings | *Good afternoon, I want to tell you that…* | *So, quick update…* |

### 2.7 Spanish-shaped habits that stretch across levels
- **Over-explicitness:** English tends to avoid repeating what is obvious (*I went to the store and bought milk*, not *I went to the store and I bought milk in the store*).
- **Over-use of *very*:** *very good* → *great, excellent, really good*.
- **Over-use of *make/do/have/take*:** use stronger verbs (*make an effort → try, take a look → look*).
- **Over-use of the passive** or abstract nouns: *The realization of the project was carried out* → *We finished the project.* English prefers **people doing things** (Phase 9).
- **Noun stacks in Spanish order:** *the table of the kitchen* → *the kitchen table*.
- **Possessives:** *the car of my brother* → *my brother's car*.
- **"Please" overuse:** English uses *please* but also tone and modals; *Please, could you…* is fine, but adding it to every sentence can sound stiff.
- **Saying "yes" or "no" where English uses a full phrase:** *Do you mind?* → *No, go ahead* / *Not at all.*

### 2.8 The Translation Trap revisited (Lesson 4)
In Lesson 4 you learned why direct translation fails. Here is the **upgraded method**: instead of translating the sentence, **translate the intention**.

> **Spanish thought:** *Estoy esperando tus noticias.*
> **Literal translation:** *I'm waiting for your news.*
> **Intention:** I'd like you to reply when you can.
> **English expression of the intention:** *Let me know when you hear anything.* / *I look forward to hearing from you.*

**Steps**
1. What do I want to **do** with this sentence (ask, refuse, thank, warn, complain, invite)?
2. What's the **situation** (who, how formal, written or spoken)?
3. What would a native speaker **actually say** in this situation (chunk)?
4. Say it with **natural rhythm** (Phase 7).

### 2.9 What "natural" does not mean
- It does **not** mean using slang everywhere.
- It does **not** mean losing your accent or pretending to be American/British.
- It does **not** mean never making mistakes.
- It does **not** mean ignoring your Spanish; your Spanish thinking is an asset (see 2.10).

Natural means: **the listener doesn't have to translate back in their head.**

### 2.10 What your Spanish gives you
Not everything that comes from Spanish sounds translated. Spanish gives you:
- thousands of **Latin cognates** you already understand (great for reading and formal writing);
- strong **vowel clarity** and **consonant control**;
- a good sense of **clauses and connectors** that works well in writing;
- melodic expressiveness that can add warmth in English;
- cultural and linguistic **flexibility**, a real advantage in international work.

The goal is **choice**, not erasure: you know both patterns and pick the English one when speaking English.

## 3. Why Native Speakers Do This (and why "translated" stands out)
1. **Chunks.** Native speakers store thousands of ready-made phrases. They produce *make a decision* as one unit; a learner builds it from words and may choose a different verb.
2. **Statistical expectation.** Listeners predict what comes next (Lesson 69). A rare pairing like *strong rain* is noticed, even if it is understandable.
3. **Politeness conventions are cultural.** Directness norms differ, and native listeners react to tone more than grammar.
4. **Register awareness.** Natives match vocabulary to situation without thinking. A mismatch (too formal in a café) stands out.
5. **Rhythm is learned early.** Children learn the music of the language before the grammar. A different rhythm marks an accent immediately.
6. **Efficiency.** Natural English tends to be shorter and more verb-based.
7. **Tolerance.** Native speakers usually *forgive* translated phrasing, but it can make you seem less fluent or less sure than you are. That's why fixing it helps you in interviews, negotiations, and networking.

## 4. When to Use / When Not To

**Aim for natural English when**
- you want your speaking to sound confident and fluent;
- you are in interviews, meetings, presentations, or networking;
- you write emails and messages to colleagues or clients;
- you want to be remembered as articulate.

**Do not obsess when**
- the situation is simple and you are clearly understood;
- the listener is a fellow learner (clarity matters more than idiom);
- you are tired or under pressure; use simple, safe English (Lesson 70);
- the "translated" phrase is a **legitimate, shared pattern** (e.g., in international business English, which has its own conventions).

**Caution**
- Don't try to fix everything at once. Choose **3–5 priority patterns** per month.
- Don't over-use idioms to sound native; a misplaced idiom sounds worse than plain English.
- Don't copy slang from media without understanding its register.
- Check unfamiliar phrases before using them in formal contexts.

## 5. Frequency & Priority

**What gives the biggest improvement in "naturalness" (in order)**
1. **Core collocations** with *make, do, have, take, get, give* (Phase 2).
2. **Prepositions** after common verbs and adjectives (Phase 3).
3. **Phrasal verbs** instead of formal verbs in conversation (Phase 4).
4. **Polite request and refusal frames** (*Could you…, I'd like…, I'm afraid…*).
5. **Subject always present** (*It's, There are*).
6. **Short, direct sentences** with strong verbs.
7. **Contractions and weak forms** (Phase 7).
8. **Discourse words** (*well, so, actually, anyway*).
9. **Register matching.**
10. **Idioms**, only the most common (Lesson 58).

**Time guide**
| Goal | Practice |
|---|---|
| Maintenance | 10 min/day collecting one new chunk |
| Improvement | 20 min/day: chunk notebook + recording + one corpus check |
| Intensive | add weekly feedback from a native speaker or teacher |

## 6. American vs. British Notes
| Feature | US | UK |
|---|---|---|
| Requests | *Can I get a coffee?* (casual); *I'd like…* | *Could I have a coffee, please?* / *Can I have…* |
| Politeness | friendly directness; *Sure, no problem* | more indirect; understatement; *Not bad* = good |
| Thanks/apology | *Thanks, I appreciate it.* | *Cheers, thanks a lot.* / frequent *sorry* |
| Disagreeing | *I see what you mean, but…* | *I'm not sure I'd go along with that…* / *With respect…* |
| Vocabulary | *apartment, truck, vacation, soccer* | *flat, lorry, holiday, football* |
| Collocations | *take a shower, take a vacation, do the dishes* | *have a shower, take/have a holiday, do the washing up* |
| Email closings | *Best, Thanks, Regards* | *Kind regards, Best wishes, Yours sincerely* |
| Time | *Quarter after four; I'll call you on the weekend* | *Quarter past four; at the weekend* |
| Past forms | *gotten, learned* | *got, learnt/learned* |

Both varieties favor **chunks, polite modals, and short sentences** over word-for-word structures. Choose one set and be consistent.

### 7.2 Over-correction traps
| ❌ Over-correction | Problem |
|---|---|
| Using idioms in every sentence | sounds like a textbook parody |
| Copying slang you don't fully understand | misplaced register |
| Dropping all politeness to sound "direct like Americans" | can sound rude |
| Making every sentence short and simple | sounds childish in formal contexts |
| Fixing everything and speaking slowly | loses fluency |
| Avoiding all Latinate words | formal contexts need them |

### 7.3 Attitude traps
- **"If I'm understood, that's enough."** For many goals it is. For interviews, leadership, and persuasion, naturalness matters.
- **"Native speakers never make these errors."** They make their own (*less/fewer*, *should of*), but not these.
- **"I need to stop thinking in Spanish."** A realistic goal is to *use English patterns first* when speaking English; you can still think in Spanish when you want.
- **"Natural = informal."** There is natural formal English too (Phase 9).

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ **"Correct grammar is natural English."** — Grammar can be perfect and the phrasing still foreign.
- ❌ **"Natural English means slang."** — Natural means appropriate and expected, in any register.
- ❌ **"If it's in the dictionary, I can use it."** — Dictionaries list words, not which ones go together or when to use them.
- ❌ **"Cognates are safe."** — They are useful but also the source of false friends and over-formal choices.
- ❌ **"Translation tools produce natural English."** — They often produce correct but Spanish-shaped output; check collocations and register.
- ❌ **"Natives never sound translated."** — They also borrow, calque, and make mistakes, but usually in different places.
- ❌ **"Politeness means longer sentences."** — Often it means shorter sentences with a modal (*Could you…*).
- ❌ **"I should avoid using Spanish patterns entirely."** — Some transfer is harmless; focus on the patterns that confuse or distract.
- ❌ **"Once I'm fluent, this fixes itself."** — Fluency can fossilize translated patterns; you need to notice them.
- ❌ **"Only accent matters."** — Choices of words, politeness, and structure matter as much as accent.
- ❌ **"International English has no rules."** — It has conventions; clarity and shared patterns matter.
- ❌ **"It's too late to change."** — Adults improve through chunk learning and feedback.

## 9. Pronunciation Note: Training Method

### 9.1 The Spot–Rebuild–Rehearse loop
1. **Spot.** Record yourself speaking for 2 minutes, or collect an email you wrote. Underline anything that "feels Spanish."
2. **Diagnose the level** (word, collocation, grammar, pragmatics, rhythm).
3. **Rebuild.** Find the natural version in a dictionary, corpus, or by asking a teacher/native speaker.
4. **Rehearse.** Say the natural version 5 times in a full sentence, with natural rhythm; add it to your chunk notebook.
5. **Review.** Spaced repetition (days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30).

### 9.2 Tools for checking (availability and interfaces change)
- **Collocation dictionaries** (e.g., Oxford Collocations Dictionary, Macmillan, Cambridge).
- **Learner dictionaries** with example sentences (Cambridge, Oxford Learner's, Longman, Merriam-Webster Learner's).
- **Corpora:** COCA (Corpus of Contemporary American English), BNC (British National Corpus) — see how real speakers use a phrase.
- **Quote search:** search a phrase in quotation marks in a search engine and see how often it appears on English-language sites.
- **Bilingual concordancers** (e.g., Linguee, Reverso Context): see real translated examples; be careful about quality.
- **Native-speaker feedback:** the most reliable.
- **AI tools:** useful for suggestions; verify unfamiliar phrases and ask for "how would a native speaker say this in conversation?"

### 9.3 Chunk notebook
Create a table:
| Spanish intention | Translated | Natural chunk | Example | Register |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Estoy esperando tus noticias | I'm waiting for your news | Let me know when you hear anything | "Let me know when you hear anything." | neutral |
| Perdí el bus | I lost the bus | I missed the bus | "I missed the bus again." | neutral |

### 9.4 Shadowing for chunks
Choose a series or podcast. Pick 10 natural-sounding phrases, write them down, shadow them with the same rhythm and melody. Use them in a conversation within 48 hours.

### 9.5 Reverse translation test
Take a natural English sentence and translate it literally into Spanish. If the Spanish sounds odd, the English probably follows a different pattern; mark it as a chunk to learn.

### 9.6 "How would I say this to a friend?" test
Say the sentence aloud as if to a friend in a café. If it sounds like a presentation or a letter, simplify.

### 9.7 Self-editing checklist (spoken or written)
- [ ] Is there a subject in every clause?
- [ ] Are the verbs and nouns paired naturally (collocations)?
- [ ] Are the prepositions right?
- [ ] Is the request polite enough (modal verb)?
- [ ] Am I using a formal word where a simple one works?
- [ ] Is the sentence too long?
- [ ] Did I repeat the same idea twice?
- [ ] Is the rhythm natural (stress, weak forms)?

### 9.8 Feedback routine
Once a week, send a 1-minute recording or a paragraph to a teacher or native speaker with the question: "What sounds unnatural here, and how would you say it?" Add their versions to your notebook.

### 9.9 Immersion with purpose
When watching or reading, pause on phrases that surprise you: *"I didn't know you could say it like that."* Those are your best learning items.

### 9.10 Trouble spots
- Trusting machine translation for phrasing.
- Learning new words without their partners.
- Over-formal vocabulary in speech.
- Skipping the rehearsal step.
- Not recording yourself.

## 10. Memory Technique

### 10.1 Translate the intention, not the sentence
Ask: "What do I want to do with this sentence?" Then find the English chunk for that action.

### 10.2 Chunk, not word
Store *make a decision, miss the bus, do me a favor, I'd like…* as single items.

### 10.3 The two-pattern table
For every Spanish habit, keep two columns: **Spanish pattern → English pattern**. Example: *Tengo 35 años → I'm 35.*

### 10.4 The café test
If you wouldn't say it in a café to a new friend, ask whether it's too formal.

### 10.5 The "would a native say it?" search
Put a phrase in quotes. Few results or only from non-native sites is a red flag.

### 10.6 Top ten list
Keep your personal "top ten translated habits" on a card and review it before speaking events. Replace items as you master them.

### 10.7 Spaced retrieval cards
- *Five levels of "translated"?* → **word, collocation, grammar, pragmatics, rhythm/discourse**
- *Which level matters most for advanced learners?* → **collocation, pragmatics, rhythm**
- *Spanish "tengo 35 años"?* → **I'm 35.**
- *Spanish "perder el bus"?* → **miss the bus**
- *Polite request frame?* → **Could you…? / I was wondering if…**
- *Latinate vs. everyday: "obtain"?* → **get**
- *Method for fixing a translated sentence?* → **translate the intention; find the chunk**
- *Where to check phrases?* → **collocation dictionaries, corpora (COCA/BNC), native feedback**
Review on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30.

### 10.8 Monthly focus
Pick 3 patterns each month (e.g., make/do collocations; polite requests; *It's/There are*). Track them in your notebook and recordings. At the end of the month, check which have become automatic.

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1: Translate the intention, then choose the English chunk.**

> **Pattern 2: Check at five levels: word, collocation, grammar, pragmatics, rhythm.**

> **Pattern 3: English needs a subject (*It's…*, *There are…*).**

> **Pattern 4: Common verbs pair with fixed nouns (*make a decision, do a favor, miss a bus*).**

> **Pattern 5: Requests and refusals soften through modals and hedges, not through extra words.**

> **Pattern 6: In conversation, prefer short Germanic words over long Latinate ones.**

> **Pattern 7: Short sentences, strong verbs, people as subjects.**

> **Pattern 8: Match register to situation.**

> **Pattern 9: Cognates are helpful for reading and dangerous for speaking choices; check them.**

> **Pattern 10: Rhythm and melody can make correct sentences sound foreign (Phase 7).**

> **Pattern 11: Collect and rehearse chunks, don't just correct errors.**

> **Pattern 12: Seek feedback from real speakers.**

## 12. Real-Life Examples

### 12.1 Same message, four levels of naturalness
> **Intention:** Ask a colleague to review your report.
> 1. ❌ *Please review my report and give me your opinion.* (correct, but blunt and a little formal)
> 2. 🟡 *Could you review my report and tell me what you think?* (polite, fine)
> 3. ✅ *Do you have a few minutes to look over my report? I'd love to hear what you think.* (natural, warm)
> 4. ✅ *When you get a chance, could you take a look at my report? Any feedback would be great.* (natural, workplace)

### 12.2 Before and after: speaking
| Translated | Natural |
|---|---|
| "I have a doubt about the project." | "I have a question about the project." |

## 13. Collocations & Related Expressions

**Vocabulary**
- *natural, fluent, idiomatic, authentic, native-like, colloquial, stilted, formal, wooden, awkward, clumsy, foreign-sounding, translated, literal, word-for-word*
- *a calque, a false friend, a collocation, a chunk, a set phrase, a fixed expression, an idiom, a register, a speech act*
- *sound natural / sound like a native / sound stiff / sound off / sound translated / come across as (formal, blunt, rude, unsure)*
- *get the wording right, find the right phrase, put it more naturally, say it more idiomatically, phrase it differently, reword, polish*
- *interference (from your first language), transfer, fossilize, error pattern*
- *native-like, near-native, advanced, proficient, fluent*

**Useful expressions**
- *How would you say that?*
- *Is that how you'd say it?*
- *Does that sound natural?*
- *Is there a more natural way to put it?*
- *What would a native speaker say here?*
- *That sounds a bit odd. You'd normally say…*
- *We'd usually say…*
- *Is that a bit formal?*
- *Is that too direct?*

**Contrast groups**
| Items | Difference |
|---|---|
| *natural / idiomatic / colloquial* | sounds right / uses native-like expressions / informal spoken |
| *correct / natural* | grammatically right / what speakers actually say |
| *formal / stilted* | appropriate for serious contexts / stiff and unnatural |
| *literal / word-for-word* | following the meaning of each word / copying each word |
| *calque / false friend* | copied phrase structure / similar-looking word with a different meaning |
| *come across as / sound* | give an impression / how it is heard |

## 17. Speaking Practice

**Drill 1 — Intention to chunk.** Take 10 Spanish intentions (ask a favor, refuse, apologize, disagree, thank, complain, invite, postpone, clarify, close a call). Say the natural English chunk for each.

**Drill 2 — Polite request ladder.** Make the same request five ways, from casual to formal: *Pass the salt. → Could you pass the salt? → Would you mind passing the salt? → I was wondering if you could pass the salt.*

**Drill 3 — Latinate swap.** Take 10 formal sentences and say them in everyday English.

**Drill 4 — Collocation bursts.** Say 5 sentences for each verb: *make, do, take, get, miss, spend, catch*.

**Drill 5 — Mirror a native.** Shadow a 1-minute clip and write down three natural phrases you want to use. Use them in conversation.

**Drill 6 — Role-play.** Do an interview, a café order, a complaint, and a polite refusal with a partner. Ask them to stop you whenever something sounds translated.

**Drill 7 — Rewrite aloud.** Read the "translated" sentences in Section 7.1, then say the natural version without looking.

**Drill 8 — Monthly recording.** Record the same 2-minute topic every month. Compare the number of translated habits.
