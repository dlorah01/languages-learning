# Lesson 11 — Mood: Indicative, Subjunctive, Imperative — and the English Subjunctive Spanish Speakers Miss
### Phase 1: Grammar | Block A (Tense, Aspect, Mood, Voice) | Lesson 6 of 7

---

## 0. Where this fits
Lessons 6–10 covered tense and aspect (when + how an action is framed). This lesson covers **mood** — a different grammatical dimension entirely: the speaker's *attitude* toward the reality of what they're saying (stating a fact, commanding, or expressing something hypothetical/non-factual). Spanish speakers often assume English "doesn't really have" a subjunctive because it doesn't look like Spanish's obvious subjunctive endings — this lesson shows you it exists, just camouflaged.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation

Mood answers the question: **"Is the speaker presenting this as real, as a command, or as unreal/hypothetical/wished-for?"**

- **Indicative** — the default mood, for stating facts and real situations: "She works here." "It rained yesterday." (You've been using this the entire course so far.)
- **Imperative** — for commands/requests, using the bare base verb: "Close the door." "Please sit down."
- **Subjunctive** — for situations that are hypothetical, wished-for, demanded, or contrary to fact — grammatically marked in special, limited ways in English.

The key surprise for you: **English's subjunctive is real, but it's invisible in most cases** because it uses the *base form* of the verb, which often looks identical to other forms — so you might be using it correctly by accident, or missing it and defaulting to indicative without realizing there's a "should have been" subjunctive slot.

---

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2a. Indicative — no new material needed
Everything in Lessons 6–10 was indicative mood. No special marking; this is your baseline.

### 2b. Imperative
**Form:** base verb, no subject (subject "you" is implied/omitted)
- "Close the door." / "Don't touch that." / "Let's go." (first-person plural imperative, via "let's")
- Politeness is added via other means, not verb form: "Could you close the door?" / "Please close the door." (imperative softened by "please" or converted into a question — we'll cover politeness strategies fully in Phase 6.)

### 2c. Subjunctive — the part that's usually invisible to learners
English's subjunctive survives in a few specific, limited constructions. It doesn't have unique verb endings the way Spanish does (*hable, hables, hable...*) — instead, it uses the **bare base form of the verb**, which is only visually distinct from indicative in third-person singular (where indicative would add -s) and with the verb "be."

**i) Mandative subjunctive** — after verbs/expressions of demand, suggestion, recommendation, insistence:
- Trigger verbs: suggest, recommend, insist, demand, request, propose, require, ask (in this sense), and expressions like "it's important that," "it's essential that," "it's necessary that."
- Pattern: [trigger] + that + subject + **base verb** (no -s, even for third person singular; no tense change)
- "I suggest that she **call** him." (NOT "calls") — this is the invisible part: it looks like a mistake to learners because the -s is missing, but it's actually the subjunctive, correctly formed.
- "It's essential that he **be** on time." (NOT "is") — "be" is the clearest, most visible subjunctive marker in English, because "be" has a distinct base form unlike other verbs.
- "The committee recommended that the policy **be** reviewed." (passive subjunctive — still uses base "be")

**ii) "Were" subjunctive** — in hypothetical/unreal statements, especially with "if" and "wish":
- "If I **were** you, I would apologize." (NOT "was," even though "I" would normally take "was" in indicative — this is the subjunctive "were," used for ALL persons in hypothetical conditionals, singular or plural)
- "I wish I **were** taller." (NOT "was" — though "was" is increasingly heard informally; "were" remains the standard/preferred form, especially in formal English)
- This is directly relevant to Lesson 13 (Conditionals) — the "were" subjunctive is exactly what appears in second conditional "if" clauses, and we'll connect back to this lesson explicitly there.

**iii) Fixed/formulaic subjunctive expressions** (fossilized, no longer productive, but common):
- "God bless you." (not "blesses") / "Long live the king." (not "lives") / "So be it." / "If need be." / "Come what may."
- These are frozen historical remnants of a once much more active English subjunctive system (recall Lesson 1: English used to be more synthetic/inflected and has been steadily losing grammatical marking for centuries — the subjunctive's near-disappearance is part of that same long historical trend).

---

## 3. Why Native Speakers Use This (Even Without Realizing It)
Most native speakers can't explain "the subjunctive" as a grammar term, but they produce it correctly by pattern-matching from exposure — precisely the chunking mechanism from Lesson 3. "I suggest that he call" simply *sounds right*, and "I suggest that he calls" sounds subtly off to a native ear, even if they can't say why. Your goal is the same: internalize the pattern well enough that it becomes intuitive, not something you consciously calculate each time.

---

## 4. When This Matters Most
- **Mandative subjunctive** matters most in **formal/professional writing and speech** — emails, reports, meetings, academic writing (recommendations, proposals, policy language). It's somewhat less strictly enforced in casual speech, where many native speakers (especially in less formal contexts) drift toward indicative ("I suggest that he calls him") without it being considered a serious error — but the subjunctive remains the "correct," more polished, professionally expected form.
- **"Were" subjunctive** matters in any hypothetical statement — very common across all registers, especially "if I were you" (an extremely frequent chunk worth memorizing directly as a fixed expression, per Lesson 3's method).
- **When it matters less:** the fixed/formulaic expressions (God bless you, long live...) are just vocabulary to recognize, not a productive pattern you need to generate yourself.

---

## 5. Frequency & Register
Mandative subjunctive is notably more common and more strictly followed in American English than in British English (British English often prefers "should" + base verb instead: "I suggest that he should call him" — a modal workaround that achieves a similar meaning without needing the bare subjunctive form). This is a genuine, well-documented US/UK difference — flagged fully in Section 6 below. "Were" subjunctive is common in both varieties, though "was" is creeping into very casual speech in both, without full acceptance in formal use yet.

---

## 6. American vs. British Note
| Context | American English | British English |
|---|---|---|
| Mandative subjunctive | "I suggest that he call her." (bare subjunctive strongly preferred) | "I suggest that he should call her." (modal "should" often preferred instead) OR "I suggest that he calls her." (indicative, informally accepted) |
| "Were" subjunctive | "If I were you..." (standard in both) | "If I were you..." (standard in both) |

For a learner without a strong personal preference yet, **default to American mandative subjunctive** (bare base verb after suggest/recommend/insist/etc.) since it's cleaner, more universally understood, and considered correct in both varieties — while the British "should" alternative is also perfectly acceptable if you prefer that pattern.

---

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

| Mistake | Why | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "I suggest that he calls her." | Defaulting to indicative because the subjunctive is invisible/unfamiliar in English (unlike Spanish's clearly marked subjunctive) | "I suggest that he call her." (mandative subjunctive, bare base form) |
| "If I was you, I would..." | Applying regular past-tense "was" instead of recognizing the special hypothetical "were" | "If I were you, I would..." |
| Missing subjunctive entirely and using an infinitive/gerund construction that sounds foreign | Trying to avoid the unfamiliar structure altogether | Learn the specific trigger-verb list (suggest, recommend, insist, demand, require, propose + "it's important/essential/necessary that") as fixed patterns (per Lesson 3's chunking method), rather than trying to derive them from Spanish subjunctive logic, which doesn't map 1:1 |
| Assuming English subjunctive works like Spanish subjunctive (wide range of emotional/doubt triggers: *espero que, dudo que, me alegra que...*) | Spanish subjunctive is far more productive/widely triggered than English's | English mostly uses modals (might, could, may) or infinitives instead of subjunctive for emotion/doubt: *"Espero que venga"* → "I hope he comes" (indicative, NOT subjunctive — a very common Spanish-speaker overcorrection error, trying to force an English subjunctive where none exists) |

---

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ "English doesn't have a subjunctive." — It does, just in a narrower range of contexts and with less visible marking than Spanish.
- ❌ "Since English's subjunctive looks like the base form, I can just always use base forms after 'that' clauses." — Only the specific triggers listed above (demand/suggestion/necessity verbs and expressions) call for subjunctive; ordinary factual "that" clauses use normal indicative: "I know that she works here" (NOT "that she work here" — no subjunctive trigger here, "know" isn't a mandative verb).
- ❌ "Hope" triggers subjunctive like Spanish *esperar que*. — It doesn't; "I hope that she comes" uses ordinary indicative in English, a very common overcorrection trap flagged in Section 7.

---

## 9. Pronunciation Note
Because mandative subjunctive removes the third-person -s, the verb's ending sound changes noticeably: "he calls" (/kɔːlz/, indicative) vs. "he call" (/kɔːl/, subjunctive) — worth listening for specifically in formal contexts (news, professional meetings) to train your ear to notice this small but meaningful difference.

---

## 10. Memory Technique
**The trigger-verb list as a single memorized chunk group:** suggest / recommend / insist / demand / request / propose / require + "it's important/essential/necessary/vital that." Memorize these together as one functional category ("subjunctive triggers"), the same way you'll later memorize phrasal verb groups (Phase 4) — as a set, not individually.

For "were": **"If I were you" is a fixed, extremely frequent chunk** (Lesson 3's method) — drill this exact phrase until it's fully automatic, and the "were" pattern will generalize naturally to other hypothetical subjects.

---

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern:** [demand/suggestion/necessity trigger] + that + subject + **base verb** (never -s, never past tense, regardless of the main clause's own tense): "She insisted that he **leave**." / "She insists that he **leave**." / "She will insist that he **leave**." — the subjunctive verb form stays constant no matter what tense the main clause is in, because subjunctive is a *mood*, not a *tense*.

> **Pattern:** hypothetical/unreal "if" + were (any subject) — this pattern connects directly to Lesson 13's second conditional, where it will reappear as the standard form for unreal present/future hypotheticals.

---

## 12. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1:**
- "The doctor recommended that she **rest** for a week." (not "rests")
- "If I **were** rich, I would travel more." (not "was")

**Level 2 — subjunctive tense-invariance:**
- "It was essential that he **be** present." (past main clause, still base subjunctive "be," not "was")
- "It is essential that he **be** present." (present main clause, same subjunctive form — proving mood ≠ tense)

**Level 3 — subjunctive vs. false-friend "hope" trap:**
- ❌ "I hope that she **be** happy." → ✅ "I hope that she **is** happy." (ordinary indicative — hope isn't a subjunctive trigger in English)

**Level 4 — formal/professional register subjunctive:**
- "The board requires that all members **submit** their reports by Friday, and it is recommended that each report **be** reviewed twice before submission." (two subjunctive triggers in one sentence: "requires that...submit" and "recommended that...be")

---

## 13. Related Expressions / Terms
- **Mandative subjunctive** — this lesson's main new grammatical term (demand/suggestion-triggered subjunctive).
- **"Were" subjunctive / irrealis were** — the hypothetical-conditional "were" form.
- **Mood** (broader category) vs. **tense** (Lesson 6) vs. **aspect** (Lessons 6–10) — three genuinely separate grammatical dimensions, often confused by learners as "all just verb stuff."
