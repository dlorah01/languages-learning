# Lesson 70 — Real-Time Speaking Strategies
### Phase 8: Listening & Speaking Fluency | Module 2 of 3

---

## 0. Where this fits
Lesson 69 trained your ears. This lesson trains the other half of live conversation: **what you do in the two seconds after someone asks you something and your mind goes blank.**

Every speaker, in every language, runs out of words, loses the thread, or says the wrong thing in real time. The difference between a confident speaker and a hesitant one is rarely perfect knowledge. It is a **toolkit for recovery**: buying time, correcting yourself, saying it another way, and keeping the conversation moving.

Connections:
- **Lesson 60:** fillers and discourse words (*well, so, you know, I mean*) are the raw material for buying time.
- **Lesson 59:** hedging and softening phrases double as stalling phrases.
- **Lesson 63:** keeping the floor and polite interruption.
- **Lessons 65–68:** thought groups, rhythm, and melody make pauses sound natural instead of broken.
- **Lesson 69:** repair phrases on the listener side; here we build the speaker side.
- **Lesson 71 (next):** sounding natural vs. translated, which depends on the habits you build now.

> **Core idea:** Fluency is not speed and not perfection. It is **keeping the conversation moving with the language you have**, using planned chunks, natural stalling, and graceful repair.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
Think of a **driver on an unfamiliar road**. A nervous driver stops in the middle of the road at every doubt, blocking everyone. A skilled driver slows down, signals, takes a slightly different route, and arrives anyway.

In conversation:
- **Stopping dead** (silence, "ehh...", starting over, mumbling) blocks the other person.
- **Signaling** ("Let me think...", "How can I put this?") tells them you're still going.
- **Taking another route** (paraphrase: "the thing you use to...") gets you to the same meaning with different words.
- **Correcting course** ("I mean...", "or rather...") fixes a wrong turn without losing the passenger.

A second image: **a juggler who keeps one ball in the air while preparing the next.** Fluent speakers plan the **next chunk** while they say the **current one**. You can build that habit with ready-made chunks.

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 What happens when you speak in real time
Speech production (in simplified form) involves:
1. **Deciding what to say** (message planning).
2. **Finding words and grammar** (formulation).
3. **Producing sounds** (articulation).
4. **Monitoring** what you said and fixing it.

All four run in parallel and under time pressure. In your first language, steps 2 and 3 are nearly automatic, so you can focus on 1 and 4. In a second language, step 2 uses more attention, and it competes with the others. That is why even advanced speakers hesitate: not because they don't know English, but because **formulation is still expensive**.

**Implications**
- The more you **automate** (chunks, routines, rehearsed openings), the more attention you free.
- **Shorter units** reduce the planning load.
- **Stalling devices** give formulation time without breaking the interaction.
- **Repair strategies** keep errors from derailing you.

### 2.2 The three skills in this lesson
| Skill | Question it answers | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Buying time** | "I need a moment." | *Let me think...*, *That's a good question.*, *Well, ...* |
| **Self-correction** | "I said it wrong." | *I mean...*, *or rather...*, *sorry, let me rephrase.* |
| **Paraphrasing / circumlocution** | "I don't have the word." | *the thing you use to...*, *it's like a...*, *a kind of...* |

Plus a fourth, structural skill: **organizing an answer quickly** (Section 2.9).

### 2.3 Buying time: natural stalling devices
Silence of 1–2 seconds is normal in English conversation. Longer silences feel awkward, and you can fill them with sounds or phrases that signal "I'm thinking, don't interrupt."

**(a) Filled pauses (sounds)**
- *um, uh, er* (British often *er*; American *uh/um*): neutral, universal.
- *hmm*: thoughtful.
- Stretching a word: *sooo...*, *wellll...*

Compared with Spanish *eh... / este... / pues...*, English *um / uh* are the neutral choice. Using Spanish fillers in English is a very visible transfer, so replace them.

**(b) Discourse markers**
- *Well, ...* (introduces a considered answer, often with slight disagreement)
- *So, ...* (organizes, starts an explanation)
- *Okay, so...* (resets, starts)
- *Right, ...* (UK/US, shows you're processing)
- *Actually, ...* (introduces a correction or surprise)
- *Basically, ...* (promises a simple version)

**(c) Stalling phrases**
| Phrase | Use | Register |
|---|---|---|
| *Let me think...* | needs a moment | neutral |
| *Let me see...* | quick check | neutral |
| *Give me a second.* | longer than a moment | neutral |
| *That's a good question.* | buys 2–3 seconds | neutral/professional |
| *That's an interesting point.* | buys time, shows engagement | professional |
| *How can I put this?* | searching for the right way to say it | neutral |
| *What's the best way to say this?* | same | neutral |
| *It's hard to say...* | uncertain | neutral |
| *If I remember correctly...* | recalling | neutral |
| *As far as I know...* | hedging while thinking | neutral |
| *Let me get back to you on that.* | postpones | professional |
| *I'd have to check.* | postpones | professional |
| *How shall I put it?* | formal/British | formal |
| *Where do I start?* | complex answer coming | neutral |

**(d) Repeating or rephrasing the question**
- *So, what do I think about remote work... Well, ...*
- *You're asking why I changed jobs? Well, ...*

This is natural and also confirms you understood the question.

**(e) Short, safe openers** (answer starters that need no planning)
- *To be honest, ...*
- *The way I see it, ...*
- *From my experience, ...*
- *It depends on...*
- *I guess it's partly...*

**Limits.** Use stalling devices **moderately**. One per answer sounds natural; three in a row sounds nervous. Avoid over-using *like* as a filler (Lesson 60), and avoid long strings of *um*.

### 2.4 Self-correction
Self-correction is normal in native speech. Natives restart, rephrase, and change direction all the time, often without noticing. The goal is not to avoid errors; it is to **correct without drama**.

**Signals of correction**
| Phrase | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *I mean...* | restate more precisely | *It's on Monday, I mean Tuesday.* |
| *or rather...* | refine | *He's a friend, or rather, an acquaintance.* |
| *sorry, ...* | quick fix | *We met in 2019, sorry, 2018.* |
| *actually, ...* | correct a detail | *It's three, actually, four people.* |
| *let me rephrase that.* | restart a whole idea | *Let me rephrase that: ...* |
| *what I meant was...* | clarify | *What I meant was that it's possible, not certain.* |
| *no, wait...* | spot an error mid-sentence | *We'll leave at six — no, wait, seven.* |
| *scratch that.* | informal cancel | *Scratch that, let's do it tomorrow.* |
| *to put it another way...* | rephrase for clarity | |
| *that came out wrong.* | repair an unintended meaning | |

**How to do it well**
1. **Stop at the error**, not at the start of the sentence.
2. **Signal** with a short marker (*I mean, sorry*).
3. **Replace only what's wrong**, then continue.
4. **Don't apologize at length.** One word is enough.
5. **Keep the same tune and rhythm.** A calm voice makes it sound natural.

**Don't**: restart the whole sentence over and over, comment on your mistake ("my English is terrible"), or fall silent after correcting.

### 2.5 Paraphrasing and circumlocution
When you can't find a word, **say what the thing is or does**. This is called circumlocution, and native speakers use it constantly too (*the thing, whatchamacallit*).

**Techniques**
| Technique | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Function** | *It's the thing you use to ___.* | *the thing you use to open bottles* (bottle opener) |
| **Category + feature** | *It's a kind of ___ that ___.* | *a kind of bag that you wear on your back* (backpack) |
| **Comparison** | *It's like a ___ but ___.* | *like a bus but smaller* (minibus/van) |
| **Opposite** | *It's the opposite of ___.* | *the opposite of cheap* (expensive) |
| **Example** | *For example, ___.* | *things like pasta or rice* (carbohydrates) |
| **Description** | *It's ___-shaped / made of ___.* | *a round metal thing you put on the stove* (pot/pan) |
| **General word** | *thing, stuff, place, person, someone* | *the stuff you put on your skin* (lotion) |
| **Approximation** | *a kind of, sort of, something like* | *sort of a small sofa* (loveseat) |
| **Ask for help** | *What's the word for ___?* | *What's the word for a person who repairs shoes?* |
| **Mime and show** | gesture, point, draw | in person/video |
| **Use a related word** | *worker* for *employee* | acceptable temporary substitute |

**Useful frames**
- *I can't think of the word. It's the thing that...*
- *What do you call it when ___?*
- *What's the word for ___?*
- *Is there a word for ___?*
- *It's on the tip of my tongue.*
- *I'm blanking on the word.*
- *You know, the ___ thing.*

(Note: "How do you say *colador* in English?" is also perfectly good when you want a specific translation. Circumlocution is for when you need to keep going without leaving the conversation.)

### 2.6 Keeping it simple: say less, say it clearly
When under pressure, **shorten and simplify**:
- Break a long sentence into two shorter ones.
- Use a common verb with a clear structure instead of a rare one.
- Say the main point first.
- Avoid grammar you aren't sure of (e.g., complex conditionals) if a simpler structure works.

| Complicated attempt | Simpler and clearer |
|---|---|
| If I would have known that the meeting was moved, I would have arrived to the new time. | I didn't know they moved the meeting. I would have come at the new time. |
| The thing that happened is that the client, who I told you about, he didn't want... | The client didn't want to continue. I told you about him last week. |

Simplicity is not weakness. Native speakers use short sentences in conversation.

### 2.7 Chunks and routines: your automatic speech
Fluent speech relies on **ready-made sequences**. Collect and practice chunks for common situations so you don't build them from scratch:

| Situation | Chunks |
|---|---|
| Starting an answer | *Well, I'd say...*, *The main thing is...*, *To be honest...* |
| Giving an opinion | *I think...*, *In my opinion...*, *As I see it...* |
| Adding | *Also,...*, *On top of that,...*, *Another thing is...* |
| Contrasting | *On the other hand,...*, *That said,...*, *Having said that,...* |
| Giving an example | *For instance,...*, *Take ___, for example.* |
| Clarifying | *What I mean is...*, *In other words...* |
| Concluding | *So, overall,...*, *All in all,...*, *The bottom line is...* |
| Checking | *Does that make sense?*, *Do you see what I mean?*, *Is that clear?* |

### 2.8 Planning while speaking: the "chunk and plan ahead" habit
While you say chunk 1, **prepare chunk 2**.
- Speak in **thought groups** (Lesson 65), 4–7 words each.
- At the end of a group, you may pause briefly. That pause is natural.
- Use the pause to choose the next group, not to rebuild the whole sentence.
- If you don't know how a sentence will end, **start with the part you know** and adjust: *I think the main problem is... well, it's the timing, really.*

### 2.9 Organizing an answer quickly
Three simple frameworks help you answer anything in 20–60 seconds.

**A–B–C (Answer, Because, Case)**
> **Answer:** *I prefer working from home.*
> **Because:** *It saves me two hours of commuting.*
> **Case (example):** *For instance, last week I finished a report a day early.*

**PREP (Point, Reason, Example, Point)**
> *Point:* I think remote work is here to stay. *Reason:* Companies save on office costs. *Example:* My own company closed a floor last year. *Point:* So I expect it to continue.

**Past – Present – Future**
> *I started in sales (past). Now I manage a team (present). I'd like to move into strategy (future).*

**Other quick structures:** problem–solution; two sides and a choice (*On one hand..., on the other hand..., I'd go for...*); first/second/finally.

**Rule:** put the answer first, then support. Don't start with a long background.

### 2.10 Handling mistakes: accuracy vs. fluency
- In conversation, **meaning and flow come first**. A small grammar slip rarely blocks understanding.
- Correct yourself when the mistake changes the meaning (numbers, dates, negatives, tense that confuses time) or when you notice it immediately.
- Do not correct trivial slips (a missing article) if it would break your flow.
- In formal speech (presentations, interviews), a bit more care is appropriate, but the same recovery techniques apply.
- Record yourself and fix **patterns** (Lessons 6–28) in study time, not in the middle of a conversation.

### 2.11 Managing silence and pace
- A 1–2 second pause is fine; a 4–5 second silence feels long.
- A slow, steady pace with clear chunks sounds more confident than rapid speech with restarts.
- **Breathe** at the thought-group boundaries.
- Lower your pitch slightly and slow down when nervous. It sounds calmer.
- Eye contact and a small nod keep the connection during a pause.

### 2.12 Dealing with nerves
- Prepare **openings** and **closings** for common situations (introductions, small talk, meetings).
- Have **3–5 stories** ready (a project you led, a problem you solved, a trip, your hometown, a recent change). You can adapt them to many questions.
- Practice **out loud**. Thinking in English is not the same as speaking it.
- Accept imperfection: aim for "good enough to be understood and keep going."
- Use deliberate breathing before important calls.

## 3. Why Native Speakers Do This
1. **Everyone has the same limits.** Natives also search for words, restart, and fill silences. The devices in this lesson come from them.
2. **Cooperation.** Stalling signals "I'm still here and have more to say," protecting the turn (Lesson 63).
3. **Politeness.** *That's a good question* and *How can I put this?* soften difficult answers.
4. **Efficiency.** *The thing, stuff, whatchamacallit* let speakers move on rather than stop.
5. **Social tolerance.** Natives expect imperfection in speech and don't judge it as they judge writing.
6. **Planning.** Pauses and fillers give the brain time to plan the next chunk; they are part of normal fluency.
7. **Face-saving.** Quick corrections (*I mean, sorry*) keep the conversation friendly and show care for accuracy.

## 4. When to Use / When Not To

**Use these strategies when**
- you're asked a question you need to think about;
- you've lost a word or structure;
- you realize you said something wrong;
- you need to organize a long answer (interview, meeting, presentation Q&A);
- you want to keep the floor while thinking.

**Choose by register**
| Situation | Preferred devices |
|---|---|
| Casual | *um, well, like (sparingly), you know, hang on, give me a sec* |
| Workplace | *Let me think, That's a good question, I'd have to check, Let me get back to you* |
| Interview | *That's a great question, Let me gather my thoughts, If I had to pick one...* |
| Presentation Q&A | *Thanks for asking, That's an important point, Let me make sure I understand...* |
| Phone call | *One moment please, Let me check on that, Bear with me* |

**Don't**
- fill every pause with sounds;
- say "I don't know how to say it" and stop;
- apologize repeatedly;
- switch to Spanish unless the listener also speaks it and you both agree;
- over-rely on *like*, *you know*, and *basically*;
- leave a correction half-finished;
- memorize rigid scripts you can't adapt.

## 5. Frequency & Priority

**Most valuable skills (in order)**
1. **Answer-first structures** (A–B–C, PREP).
2. **Five to eight stalling phrases** you can say without thinking.
3. **Circumlocution frames** (*the thing you use to..., it's like a...*).
4. **Quick self-correction phrases** (*I mean, sorry, or rather*).
5. **Chunks** for transitions and opinions.
6. **Prepared stories** for common topics.
7. **Rhythm and pacing** (breathing, thought groups).
8. **Asking for the word** (*What's the word for...?*).

**Frequency in native speech** (descriptive)
| Device | Frequency | Register |
|---|---|---|
| *um / uh / well / so / you know* | very high | all |
| *I mean* | high | informal–neutral |
| *sort of / kind of* | high | informal–neutral |
| *the thing / stuff* | high | informal |
| *Let me think / Let me see* | common | neutral |
| *That's a good question* | common | neutral/professional |
| *or rather* | moderate | neutral/formal |
| *scratch that* | moderate | informal |

## 6. American vs. British Notes
| Feature | US | UK |
|---|---|---|
| Filled pauses | *um, uh* | *um, er* |
| Stalling | *Let me think, Gimme a sec, Hang on* | *Let me think, Hang on, Bear with me, Just a moment* |
| Correction | *I mean, sorry, rather* (less common) | *I mean, sorry, or rather* (more common) |
| Unknown word | *the thing, whatchamacallit, thingamajig* | *thingy, whatsit, whatchamacallit, thingamabob* |
| Fillers | *like, you know, I mean* | *like, you know, sort of, kind of* |
| Postponing | *I'll get back to you* | *I'll come back to you, I'll revert* (business, mainly in India/UK) |
| Politeness stalling | *That's a great question* | *That's an interesting question* |

*Tip*: *revert* meaning "reply" is common in some business Englishes (including Indian and some UK usage) but sounds odd in American English, where "revert" means "return to a previous state."

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

### 7.1 Strategy and habit
| ❌ Habit | Effect | ✅ Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Long silence while translating | breaks the flow | *Let me think...* then speak |
| Spanish fillers (*eh, este, pues, o sea*) in English | sounds foreign, distracting | *um, well, I mean* |
| Restarting the whole sentence | wasteful, anxious | correct only the error, then continue |
| Apologizing repeatedly (*sorry, my English is bad*) | draws attention to errors | one quick correction, move on |
| Saying "I don't know how to say it" and stopping | stops conversation | paraphrase |
| Starting with a long background before the point | listener loses interest | answer first |
| Over-explaining with complicated grammar | more errors | shorter, simpler sentences |
| Translating idioms | confusing | use a simple equivalent |
| Using "How do you say...?" for every missing word | interrupts flow | try circumlocution first, then ask |
| Speaking too fast when nervous | unclear | slower, with pauses |
| Staying too quiet to avoid mistakes | no practice | speak and accept errors |
| Memorized scripts | stiff, breaks when the question changes | memorize chunks, not paragraphs |
| Using the same filler every time (*well* x5) | sounds nervous | vary or use silence |

### 7.2 Frequent errors in this area
| ❌ | ✅ | Note |
|---|---|---|
| I don't find the word. | I can't **find / think of** the word. | *No encuentro la palabra* transfer |
| How can I say this? (when you mean you're searching for tact) | **How can I put this?** / **How should I put this?** | |
| I forgot the word. | I **can't remember** the word. / It's slipped my mind. | *forgot* needs a past event |
| What means ___? | What does ___ mean? | word order |
| How do you call it? | **What do you call it?** | |
| Wait, wait, wait. | **Hold on.** / **Give me a second.** | register |
| Let me to think. | Let me think. | no *to* after *let* |
| Sorry for my bad English. | (usually unnecessary) | focus on content |
| How is it called in English? | What's it called? / What's the word for it? | |
| I want to say that... (as a stall every time) | I mean... / What I'm trying to say is... | variety |
| Please wait (to a person) | One moment, please. / Bear with me. | softer |
| I am blocked. | I'm **blanking**. / My mind's gone blank. | |

### 7.3 Attitude traps
- **"I must speak perfectly or not at all."** Fluency comes from speaking imperfectly and repairing.
- **"Pauses mean I'm weak."** Native speakers pause constantly.
- **"Paraphrasing is cheating."** It's a core communication skill.
- **"If I ask for a word, people will think less of me."** Most people are happy to help and will respect the effort.
- **"I'll learn to speak after I know everything."** You never will; the strategies come first.

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ **"Fluent speakers never hesitate."** — They hesitate often; they hesitate smoothly.
- ❌ **"Fillers are always bad."** — Used moderately, they are natural and useful.
- ❌ **"Self-correction means my English is poor."** — Natives self-correct constantly.
- ❌ **"I should speak slowly to avoid errors."** — A steady, natural pace with chunks works better.
- ❌ **"Paraphrasing is for beginners."** — Advanced speakers use it for precision and style.
- ❌ **"Thing and stuff are bad words."** — They are standard in informal English when used appropriately.
- ❌ **"Stalling sounds unprofessional."** — *That's a good question* is standard in meetings.
- ❌ **"A long silence is more polite than a filler."** — In English, a long silence can feel awkward; a quick filler signals engagement.
- ❌ **"I should memorize answers."** — Memorize flexible chunks and a few stories.
- ❌ **"If I make a mistake, the conversation is ruined."** — Almost never.
- ❌ **"Switching to Spanish for one word is a failure."** — It's a last resort, but sometimes fine in the right setting; it's better to try circumlocution first.
- ❌ **"Native speakers always know the word."** — They say *What's the word?* all the time.

## 9. Pronunciation Note: Training Method

### 9.1 The 4/3/2 fluency routine
(Known in language teaching as the 4/3/2 technique.)
1. Choose a topic you know (your job, a trip, your city).
2. Speak for **4 minutes** to a partner (or record yourself) without stopping.
3. Repeat the same talk in **3 minutes**.
4. Repeat again in **2 minutes**.
Each time you compress, you automate chunks and reduce hesitation. If you are alone, use a timer and record.

### 9.2 One-minute drills
Pick a random topic (a place, a person, a decision, a problem). Speak for one minute, no stopping. If you get stuck, use a stalling phrase or circumlocution. Record, listen, and note the places you hesitated.

### 9.3 Circumlocution game
Describe an object without naming it (or the first letter): *a colander, a stapler, a zipper, a charger, a screwdriver, a hanger*. A partner guesses. Swap.

### 9.4 Stalling-phrase warm-up
Practice five stalling phrases with a natural, falling-rising melody (Lesson 68): *Let me think ↘↗... / That's a good question ↘... / Well ↘↗... / Give me a second ↘ / How can I put this ↘?*

### 9.5 Self-correction drill
Say a sentence with a deliberate error and correct it smoothly:
- *We'll meet on Monday — I mean, Tuesday.*
- *It costs fifty — sorry, fifteen dollars.*
- *She's my colleague — or rather, my manager.*
Keep the rhythm and melody steady, without a long break.

### 9.6 Answer-first rehearsal
Take 10 common questions (Section 16) and answer in 3 sentences: answer, reason, example. Record and listen.

### 9.7 Story bank
Prepare 5 short stories (60–90 seconds each): a success, a problem solved, a mistake and lesson, a trip or experience, a funny moment. Practice until you can tell each in several versions, not word for word.

### 9.8 Shadowing with pauses
Listen to interviews and notice how native speakers pause, restart, and fill silence. Shadow a clip including the "ums" and restarts, so you hear that they are normal.

### 9.9 Self-monitoring
After a recording, count: fillers per minute, restarts, long silences. Aim to reduce **long silences and restarts** first; a few fillers are fine.

### 9.10 Trouble spots
- Falling into Spanish fillers.
- Restarting from the beginning after any error.
- Long silences before the first word.
- Over-apologizing.
- Never practicing aloud.

## 10. Memory Technique

### 10.1 The road metaphor
Slow down, signal, take another route, correct course. Stop only at red lights (end of turn).

### 10.2 The "three S" rule
When stuck: **Stall** (buy a second), **Simplify** (shorter sentence), **Signal** (tell the listener what you're doing: *Let me rephrase that*).

### 10.3 Five-phrase starter pack
Memorize these first, and use them every day:
1. *Let me think...*
2. *That's a good question.*
3. *How can I put this?*
4. *I mean...*
5. *It's the thing you use to...*

### 10.4 The circumlocution formula
**It's a [category] that [function/feature], like [comparison].**
*It's a tool that you use to remove nails, like a hammer but with a split end.* (claw hammer)

### 10.5 Answer-first formula
**Answer + Because + For example.** Three sentences, always.

### 10.6 Daily micro-practice
Once a day, explain something out loud for 60 seconds (your commute, your breakfast, a news item). No writing. Record once a week.

### 10.7 Spaced retrieval cards
- *Three skills in this lesson?* → **buying time, self-correction, paraphrasing**
- *Neutral English fillers?* → **um, uh, hmm, well, so**
- *Correct a date smoothly?* → **Monday — I mean, Tuesday.**
- *When you can't find a word?* → **the thing you use to... / It's like a... / What's the word for...?**
- *Answer-first structure?* → **Answer, because, example (A–B–C)**
- *Stalling phrase for a hard question?* → **That's a good question. / Let me think.**
- *Spanish "no encuentro la palabra"?* → **I can't find / think of the word.**
- *How long is a normal pause?* → **1–2 seconds**
Review on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30.

### 10.8 Weekly recording
Every Friday, record 2 minutes on the same topic. Compare with the first week: fewer long pauses? Smoother corrections? More use of chunks?

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1: Stall, don't stop.** A short phrase beats silence.

> **Pattern 2: Fix the error, not the whole sentence.**

> **Pattern 3: If the word is missing, describe it** (function, category, comparison, example).

> **Pattern 4: Answer first, then explain.**

> **Pattern 5: Short chunks beat long structures under pressure.**

> **Pattern 6: Plan the next chunk while saying the current one.**

> **Pattern 7: Use neutral English fillers; replace Spanish ones.**

> **Pattern 8: Prepared chunks and stories reduce planning load.**

> **Pattern 9: Meaning first, accuracy later (in study time).**

> **Pattern 10: Calm voice, steady rhythm, natural melody make repairs invisible.**

> **Pattern 11: Ask for the word only after trying to describe it.**

> **Pattern 12: Practice aloud; it's a motor skill.**

> **Connections:** Lessons 59–63 (spoken discourse), Lesson 65–68 (rhythm and melody), Lesson 69 (listening repair), Lesson 71 (naturalness), Phase 9 (organizing ideas in writing).

## 12. Real-Life Examples

### 12.1 Buying time in action
> **Interviewer:** "What's your biggest weakness?"
> **You:** "That's a good question. Let me think... I'd say my biggest weakness is that I sometimes take on too much. For example, last year I volunteered for three projects at once. Now I check my workload before saying yes."

> **Colleague:** "What do you think we should do about the delay?"
> **You:** "Well, ... the way I see it, we have two options. One is to ask for more time. The other is to add people."

### 12.2 Self-correction in action
> "The meeting is on Thursday — sorry, Friday — at three."
> "We have about forty people, or rather, forty-five."
> "I'd like to, um, I mean, I'd love to join, but I have another meeting."
> "She's my boss — well, my former boss."
> "Let me rephrase that: I'm not against the plan, I just think we need more data."

### 12.3 Circumlocution in action
| Missing word | What you say | Listener says |
|---|---|---|
| colander | "The thing you use to drain pasta, with lots of little holes." | "A colander!" |

### 12.4 Dialogues
**1. Searching for a word**
> **A:** "I need to buy a... uh... what's the word? The thing you put on a wall to hang pictures."
> **B:** "A nail? A hook?"
> **A:** "A hook, yes, thanks!"

**2. Changing course**
> **A:** "So we can meet at nine — no, wait, I have a call. Ten?"
> **B:** "Ten works."

**3. Postponing**
> **A:** "Do you have the latest sales figures?"
> **B:** "I'd have to check. Let me get back to you this afternoon."

**4. Answering a hard question**
> **A:** "Why did you leave your last job?"
> **B:** "That's a fair question. Basically, I wanted more responsibility, and there wasn't room to grow. So I started looking for something new."

**5. Clarifying**
> **A:** "It's a bit... how can I put this... not what I expected."
> **B:** "In what way?"
> **A:** "It's more expensive than we agreed."

### 12.5 Before and after
> ❌ "Eh... este... I... I... how do you say... the... the... *colador*... I don't know."
> ✅ "Um, it's the thing you use to drain pasta... with little holes. What's it called?"

> ❌ "I think that... I think that the... that the problem is... no, the problem... the problem is the time."
> ✅ "I think the main problem is the timing."

> ❌ "I'm sorry, my English is very bad. I don't have the words."
> ✅ "Let me put it another way: ..."

**Contrast groups**
| Items | Difference |
|---|---|
| *stall / hesitate / pause* | delay on purpose / be unsure / stop briefly |
| *rephrase / correct / backtrack* | say in different words / fix an error / retreat from a statement |
| *filler / stalling phrase* | a sound (*um*) / a phrase (*Let me think*) |
| *paraphrase / circumlocution* | restate in other words / describe a missing word indirectly |
| *a slip of the tongue / a mistake* | an accidental wrong word in speech / any error |
| *beat around the bush / get to the point* | avoid the main issue / say it directly |

## 17. Speaking Practice
