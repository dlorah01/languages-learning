# Lesson 30 — TAKE
### Phase 2: High-Frequency Verb System | Lesson 2 of 8

---

## 0. Where this fits
Continuing the chunk-based "meaning map" method from Lesson 29, this lesson tackles "take" — a verb whose core meaning (moving something from one place/state/possession to another, often actively/deliberately) generates an enormous number of fixed collocations that don't translate literally from Spanish, making it a genuinely high-value lesson for error correction.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation

If "get" is about arriving at a new state/possession (often somewhat passively — things happen to you), "take" is about **actively grasping, choosing, or engaging with something** — a more deliberate, agentive verb. This core distinction (get = receive/arrive; take = actively grasp/choose) will help you intuitively sort between the two verbs in the many contexts where Spanish would use *tomar* or *coger* for both.

---

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation — TAKE's Major Meaning Families

### 2a. Physically Grasp/Carry
- "Take my hand." (grasp)
- "Take this bag to your room." (carry)

### 2b. Time-Related — "Take" for Duration
- "It takes two hours to get there." (duration required)
- "Take your time." (don't rush — a very common fixed expression)

### 2c. Fixed Collocations — THE High-Value Category
This is where "take" generates enormous numbers of chunks that Spanish speakers frequently get wrong by substituting "make" or "do" instead (a trap flagged back in Lesson 3):

| Correct collocation | Common Spanish-speaker error |
|---|---|
| take a decision → **make** a decision | ❌ "take a decision" |
| take a photo | ❌ "make a photo" |
| take a shower/bath | ❌ "make a shower" |
| take a break | ❌ "make a break" |
| take a risk | ❌ "make a risk" (though "take a chance" and "run a risk" are related, "make a risk" is not standard) |
| take an exam/test | ❌ "make an exam" |
| take a look | ❌ "make a look" |
| take a seat | ❌ "make a seat" |
| take responsibility | ❌ "make responsibility" |
| take advantage of | ❌ "make advantage of" |
| take place (= happen) | ❌ "take a place" / "make place" |
| take care of | ❌ "make care of" |
| take notes | ❌ "make notes" (note: "make a note of something" IS correct for a single specific note — "take notes" is for ongoing/general note-taking, a subtle but real distinction) |

### 2d. Take (someone) somewhere — Escort/Transport
- "I'll take you to the airport." (transport/escort — contrast with "bring," covered in Lesson 33, which involves movement toward the speaker rather than away from them)

### 2e. Take as Tolerate/Endure
- "I can't take this heat anymore." (tolerate)
- "She can really take criticism well." (handle/endure)

### 2f. Take After (resemble, previewed here, fully covered as a phrasal verb in Phase 4)
- "She takes after her mother." (resembles)

---

## 3. Why "Take" Generates So Many Fixed Collocations
"Take" pairs with a huge number of abstract nouns (a decision, a risk, a break, responsibility, advantage, a seat) precisely because English treats these nouns as things you actively "pick up" or "engage with" conceptually — this is a genuinely arbitrary, historically-settled lexical pattern (Lesson 3's "collocation" category), not something derivable from logic alone, which is exactly why these need to be memorized as fixed chunks rather than constructed word-by-word.

---

## 4. When Each Meaning Matters Most
- **Fixed collocations (Section 2c)** — the highest-priority category in this entire lesson, given how frequently Spanish speakers substitute "make" incorrectly (this directly extends Lesson 3's original "take a decision" example).
- **Time-related "take"** — essential for describing duration, extremely common in everyday practical communication.
- **Take someone somewhere** — common in everyday logistics/plans, and important to contrast correctly with "bring" (Lesson 33).

---

## 5. Frequency & Register
"Take" collocations are used across all registers — "take a decision" vs. "make a decision" isn't a formality issue, it's simply which verb is grammatically fixed to that particular noun (only "make a decision" is correct, regardless of register). This makes Section 2c's list genuinely high-priority: these aren't casual-vs-formal alternatives, they're the ONLY correct option.

---

## 6. American vs. British Note
No major systematic differences in the core "take" collocations. One minor exception: "take a decision" (rather than "make a decision") is heard somewhat more in British English, likely influenced by French *prendre une décision* — but "make a decision" remains the dominant, universally correct choice in both varieties, and is the safer default to learn and use.

---

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

| Mistake | Why | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "I need to take a decision." (as the primary/only form learned) | Direct translation from *tomar una decisión* | "I need to make a decision." (the dominant, universally correct form — "take a decision" exists but is less common/more British-leaning) |
| "Can you make a photo of us?" | Direct translation from *tomar/sacar una foto* | "Can you take a photo of us?" |
| "I'm going to make a shower." | Direct translation from *tomar una ducha* | "I'm going to take a shower." |
| "The event will make place next week." | Direct translation from *tener lugar* (mixing "take place" with "have place") | "The event will take place next week." |
| "I need to make notes during the lecture." | Direct translation from *tomar apuntes* | "I need to take notes during the lecture." |

---

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ "Take and make are interchangeable, general-purpose verbs for 'doing' something." — Each specific collocation is fixed and non-interchangeable; you cannot substitute one for the other based on logic alone, only by learning the correct fixed pairing.
- ❌ "Since Spanish uses *tomar* for many of these, English 'take' must always be the correct translation." — Sometimes yes (take a shower, take a photo), but sometimes English requires "make" instead (make a decision) even where Spanish uses *tomar* — the mapping isn't reliable enough to translate word-for-word, which is precisely why memorizing the English collocation as its own chunk (not as a translation of the Spanish chunk) is the more reliable strategy.

---

## 9. Pronunciation Note
"Take" collocations are typically produced as single fluent chunks with natural stress on the following noun, not on "take" itself: "take a DECISION" → but since the correct form is "make a DECISION," the broader point is: practice the entire chunk's natural rhythm together, not "take" and the noun as two separately-stressed, separately-learned items.

---

## 10. Memory Technique
**Build a "Take vs. Make" contrast list** as a single memorized reference (directly extending Lesson 3's chunk-grouping method) — since these two verbs are so frequently confused, studying them side-by-side in a dedicated contrast table (rather than learning "take" collocations and "make" collocations separately, in isolation) is more effective for locking in the correct pairing.

---

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern:** Fixed verb+noun collocations (take a shower, make a decision) are arbitrary from a logical standpoint — they cannot be derived from the verb's "core meaning" alone, and must be learned and drilled as complete chunks, exactly per Lesson 3's central thesis about how vocabulary is actually stored and retrieved by fluent speakers.

---

## 12. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1:**
- "Take a seat, please." (physical + fixed collocation)
- "It takes ten minutes to walk there." (duration)

**Level 2 — take vs. make, side by side:**
- ❌ "I need to make a break." → ✅ "I need to take a break."
- ✅ "I need to make a plan." (make, correctly, for this specific noun)

**Level 3 — take as tolerate, more nuanced usage:**
- "I really can't take his complaining anymore." (tolerate — emotional endurance limit)

**Level 4 — combining multiple TAKE meanings and collocations in one natural passage:**
- "It took me a while to take the decision, but once I did, I decided to take a risk and take the opportunity — even though it meant I'd have to take care of everything on my own." (duration, fixed collocation [note: "take the decision" here reflects the less common but existing British-leaning variant, included deliberately to show it does exist, though "make the decision" remains the safer universal default], fixed collocation, fixed collocation, fixed collocation)
