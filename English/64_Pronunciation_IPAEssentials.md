# Lesson 64 — IPA Essentials for Spanish Speakers
### Phase 7: Pronunciation & the Sound System | Module 1 of 5

---

## 0. Where this fits
Phase 6 showed you that **sounds and melody carry meaning**: a falling pitch yields the floor, a reduced *kinda* sounds natural, *sheet* and *shit* are not the same word. Phase 7 now trains the sound system itself.

This lesson gives you the foundation: **the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)** and the **English sounds that Spanish does not have or treats differently**. Every later pronunciation lesson (word stress, sentence stress, connected speech, intonation) builds on it.

Why IPA, and not "just imitate"?
- English spelling is unreliable (*though, through, tough, cough, thought*). IPA is reliable: **one symbol = one sound**.
- Every serious dictionary shows IPA. Once you read it, you can pronounce **any word** correctly without a teacher.
- Spanish speakers tend to read English words with Spanish spelling rules (*Wednesday* → "Wed-nes-day"). IPA breaks that habit.
- You can only **produce** a sound reliably if you can first **hear** it as different. IPA labels the differences your ear has been trained to ignore.

Connections: Lesson 5 (basic pronunciation foundations), Lessons 59–63 (every spoken lesson had a Pronunciation Note), Lesson 65 (next: word and sentence stress), Phase 8 (listening and speaking).

> **Core idea:** Spanish has **5 vowel sounds** and English has **about 20** (vowels and diphthongs). Your biggest task is not learning "hard" consonants like /θ/; it is **learning to hear and produce the new vowels, the final consonants, and the aspirated stops.**

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
Imagine your mouth is an instrument with three main controls:

1. **Tongue height and position** (high/low, front/back) → mainly decides **vowels**.
2. **Lip shape** (rounded/spread) → colors vowels.
3. **Airflow and obstruction** (stop it, squeeze it, vibrate the vocal cords) → decides **consonants**.

Spanish uses a small, well-organized set of positions. English uses more of the available space, especially in the vowels. When a Spanish speaker says *ship* and *sheep*, both come out like *sheep*, because Spanish has one /i/ slot and English has two (/ɪ/ and /iː/). The listener doesn't hear "an accent"; they hear a **different word**.

Phonetics gives you a **map** of the space and a label for every spot. With the map, you can:
- find which sounds you already have (Spanish /a/, /e/, /o/, /p/, /t/, /k/, /m/, /n/, /f/, /s/, /l/, /tʃ/…),
- find the **new ones** you need to build (/ɪ/, /æ/, /ʌ/, /ʊ/, /ə/, /θ/, /ð/, /ʃ/, /ʒ/, /dʒ/, /v/, /z/, /h/, /r/, /ŋ/),
- and find the **same symbols that behave differently** (Spanish /p t k/ are unaspirated; English ones are aspirated at the start of stressed syllables).

You don't need to master all 44 symbols at once. About **25** are new or different for you. We rank them by importance (Section 5).

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 What the symbols mean
- **/ slashes /** show **phonemes**: the sounds that distinguish words. Dictionaries use these: */ʃɪp/*.
- **[ square brackets ]** show **phonetic detail** (how a sound is actually produced): *[pʰɪn]* with aspiration.
- **ˈ** (high vertical mark) before a syllable: **primary stress**: */ɪkˈskjuːz/*.
- **ˌ** (low vertical mark) before a syllable: **secondary stress**: */ˌɪntəˈrʌpt/*.
- **ː** after a vowel: **long vowel**: */iː/*.
- **Dots (.)** sometimes mark syllable breaks; many dictionaries omit them.
- **Parentheses (r)** in a UK entry may show that /r/ is pronounced only before a vowel.
- Dictionaries often show **US** and **UK** transcriptions side by side (Section 6).

**A note on the model.** This curriculum uses **General American (GA)** as the main model and notes **British (RP)** differences. Both are fully standard. What matters is to **choose one and be consistent.**

### 2.2 The English vowels (GA with UK notes)

Each vowel is named by a **key word** (these "lexical sets" are used by phoneticians as anchors).

#### Monophthongs

| Symbol | Key word | Examples | Closest Spanish | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **/iː/** | FLEECE | see, beat, seat, field, key | Spanish /i/, but **longer and tenser** | smile slightly, tongue high and forward, hold it |
| **/ɪ/** | KIT | sit, bit, ship, women, busy, build | **none** (between /i/ and /e/) | short, relaxed, lips neutral, tongue a little lower than /iː/ |
| **/ɛ/** | DRESS | bed, said, head, many | Spanish /e/ is close | similar to Spanish *e*, slightly more open |
| **/æ/** | TRAP | cat, bad, man, hat, black | **none** (between /e/ and /a/) | mouth open wide, tongue front and low, jaw drops |
| **/ɑː/** | LOT/PALM (GA) | father, hot (GA), calm, spa | Spanish /a/ (more back) | open mouth, tongue low and back |
| **/ɔː/** | THOUGHT | law, caught, talk, all | Spanish /o/ (more open) | rounded lips, tongue low-mid back; in many US dialects it merges with /ɑː/ |
| **/ʌ/** | STRUT | cup, love, blood, but, up | **none** | short, central, relaxed, mouth slightly open, **not /a/ and not /o/** |
| **/ʊ/** | FOOT | put, book, could, good | none (Spanish /u/ is tenser) | short, relaxed, lips slightly rounded |
| **/uː/** | GOOSE | food, blue, shoe, who, you | Spanish /u/ but **longer** | round lips, tongue high and back, hold it |
| **/ə/** | schwa | **a**bout, banan**a**, tak**e**n, lem**o**n | **none** (neutral) | the weakest sound; relaxed mouth, no particular shape |
| **/ɜːr/** | NURSE | bird, work, learn, turn, her | none | tongue pulled back, r-colored, no clear Spanish vowel |
| **/ɚ/** | lettER | teach**er**, doct**or**, sugar | none | unstressed r-colored schwa |

*Note on UK:* LOT is **/ɒ/** (*hot* /hɒt/), NURSE is **/ɜː/** (no r), and BATH words (*bath, dance, can't*) have **/ɑː/** in the UK vs. **/æ/** in the US.

#### Diphthongs (two vowels glided together)

| Symbol | Key word | Examples | Notes for Spanish speakers |
|---|---|---|---|
| **/eɪ/** | FACE | say, day, make, eight | Spanish /e/ is a pure vowel. English glides toward /ɪ/. Don't cut it short. |
| **/aɪ/** | PRICE | my, time, nine, high | similar to Spanish *ai*, but starts more open |
| **/ɔɪ/** | CHOICE | boy, coin, voice | similar to Spanish *oy* |
| **/aʊ/** | MOUTH | now, house, out, how | similar to Spanish *au* |
| **/oʊ/** | GOAT | go, home, know, boat | Spanish /o/ is pure. English glides toward /ʊ/. UK: **/əʊ/**. |

#### R-colored vowels (GA) / centring diphthongs (UK)
| GA | Key word | Examples | UK |
|---|---|---|---|
| **/ɪr/** | NEAR | here, beer, clear | /ɪə/ |
| **/ɛr/** | SQUARE | care, there, where | /eə/ |
| **/ʊr/** | CURE | sure, tour, pure | /ʊə/ (often /ɔː/) |
| **/ɑːr/** | START | car, far, star | /ɑː/ |
| **/ɔːr/** | NORTH/FORCE | more, door, port | /ɔː/ |

**Key point:** in GA, /r/ after a vowel is **always pronounced** (*car, doctor, bird*). In RP it is not (non-rhotic). Spanish speakers usually pronounce a Spanish tap/trill for *r*; the English /r/ is an **approximant** (the tongue never touches).

### 2.3 The English consonants (and Spanish comparisons)

| Group | Symbols | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Stops** | /p b t d k ɡ/ | /p t k/ **aspirated** at the start of stressed syllables (*pin* [pʰɪn]); /b d ɡ/ voiced |
| **Fricatives** | /f v θ ð s z ʃ ʒ h/ | /θ ð ʃ ʒ v z h/ are the trouble spots |
| **Affricates** | /tʃ dʒ/ | /tʃ/ as in *church*; /dʒ/ as in *judge* |
| **Nasals** | /m n ŋ/ | /ŋ/ as in *sing* |
| **Approximants** | /l r j w/ | /l/ has light and dark variants; /r/ is not a tap or trill |

|---|---|---|
| /p/ | /b/ | pat / bat |
| /t/ | /d/ | time / dime |
| /k/ | /ɡ/ | back / bag |

**Voicing test:** place fingers on your throat. If you feel vibration, it's voiced.

### 2.4 Consonants that are new or different (detailed)

| Symbol | Key word | Examples | Spanish situation | How to make it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **/θ/** | **th**ink | think, three, month, bath | Colombian Spanish (seseo) has no /θ/ | tongue tip lightly between teeth, blow air, no voice |
| **/ð/** | **th**is | this, that, mother, breathe | [ð] exists only as a soft *d* between vowels (*lado*) | same tongue position as /θ/ + voice |
| **/ʃ/** | **sh**ip | ship, fish, nation, machine | not a normal phoneme in Colombian Spanish | round lips slightly, tongue back from teeth, long "sh" |
| **/ʒ/** | mea**s**ure | measure, vision, usual, beige | none | voiced version of /ʃ/ |
| **/tʃ/** | **ch**urch | church, cheese, match | exists (*chico*) | same sound; avoid replacing /ʃ/ with it |
| **/dʒ/** | **j**udge | judge, jam, age, gym | not standard | voiced /tʃ/: "d" + "zh" blended |
| **/v/** | **v**ery | very, love, five | Spanish *b* and *v* are the same sound | upper teeth on lower lip + voice (like /f/ with voice) |
| **/z/** | **z**oo | zoo, easy, dogs, has | no /z/ phoneme in Colombian Spanish | same as /s/ with voice (buzzing) |
| **/h/** | **h**ouse | house, who, behind | Spanish *j* is a harsh /x/ | just a breath, throat open, no friction |
| **/r/** | **r**ed | red, right, around | Spanish tap /ɾ/ and trill /r/ | tongue tip curled or bunched, never touches the roof |
| **/l/** | **l**ight / ba**ll** | light (clear), ball (dark) | Spanish /l/ is always light | for final /l/, back of tongue rises (dark /l/) |
| **/ŋ/** | si**ng** | sing, long, thinking | exists before /k/ or /g/ (*banco*) | tongue back touches soft palate; **no /ɡ/ at the end** |
| **/j/** | **y**es | yes, you, use | similar to Spanish *y* | glide, no friction; not /dʒ/ |
| **/w/** | **w**ater | we, want, away | Spanish [w] in *hueso* | round lips; **no /ɡ/ before it** (avoid saying "gwater") |
| **/p t k/** | **p**in, **t**ime, **k**ey | pin, time, key | unaspirated in Spanish | at the start of stressed syllables, add a puff of air |

### 2.5 Aspiration (an invisible difference that changes meaning)
English /p t k/ at the start of stressed syllables are produced with a puff of air:
- *pin* [pʰɪn], *time* [tʰaɪm], *key* [kʰiː].
- After /s/ there is no puff: *spin* [spɪn], *star* [stɑːr], *sky* [skaɪ].
- Spanish /p t k/ have **no puff**, so to an English ear *pin* sounds like *bin*, *time* like *dime*, *come* like *gum*.

**Test:** hold a thin tissue near your mouth. Say *pin*: the tissue moves. Say *spin*: it barely moves. Practice until *pin* makes the tissue move.

### 2.6 Final consonants and clusters
- Spanish words end mainly in vowels or in /n, s, r, l, d/. English words end in nearly any consonant: *cat, bed, back, laugh, bath, cups*.
- English clusters can have up to three consonants at the start (*street, splash, strong*) and four at the end (*texts, sixths /sɪksθs/*).
- Spanish speakers often **add a vowel** before s-clusters (*Spain* → "e-Spain") or **drop** the final consonant (*hand* → "han").
- **Fix:** practice *s* + consonant as one unit: /sp/ /st/ /sk/ /sl/ /sm/ /sn/ /str/ /spr/ /skr/. Keep every final consonant.

**Vowel length and final voicing**
Before a voiced consonant the vowel is **longer**; before a voiceless consonant it is **shorter**:
- *seat* (shorter vowel, voiceless /t/) vs. *seed* (longer vowel, voiced /d/).
- *bet* vs. *bed*, *back* vs. *bag*, *rice* vs. *rise*.
In English, **the vowel length** is the main cue distinguishing final /t/ and /d/. Learn to lengthen the vowel before voiced endings.

### 2.7 How the Colombian Spanish system affects English
(Variation exists; use this as a checklist, not a rule.)
- **Seseo:** *s* for *z/c*. You have no /θ/ in your system; you need to build it.
- **Coastal Colombian (Caribbean) Spanish:** final /s/ is often weakened or dropped (*los amigos* ≈ "lo amigo"). This can lead to missing plural /s/ and third-person /s/ in English (*he work*, *two book*), which changes grammar and meaning. Train final /s/ and /z/ explicitly.
- **Andean/Bogotá Spanish:** keeps final /s/ but may reduce unstressed vowels more than other dialects; and some regions assibilate /r/ (a buzzy /r/). Watch for a buzzy English /r/.
- **/x/ (jota):** in much of Colombia it's a strong velar or glottal fricative. English /h/ is much softer.
- **Spanish b/v merger:** *very* and *berry* sound identical. Build /v/ with teeth on lip.
- **Syllable timing:** Spanish is syllable-timed (each syllable about equal); English is stress-timed. This affects vowel reduction (Lesson 65).

### 2.8 How to read a dictionary entry
Example (Cambridge/Oxford style):

> **photograph** *noun* — US /ˈfoʊtəˌɡræf/ — UK /ˈfəʊtəɡrɑːf/

Reading it:
1. **ˈ** before *foʊ*: primary stress on the first syllable.
2. */foʊ/*: /f/ + diphthong /oʊ/ (GOAT).
3. */tə/*: /t/ + schwa (unstressed).
4. **ˌ** before *ɡræf*: secondary stress.
5. */ɡræf/*: /ɡ/ + /æ/ (TRAP) + /f/.
6. UK: /əʊ/ for GOAT, and /ɑː/ for BATH in the final syllable.

**Practice process (for any new word)**
1. Look it up.
2. Play the audio (US and UK).
3. Read the IPA while listening.
4. Mark stress and any new sounds.
5. Say it three times; record once.
6. Add it to your notebook with IPA.

### 2.10 Intelligibility priorities (what matters most)
Research on English as an international language (for example, Jenkins's "Lingua Franca Core") suggests that for **being understood** by diverse listeners, the most important features are:
1. vowel **length** contrasts (*ship/sheep, seat/seed*);
2. **aspiration** of /p t k/;
3. **consonant clusters** (don't delete or add vowels);
4. **nuclear stress** (the main stressed syllable in a phrase, Lesson 65);
5. most consonants (especially /v/, /z/, /ʃ/, /h/).
Less critical for intelligibility (though valued for accuracy): /θ ð/ (often replaced by /s z/ or /t d/ without breakdown), dark /l/, and exact weak forms.

This doesn't mean "skip /θ/." It means **prioritize** so your effort goes where it makes you easiest to understand.

## 3. Why Native Speakers Do This
Native speakers don't "decide" to use these sounds; they are the sounds of the language they acquired. But several facts explain **why English sounds the way it does**:
1. **A large vowel inventory.** English evolved from several languages (Germanic, French, Latin), which left it with many vowel contrasts and an irregular spelling.
2. **Stress-timed rhythm.** Stressed syllables come at roughly regular intervals; unstressed syllables are squeezed and their vowels reduced to /ə/ or /ɪ/. This is why English has so many schwas.
3. **Voicing and length as cues.** English relies on vowel length and voicing to mark word endings, because many final consonants are weakly released.
4. **Aspiration as a boundary signal.** Aspirated stops mark the start of stressed syllables, helping listeners parse speech.
5. **Spelling reflects history, not sound.** Many silent letters (*knight, psychology*) are fossils of older pronunciation.
6. **Connected speech.** In natural speech sounds are linked, reduced, and merged (*want to* → "wanna"). Learning the sounds in isolation is only the first step; Phase 7 continues with them in context.

## 4. When to Use / When Not To

**Use IPA when**
- you meet a new word (always check the transcription);
- you notice you can't hear a difference (minimal pairs);
- you study pronunciation systematically (notebook with IPA);
- you teach or explain sounds to others;
- you want to practice with dictionaries and apps.

**You don't need IPA when**
- you are shadowing connected speech for rhythm (use listening and imitation);
- you are chatting (don't think in symbols);
- you can already hear and produce the sound reliably.

**Approach**
- **Notice → label → drill → use.** Notice a problem sound in your speech or listening; label it with IPA; drill it in minimal pairs and words; then use it in sentences.
- **Don't try to sound like someone else.** Aim for **clear, comfortable, consistent** pronunciation.
- **Do not mix models randomly** (US /r/ with UK vowels). Pick a primary model.
- **Avoid over-articulation.** Exaggerating every sound in connected speech sounds stiff. Isolate in practice, relax in conversation.

## 5. Frequency & Priority

Rank the sounds by **importance for Spanish speakers** (frequency of impact on understanding plus how common they are):

| Priority | Sounds | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | /ɪ/ vs /iː/ | extremely frequent; distinguishes many words (*it/eat, ship/sheep, live/leave*) |
| **2** | /æ/, /ʌ/, /ɑː/ (and /ɛ/) | frequent; Spanish merges them into /a/ or /e/ (*cat/cut/cot, bad/bed*) |
| **3** | /ə/ (schwa) and vowel reduction | the most common vowel in English; needed for natural rhythm |
| **4** | final consonants and clusters | affect grammar (*-s, -ed*) and clarity |
| **5** | aspiration of /p t k/ | prevents *pin → bin* confusion |
| **6** | /ʊ/ vs /uː/ | *full/fool, pull/pool* |
| **7** | /s/ vs /z/ endings | plural and third-person marking; voicing |
| **8** | /v/ vs /b/ | *very/berry, vest/best* |
| **9** | /ʃ/, /tʃ/, /dʒ/, /ʒ/ | *ship/chip/jip/…, share/chair* |
| **10** | /h/ and /r/ | *hair, red, right, around* |
| **11** | /θ/, /ð/ | highly visible, less critical for understanding |
| **12** | dark /l/, /ŋ/, /w/, /j/ | refinements |
| **13** | diphthong gliding (/eɪ/, /oʊ/) | naturalness |

**A weekly plan:** one sound pair per week, 10 minutes a day. After 12 weeks you will have covered the priority list.

## 6. American vs. British Notes

| Feature | General American (GA) | British (RP) |
|---|---|---|
| **Postvocalic /r/** | pronounced (*car, hard, doctor*) | not pronounced unless before a vowel (*car* /kɑː/) |
| **LOT vowel** | /ɑː/ (*hot, stop, box*) | /ɒ/ (*hot* /hɒt/) |
| **THOUGHT vowel** | /ɔː/ (merged with /ɑː/ in many US dialects) | /ɔː/ |
| **BATH words** | /æ/ (*bath, dance, can't, ask*) | /ɑː/ |
| **GOAT vowel** | /oʊ/ | /əʊ/ |
| **NURSE vowel** | /ɜːr/ (r-colored) | /ɜː/ |
| **Intervocalic /t/** | flapped [ɾ] (*water, better, city* ≈ "wader") | clear /t/ or glottal stop in casual speech ("wa'er") |
| **/t/ before /n/** | often glottal (*button* [ˈbʌʔn]) | similar |
| **Yod dropping** | *new* /nuː/, *Tuesday* /ˈtuːzdeɪ/ | *new* /njuː/, *Tuesday* /ˈtjuːzdeɪ/ |
| **/æ/ in some words** | *can't* /kænt/ | *can't* /kɑːnt/ |
| **Stress in some words** | *advertisement* /ˌædvərˈtaɪzmənt/, *garage* /ɡəˈrɑːʒ/ | *advertisement* /ədˈvɜːtɪsmənt/, *garage* /ˈɡærɑːʒ/ |
| **-ile endings** | *fertile* /ˈfɜːrtl/ | *fertile* /ˈfɜːtaɪl/ |
| **Schedule** | /ˈskɛdʒuːl/ | /ˈʃɛdjuːl/ |
| **Either/neither** | /ˈiːðər/, /ˈniːðər/ | /ˈaɪðə/, /ˈnaɪðə/ (both accepted in both) |
| **Linking/intrusive r** | rare | *law and order* → "lawr and order" |
| **Pitch** | generally flatter | wider, with more falls |

**Which model should you choose?** Colombian learners are most exposed to American English (media, work, travel), so GA is a natural default. If you work with British companies, adapt. In either case, aim for **consistency** and **clarity**, not "perfect" accent.

**Note on dictionaries.** Cambridge and Oxford Learner's show both US and UK; Merriam-Webster uses its own respelling (e.g., \ˈfō-tə-ˌgraf\), not IPA; read its audio rather than its symbols unless you know the system.

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

### 7.1 Vowels
| Sound problem | Typical error | Examples | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| **/ɪ/ → /iː/** | *ship* sounds like *sheep* | sit/seat, live/leave, fill/feel, bit/beat, ship/sheep | shorten and relax; no smile |
| **/iː/ → too short** | *sheep* sounds like *ship* | beach/bitch (⚠ avoid), sheet/shit (⚠ avoid), seen/sin | lengthen, smile |
| **/æ/ → /ɛ/** | *bad* → *bed*, *man* → *men* | bad/bed, pan/pen, sad/said, man/men | open jaw wide |
| **/æ/ → /a/** | *cat* → Spanish *cat* | hat, black, apple | drop jaw, tongue forward |
| **/ʌ/ → /a/ or /o/** | *cup* → "cap" or "cop" | cup, bus, love, much | central, relaxed, short |
| **/ɑː/ → /o/** | *hot* → "hoat" | hot, stop, box | open wide, lips unrounded |
| **/ʊ/ → /uː/** | *full* → *fool* | full/fool, pull/pool, look/Luke | short, relaxed |
| **/uː/ too short** | *pool* → *pull* | food, blue, group | round and hold |
| **/ɔː/ vs /oʊ/** | *law* → *low* | caught/coat, law/low | /ɔː/ rounded, no glide; /oʊ/ glides |
| **/eɪ/, /oʊ/ without glide** | *say* → "se", *go* → "go" (pure) | say, day, go, home | glide to /ɪ/ or /ʊ/ |
| **Schwa missing** | *banana* → "ba-NA-na" with full /a/s | banana, about, family | reduce unstressed vowels |
| **/ɜːr/** | *bird* → "berd" | bird, learn, world, work | tongue back, lips slightly rounded |
| **Silent letter vowels** | "make" → "ma-ke" | make, time, cake | final -e is silent |

### 7.2 Consonants
| Sound problem | Typical error | Examples | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| **/θ/ → /s/, /t/, /f/** | *think* → "sink", "tink", "fink" | think, three, month | tongue tip between teeth |
| **/ð/ → /d/** | *this* → "dis" | this, that, mother | same position + voice |
| **/ʃ/ → /tʃ/** | *shoe* → "chew" | ship/chip, share/chair, wash/watch | long continuous "sh" |
| **/tʃ/ → /ʃ/** | *chair* → "share" | cheap/sheep | stop then release |
| **/dʒ/ → /j/ or /ʃ/** | *Jack* → "yack" | judge, job, age, jam | "d" + "zh" blended |
| **/ʒ/ missing** | *vision* → "vi-sion" /s/ | measure, usual, beige | voiced "sh" |
| **/v/ → /b/** | *very* → "berry" | very/berry, vest/best, vote/boat | upper teeth on lower lip |
| **/z/ → /s/** | *easy* → "eesy" | zoo/Sue, peace/peas, rose/rows | add voice (buzz) |
| **/h/ → /x/** | *house* → harsh "xouse" | house, hotel, behind | soft breath |
| **Silent h pronounced** | *hour* → "hour" with /h/ | hour, honest, heir | no /h/ |
| **/r/ → trill/tap** | *red* → rolled | red, run, very, around | approximant, no tongue contact |
| **Dark /l/ missing** | *ball* → "bao" or "ball" light | ball, milk, people | raise back of tongue |
| **/ŋ/ → /n/ or /ŋɡ/** | *sing* → "sin" or "sing-g" | sing, thinking, bring | back of tongue, no /ɡ/ |
| **Aspiration missing** | *pin* → "bin" | pin, time, key | puff of air |
| **Final consonant dropped** | *bed* → "be", *hand* → "han" | hand, test, world, cats | keep every final consonant |
| **Final voiced stops devoiced** | *bed* → "bet" | bed/bet, bag/back | lengthen vowel |
| **Initial s-cluster vowel** | *school* → "eschool" | Spain, student, special, start | no vowel before /s/ |
| **/w/ → /gw/** | *want* → "gwant" | water, we, would | round lips only |
| **/j/ → /dʒ/** | *yes* → "jes" | yes, you, year | no friction |
| **-ed endings** | *walked* → "walk-ed" /ɪd/ | walked, played, wanted | /t/ after voiceless, /d/ after voiced, /ɪd/ after /t d/ |
| **-s endings** | *dogs* → "dogs" with /s/ | cats /s/, dogs /z/, buses /ɪz/ | voice the ending after voiced sounds |

### 7.4 Taboo pairs (important)
Mistaking /ɪ/ and /iː/ can create embarrassing words:
- *sheet* /ʃiːt/ vs. *shit* /ʃɪt/;
- *beach* /biːtʃ/ vs. *bitch* /bɪtʃ/;
- *peace* vs. *piss*.
Learn them early, with a short smile, and keep the long vowel long.

### 7.5 Attitudes and habits
- **Over-correcting:** exaggerating every sound makes speech stiff.
- **Giving up** after "I can't hear it." Hearing improves with training.
- **Imitating without feedback.** Record yourself and compare.
- **Practicing only isolated words.** Move to phrases and sentences quickly.
- **Ignoring vowels** while obsessing over /θ/.
- **Mixing US/UK** randomly.
- **Equating accent with mistakes.** An accent is not an error; unintelligibility is.

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ **"IPA is for linguists."** — It's a practical tool: about 25 symbols matter for you.
- ❌ **"I need to lose my accent."** — Goal: clear, comfortable, intelligible speech. Your accent is part of you.
- ❌ **"Spanish vowels are the same as English ones."** — Spanish has 5; English has ~20 vowel sounds.
- ❌ **"/θ/ is the biggest problem."** — Vowel contrasts, final consonants, and stress affect understanding more.
- ❌ **"If I can't hear a sound, I can't produce it."** — Training perception and production together works; they improve each other.
- ❌ **"English spelling is random."** — It has patterns; IPA helps you see them.
- ❌ **"Pronouncing every letter makes me clear."** — Reading letters causes errors. Use IPA.
- ❌ **"Adults can't improve pronunciation."** — Adults improve substantially with focused practice and feedback, even if some features persist.
- ❌ **"There is one correct English accent."** — Many standard accents exist (US, UK, Australian, Canadian, Irish…).
- ❌ **"Dictionaries disagree, so they are wrong."** — They reflect US/UK and sometimes alternatives.
- ❌ **"Practice must be long."** — 10 focused minutes daily beats 1 hour weekly.
- ❌ **"More mouth movement is always better."** — Precision matters; exaggeration can distort.

## 9. Pronunciation Note: Training Method

This lesson is the pronunciation lesson, so this section describes **how to train**.

### 9.1 The six-step cycle for any new sound
1. **Listen** to the sound in isolation and in 5 words (US audio).
2. **Notice** where your tongue, lips, and jaw are (use a mirror).
3. **Contrast** with the closest Spanish sound.
4. **Produce** in isolation → syllables → words → phrases → sentences.
5. **Record** yourself and compare with the model.
6. **Use** in conversation with a target word list.

### 9.2 Mouth maps (simple)
**Vowel space (front → back, high → low):**
```
           FRONT              CENTRAL            BACK
HIGH     iː (fleece)                           uː (goose)
          ɪ (kit)                           ʊ (foot)
MID       ɛ (dress)       ə (schwa)  ʌ (strut)   ɔː (thought)
LOW       æ (trap)                             ɑː (lot/palm)
```
- Moving down: jaw opens.
- Moving back: tongue pulls back.
- Rounded: /uː/, /ʊ/, /ɔː/.

**Consonant places:** lips (p b m w) → teeth + lip (f v) → between teeth (θ ð) → alveolar ridge (t d s z n l) → post-alveolar (ʃ ʒ tʃ dʒ r) → hard palate (j) → soft palate (k ɡ ŋ) → throat (h).

### 9.3 Tools and resources
- **Dictionaries with audio and IPA:** Cambridge Dictionary, Oxford Learner's Dictionaries, Longman.
- **Video-in-context search:** YouGlish (type a word, hear it in real speech).
- **Native recordings:** Forvo.
- **Mouth animations:** University of Iowa "Sounds of Speech."
- **Recording tools:** your phone's voice recorder; compare to dictionary audio.

## 10. Memory Technique

### 10.1 Key-word anchors (lexical sets)
Attach each new vowel to a **key word** you will remember: FLEECE, KIT, DRESS, TRAP, STRUT, LOT/PALM, THOUGHT, FOOT, GOOSE, FACE, PRICE, CHOICE, MOUTH, GOAT, NURSE. When you check any word, ask: "Does it rhyme with FLEECE or KIT?"

### 10.2 One vowel at a time (spaced mastery)
Week plan:
1. Week 1: /iː/ vs /ɪ/
2. Week 2: /æ/ vs /ɛ/
3. Week 3: /ʌ/ vs /æ/ vs /ɑː/
4. Week 4: /ʊ/ vs /uː/
5. Week 5: schwa and reduction
6. Week 6: final consonants and clusters
7. Week 7: aspiration and voicing
8. Week 8: /v/, /z/, /ʃ/, /dʒ/
9. Week 9: /h/, /r/, dark /l/
10. Week 10: /θ/, /ð/
11. Week 11: diphthongs and /ŋ/
12. Week 12: review in connected speech

### 10.3 The sound notebook
For each target sound:
- the IPA symbol,
- the key word,
- 10 example words,
- 3 minimal pairs,
- a mouth-position note in your own words,
- a tongue twister,
- a sentence that you will actually say.

### 10.4 Body cues
- /iː/: smile. /uː/: kiss shape. /æ/: yawn wide. /ʌ/: relaxed "uh." /θ/: tongue tip visible. /v/: bite your lower lip lightly. /h/: fog a mirror. /ʃ/: "be quiet" sound. /z/: feel buzzing. Aspiration: tissue moves.
- Use a mirror or phone camera.

### 10.5 Record–compare–adjust loop
Every day: record 30 seconds; listen for **one** feature; compare with a native recording; adjust. Keep recordings to hear progress after 4 weeks.

### 10.6 Spaced retrieval cards
- Front: *Symbol /æ/* → **TRAP: cat, bad, man**
- Front: *Symbol /ʌ/* → **STRUT: cup, but, love**
- Front: *Symbol /ɪ/* → **KIT: sit, ship, live**
- Front: *Symbol /θ/* → **think, three, month**
- Front: *Why do *pin* and *spin* differ?* → **aspiration**
- Front: *bed vs bet: main cue?* → **vowel length before the final consonant**
Review on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30.

### 10.7 Transcription as listening training
Each day pick 5 words you hear. Write the IPA from memory, then check in the dictionary. This trains your ear and your IPA reading at the same time.

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1: Short vs long vowels differ in quality too.** /ɪ/ is not just a short /iː/; it's more relaxed and lower.

> **Pattern 2: Voicing lengthens the preceding vowel.** *bet* < *bed*, *back* < *bag*, *rice* < *rise*.

> **Pattern 3: Aspiration = start of stressed syllable.** *pin, top, car*; not after /s/: *spin, stop, scar*.

> **Pattern 4: Unstressed vowels reduce to /ə/ or /ɪ/.** *about, banana, family, today*.

> **Pattern 5: -s and -ed endings follow voicing.** voiceless → /s, t/; voiced → /z, d/; sibilants/dental stops → /ɪz, ɪd/.

> **Pattern 6: The letter does not decide the sound.** Check the IPA for new words.

> **Pattern 7: Silent letters are often historical.** *kn-, wr-, -mb, -gh, -lk (walk, talk).*

> **Pattern 8: Cluster rule.** Keep s-clusters together; do not add a vowel before them.

> **Pattern 9: Final consonants carry grammar.** *-s, -ed, -t* tell tense and number.

> **Pattern 10: Diphthongs glide.** /eɪ/, /oʊ/, /aɪ/, /aʊ/, /ɔɪ/ end in a different position than they start.

> **Pattern 11: Pairs by place of articulation.** /p b/ /t d/ /k ɡ/ /f v/ /θ ð/ /s z/ /ʃ ʒ/ /tʃ dʒ/: same mouth shape, voicing different.

> **Pattern 12: Work in layers.** Sound → word → phrase → sentence → conversation.

> **Connections:** Lesson 5 (basic pronunciation), Lesson 65 (word and sentence stress will use schwa and stress marks), Lesson 66 onward (connected speech and intonation use these sounds), Phase 8 (listening for them).

## 17. Speaking Practice

**Drill 1 — Minimal pairs (daily, 5 minutes).** Say 10 pairs from Section 9.4; record; compare.

**Drill 2 — Aspiration test.** With a tissue, say 10 words with initial /p t k/ (*pin, time, key, pen, tell, cat…*) and 10 with s-clusters (*spin, stop, sky…*). Observe the tissue.

**Drill 3 — Vowel ladder.** Say a sequence slowly: /iː – ɪ – ɛ – æ/, then /uː – ʊ – ʌ – ɑː/. Feel the jaw open step by step.

**Drill 4 — Final consonants.** Say 20 words ending in clusters (*hand, test, world, texts, months, asked*) keeping every final sound.

**Drill 5 — Voicing pairs.** Say *bet–bed, back–bag, rice–rise, peace–peas*, lengthening the vowel before voiced endings.

**Drill 6 — Tongue twisters.** Choose three from Section 9.5. Start slow, then increase speed without losing accuracy.

**Drill 7 — Sentence reading.** Read a 100-word text, recording yourself, focusing on three target sounds (e.g., /ɪ/, /æ/, /θ/). Underline them before reading.

**Drill 8 — Shadowing.** Shadow 1 minute of a US speaker (podcast, YouTube). Focus on vowels and final consonants.

**Drill 9 — Partner minimal pairs.** A partner says one word from a pair; you point to which you heard (training perception). Then swap.

**Mini Quiz**
1. Slashes show phonemes (dictionary transcription); brackets show detailed phonetic realization.
2. Spanish: 5; English: about 20 (vowels and diphthongs).
3. /ɪ/ and /iː/.
4. A puff of air on /p t k/ at the start of stressed syllables; without it *pin* sounds like *bin*.
5. Final *-s* and *-ed* mark plural, third person, and past; dropping them changes meaning.
6. The weak, neutral vowel /ə/ in unstressed syllables (*about, banana*).
7. Tongue tip lightly between teeth, blow air; /ð/ is the same with voicing.
8. Spanish merges *b* and *v*; the English /v/ needs teeth on the lower lip.
9. GA pronounces /r/ after vowels; RP does not.
10. Primary stress on the following syllable.
11. It can change words (*sheet/shit, beach/bitch*).
12. Between vowels, usually before an unstressed syllable (*water, better*).
13. Vowel length is a main cue for final voiced vs. voiceless consonants (*bet/bed*).
14. Any three: listen, notice, contrast, produce in layers, record, use.
