# Lesson 50 — High-Frequency Business & Academic Phrasal Verbs
### Phase 4: Phrasal Verbs | Module 7 of 7 (Phase capstone)

---

## 0. Where this fits
Lessons 44–49 taught phrasal verbs **by verb** (GET, TAKE, PUT, COME/GO, BREAK/TURN/SET, LOOK/CARRY/BRING). That is the best way to understand the system. But in real life, you meet phrasal verbs **by situation**: a meeting, an email, a presentation, a research paper. This lesson reorganizes everything **by function**, and adds the high-frequency verbs that did not fit under a single core verb (*follow up, point out, rule out, roll out, factor in, draw on*).

It is also the **capstone of Phase 4**: after this lesson you should be able to (1) predict a phrasal verb's meaning from its particle, (2) place objects correctly, (3) choose between phrasal and formal verbs by register, and (4) use the most useful ones in professional and academic contexts without hesitation.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
In professional English there are about a dozen **jobs** that phrasal verbs do. Learn the job first; the verb follows.

| Job | Typical phrasal verbs |
|---|---|
| **Starting / launching** | set up, roll out, kick off, start up, launch |
| **Following through** | follow up, follow through, carry out, see through |
| **Raising and discussing** | bring up, point out, go over, run through, walk through |
| **Deciding and eliminating** | rule out, narrow down, weed out, opt for, settle on |
| **Reducing / increasing** | cut back, scale back, scale up, ramp up, bring down, phase out |
| **Delegating / handling** | hand over, take on, pass on, sign off on, deal with |
| **Solving / understanding** | work out, figure out, sort out, iron out, make out |
| **Supporting / proving** | back up, draw on, bear out, point to, rely on |
| **Meeting / scheduling** | set up, push back, bring forward, call off, drop in |
| **Communicating** | get back to, reach out, fill in, speak up, run by |
| **Analysing (academic)** | break down, account for, carry out, draw on, look into |
| **Concluding** | wrap up, sum up, round off, come down to |

Think of a project's life cycle: you **set up**, **kick off**, **roll out**, **follow up**, **iron out** problems, **scale up**, and **wrap up**. This lesson follows that story.

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 The main verbs by function

**A. Starting and launching**
- **set up** — establish: *We set up a new department.*
- **kick off** — begin (a meeting, project, campaign): *Let's kick off the meeting.* (From football; informal-neutral.)
- **roll out** — launch gradually: *The company rolled out the new app in three countries.*
- **start up / launch** — *start up* a business: *She started up her own firm.*
- **gear up** — prepare: *We're gearing up for the holiday season.*
- **ramp up** — increase quickly: *They ramped up production.*

**B. Following through**
- **follow up (on)** — take further action, check progress: *I'll follow up on that tomorrow.* / *Please follow up with the client.*
- **follow through (on)** — complete what you promised: *He never follows through on his commitments.*
- **see through** — carry to completion: *I want to see this project through.*
- **stick to** — adhere to: *Let's stick to the plan.*
- **live up to** — meet expectations (three-part): *The product didn't live up to expectations.*

**C. Raising and discussing**
- **bring up** — raise a topic.
- **point out** — draw attention to: *She pointed out a mistake in the figures.*
- **go over / run through / walk through** — review step by step: *Let me walk you through the process.*
- **touch on** — mention briefly: *The report touches on several issues.*
- **dwell on** — spend too long on: *Let's not dwell on the past.*
- **come up** — arise: *Something came up.*
- **bring to the table** — contribute (idiom).

**D. Deciding and eliminating**
- **rule out** — exclude: *We can't rule out a delay.*
- **narrow down** — reduce options: *We narrowed down the candidates to three.*
- **weed out** — remove unsuitable items: *The test weeds out unqualified applicants.*
- **opt for** — choose: *They opted for the cheaper plan.*
- **settle on** — finally decide: *We settled on Friday.*
- **pick out / single out** — select from many: *She was singled out for praise.*
- **sign off on** (three-part) — formally approve: *The director signed off on the budget.*
- **go ahead with** — proceed.

**E. Reducing and increasing**
- **cut back (on)** — reduce spending: *We need to cut back on expenses.*
- **scale back** — reduce size or ambition: *They scaled back their plans.*
- **scale up / scale down** — increase / decrease size.
- **phase out** — remove gradually: *The company is phasing out paper invoices.*
- **wind down** — slowly reduce: *The project is winding down.*
- **boil down to** — be essentially: *It boils down to money.* (Compare *come down to*, Lesson 47.)
- **step up** — increase effort: *We need to step up our efforts.*

**F. Delegating and handling**
- **hand over** — give responsibility: *She handed over the project to Paul.*
- **pass on** — transmit: *Please pass on my thanks.*
- **take on** — accept (Lesson 45).
- **deal with** — handle (inseparable): *I'll deal with it.*
- **step down / step in** — resign / replace temporarily.
- **cover for** — substitute for: *Can you cover for me on Friday?*
- **leave out / opt out** — exclude / withdraw.
- **hold off** — delay action: *Let's hold off on the decision.*

**G. Solving and understanding**
- **work out** — (1) solve: *We worked out a solution.* (2) exercise. (3) calculate: *work out the cost.*
- **figure out** — understand or solve (informal-neutral): *I can't figure out why it's not working.*
- **sort out** — organize, resolve: *Let's sort out the details.*
- **iron out** — remove small problems: *We still need to iron out a few issues.*
- **make out** — understand with difficulty: *I can't make out what he's saying.*
- **end up** — finally be in a situation: *We ended up cancelling.*
- **pan out** — develop (positive/negative): *Let's see how it pans out.*
- **find out** — discover.

**H. Supporting and proving**
- **back up** — (1) support: *The data backs up our claim.* (2) make a copy: *Back up your files.*
- **draw on / draw upon** — use as a resource: *She draws on years of experience.*
- **bear out** — confirm: *The results bear out the hypothesis.*
- **point to** — indicate: *The evidence points to a different conclusion.*
- **rely on / depend on / count on** — trust (inseparable).
- **account for** — explain, make up (a proportion): *Exports account for 40% of revenue.*
- **factor in** — include in calculation: *Factor in shipping costs.*
- **call for** — require: *The situation calls for caution.*
- **lead to / result in / give rise to** — cause.

**I. Meeting and scheduling**
- **push back** — delay (also: resist): *The meeting was pushed back to 4pm.* / *He pushed back on the proposal.*
- **bring forward** — move earlier.
- **call off** — cancel: *They called off the strike.*
- **drop in / drop by** — visit informally.
- **run late / run over** — exceed time: *The meeting ran over.*
- **touch base** — contact briefly (idiom): *Let's touch base on Monday.*
- **catch up (on / with)** — update: *Let's catch up next week.*
- **line up** — arrange: *We've lined up three speakers.*

**J. Communicating**
- **get back to** (three-part) — reply later: *I'll get back to you by Friday.*
- **reach out (to)** — contact: *Feel free to reach out if you have questions.*
- **fill in (on)** — inform: *Let me fill you in on what happened.*
- **speak up** — talk louder or share opinion: *Speak up if you disagree.*
- **run by / run past** — present for feedback: *Let me run this by you.*
- **loop in** — include someone in communication: *Loop in Sarah on the email.*
- **cc/bcc in** — not phrasal.
- **chip in** — contribute (money or ideas): *Everyone chipped in.*

**K. Academic writing**
- **account for** — explain.
- **carry out** — conduct (study).
- **draw on** — use sources.
- **point out** — note.
- **set out** — present clearly: *The paper sets out three arguments.*
- **build on** — develop from earlier work: *This study builds on previous research.*
- **rest on** — be based on: *The argument rests on two assumptions.*
- **hinge on** — depend crucially: *The outcome hinges on funding.*
- **call into question** — challenge (not phrasal but fixed): *The findings call into question earlier assumptions.*
- **bear on** — be relevant to: *These facts bear on the case.*
- **take into account / take account of** — consider.
- **dwell on, touch on, elaborate on** — discuss.
- **turn out / prove** — result.
- **give rise to** — cause.

**L. Concluding**
- **wrap up** — finish: *Let's wrap up the meeting.*
- **sum up** — summarize: *To sum up…*
- **round off** — end neatly: *She rounded off the speech with a joke.*
- **conclude with**, **end with** — not phrasal.
- **come to** — total or reach: *The bill came to $200.*
- **follow up** — next step after conclusion.

### 2.3 Phrasal vs. Latinate: choosing by register

| Situation | Spoken / informal-professional | Formal writing |
|---|---|---|
| Postpone | push back, put off | postpone, defer |
| Cancel | call off | cancel, abandon |
| Reduce | cut back, scale back | reduce, curtail |
| Explain | account for | explain, justify |
| Cause | bring about | cause, effect |
| Examine | look into, go over | investigate, review |
| Eliminate | rule out | exclude, discount |
| Support (claim) | back up | substantiate, corroborate |
| Finish | wrap up | conclude, finalize |
| Approve | sign off on | approve, authorize |
| Contact | get in touch with, reach out | contact |
| Raise | bring up | raise, mention |
| Use | draw on | utilize, employ |
| Include | factor in | incorporate, take into account |

A useful general rule: **spoken and email → phrasal; reports, journals, legal documents → Latinate**. But beware: some phrasal verbs are standard in academic English (*carry out, account for, draw on, build on, rely on, point to, set out*) because no Latinate equivalent sounds natural.

## 3. Why Native Speakers Use Them
Business English is full of phrasal verbs because meetings and emails need verbs that are **short, direct, and flexible**. *Follow up* does the work of "take subsequent action to ascertain progress" in two syllables. A manager who says "Let's circle back and iron out the details" sounds clear and collaborative; one who says "Let us return and eliminate the minor difficulties" sounds stiff.

Academic English uses phrasal verbs more selectively, but strong writers do use them. *This study builds on* and *The argument rests on* are common because they carry the metaphor of **structure** (foundations, support) that organizes an argument.

A caution: some business phrasal verbs are **jargon** (*circle back, ping, loop in, touch base, deep dive, double down, move the needle*). They are normal in many workplaces, but they can sound clichéd or irritating; know them for comprehension, and use them sparingly.

---

## 4. When to Use / When Not To
- **Use** *follow up* in emails: *I'm following up on my last message.* Friendly, professional.
- **Use** *get back to you* when you need time: *I'll get back to you by Thursday.* A promise: keep it.
- **Use** *rule out*, *account for*, *point to*, *bear out* in analytical conversation and writing.
- **Use** *back up* for both supporting a claim and copying files (context makes it clear).
- **Don't** over-pack a sentence with jargon: *"Let's loop in the team, circle back, and ping them to touch base"* sounds like parody.
- **Don't** use *kick off* in very formal writing (use *commence* or *begin*).
- **Don't** say *follow up about* when you mean *follow up on*: the preposition is *on* (or *with* + person).
- **Don't** use *carry out* for casual actions (*carry out a sandwich*).
- **In academic writing, avoid** very informal verbs (*figure out, sort out, iron out, chip in, loop in*) in favor of *determine, resolve, address, contribute*.

## 6. American vs. British Notes
- **figure out** (US dominant) vs. **work out** / **sort out** (UK dominant). All understood.
- **touch base** (US business origin, baseball) is now global but sounds more American.
- **reach out** — US corporate, widely adopted; some British speakers find it overused.
- **circle back** — US business jargon.
- **sort out** (UK: arrange, fix; also *get sorted*) vs. **take care of** (US).
- **ring up** (UK: phone) vs. **call up** (US).
- **post** (UK: mail) / **mail** (US) — related but not phrasal.
- **knock off** (UK: finish work) vs. **clock out** (US).
- **tidy up** (UK) vs. **clean up** (US).
- **put through** (connect a call; UK/US).
- **ring back** (UK) vs. **call back** (US).
- **at the end of the day** and **going forward** are chunks in both varieties.
- **cc someone in** (UK/US) vs. **loop someone in** (US).
- **bring forward / push back**: both use *push back* for delay; *move up* (US) for earlier.
- **wind up** (UK: end up, finish) vs. **wrap up** (US).
- **mark up/down** (prices) is used in both.

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

1. **Wrong preposition with *follow up***
   - ❌ *I'm following up about your request.* / *I'm following up your request.*
   - ✅ *I'm following up on your request.* / *I'm following up with you.*

2. **Wrong object order in *fill in*, *walk through*, *run by***
   - ❌ *Fill in me on the details.* / *Walk through me the process.* / *Run by me it.*
   - ✅ *Fill me in on the details.* / *Walk me through the process.* / *Run it by me.*

3. **Pronoun with separable verbs**
   - ❌ *Rule out it.* / *Point out them.* / *Work out it.*
   - ✅ *Rule it out.* / *Point them out.* / *Work it out.*

4. **Using the infinitive after *get back to* and similar**
   - ❌ *I'll get back to you to confirm.* (acceptable) vs. ❌ *I'll get back to answer you.*
   - ✅ *I'll get back to you with an answer.*
   - ❌ *I look forward to hear from you.* → ✅ *I look forward to hearing from you.*

5. **Confusing *account for* and *take into account***
   - ❌ *We must account for the risks* (when meaning consider) — acceptable but ambiguous.
   - ✅ *We must take the risks into account.* / *This accounts for the delay.* (explains)

6. **Translating *realizar* literally**
   - ❌ *We realized a study.* → ✅ *We carried out a study.* / *conducted a study.*
   - ❌ *The meeting will be realized on Monday.* → ✅ *The meeting will take place / be held on Monday.*

7. **Using *actually* for *actualmente* in updates (false friend)**
   - ❌ *Actually, we are following up.* (meaning currently) → ✅ *Currently, we are following up.*

8. **Using *eventually* for *eventualmente***
   - ❌ *Eventually we might call off the meeting.* (meaning possibly) → ✅ *We might possibly call off the meeting.*
   - *Eventually* = finally, in the end.

9. **Using *assist* or *attend* wrongly**
   - ❌ *I assisted the meeting.* → ✅ *I attended the meeting.*

10. **Using *pretend* for *pretender***
    - ❌ *I pretend to finish by Friday.* → ✅ *I intend to / plan to finish by Friday.*

11. **Mixing *scale back* with *go back***
    - ❌ *We scaled back to the old plan.* → ✅ *We went back to the old plan.* / *We scaled back the plan.*

12. **Leaving out a particle**
    - ❌ *Let's wrap the meeting.* → ✅ *Let's wrap up the meeting.*
    - ❌ *He pointed the mistake.* → ✅ *He pointed out the mistake.*

13. **Stress errors**
    - ❌ *follow-UP* as a verb → ✅ verb: *follow UP* (particle stress); noun: *FOLLOW-up*.

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ "Business English requires Latin-based words to sound professional." — In fact, native professionals use *follow up, sign off on, roll out* all the time. Over-formal verbs make you sound distant.
- ❌ "Phrasal verbs are for speaking; formal writing never uses them." — Academic writing uses *carry out, account for, draw on, build on, rely on, point to, set out* constantly.
- ❌ "More jargon = more professional." — Excess jargon (*circle back, ping, deep dive, synergize*) can reduce clarity. Use clear phrasal verbs, not buzzwords.
- ❌ "I can replace any phrasal verb with a single Latin verb." — Often the Latinate verb has a narrower meaning. *Look into* is not always *investigate*; *work out* is not always *solve*.
- ❌ "Follow up is a noun." — It is both: verb *to follow UP*, noun *a FOLLOW-up*.
- ❌ "If I know the verb and particle, I know the meaning." — Phase 4's central lesson: meaning ranges from literal to opaque (*iron out*, *pan out*, *touch base* are idiomatic).

## 9. Pronunciation Note
1. **Particle stress in verbs:** *follow UP, point OUT, rule OUT, back UP, wrap UP, roll OUT, scale BACK, sort OUT, figure OUT.*
2. **Noun/adjective forms stress the first part:**

| Verb | Noun / adjective |
|---|---|
| to follow UP | a FOLLOW-up |
| to roll OUT | a ROLLout |
| to back UP | a BACKup |
| to kick OFF | a KICKoff |
| to cut BACK | a CUTback |
| to wrap UP | a WRAP-up |
| to set UP | a SETup |
| to call OFF | — |
| to run THROUGH | a RUN-through |
| to walk THROUGH | a WALK-through |
| to break DOWN | a BREAKdown |

3. **Three-part verbs:** stress the first particle: *get BACK to, sign OFF on, live UP to, come UP with*.
4. **Linking and reduction:**
   - *Follow up on it* → /ˈfɑloʊ ˈʌp ɑn ɪt/ → fast: "follow-up-on-it" with a flap.
   - *Get back to you* → /ɡɛt ˈbæk tə jə/ ("get-back-ta-ya"). The /t/ in *get* often flaps in US English; *to you* reduces to /tə jə/.
   - *Sign off on* → /saɪn ˈɔf ɑn/.
   - *Account for* → /əˈkaʊnt fər/ with schwa on *for*. Stress the second syllable of *account*.
5. **Word stress inside the verb:** *ac-COUNT for, fol-LOW up, fig-ure OUT* (stress on the particle, not on *fig-*), *re-PORT back*.
6. **Spanish-speaker traps:**
   - Don't add a vowel before /s/ clusters: *scale* /skeɪl/, not "escale"; *stick to* /stɪk/, not "estick".
   - Final consonant clusters: *rolled out* /roʊld aʊt/ (final /d/ links to *out*), *called off* /kɔld ɔf/.
   - /θ/ in *through, think*; /ð/ in *with*. Practice *walk through*: /wɔk θruː/.
7. **Rhythm:** English is stress-timed (Lesson 1). In "I'll follow up on it with the team," only *FOL-low UP* and *TEAM* get strong stress; the rest compresses.

## 10. Memory Technique

### The project life-cycle story (one chain, 12 verbs)
> *"We **set up** a team, **kicked off** the project, **rolled out** a pilot, **pointed out** the problems, **ironed out** the bugs, **worked out** the costs, **backed up** our claims with data, **signed off on** the plan, **scaled up** production, **followed up** with clients, **wrapped up** the quarter, and **called off** the celebration because we **ran over**."*

Reciting a story is easier than reciting a list. Change details to your own life.

### Function map
Draw a circle with twelve slices (the jobs in Section 1). Put 3–4 verbs in each slice. Review the map daily for a week.

### The particle logic still works
| Particle | Business meaning (extension of physical meaning) |
|---|---|
| **up** | start, complete, increase (*set up, wrap up, scale up*) |
| **down** | reduce, analyze, finalize (*scale down, break down, narrow down*) |
| **out** | reveal, distribute, solve (*point out, roll out, work out*) |
| **off** | start or end (*kick off, sign off, call off*) |
| **back** | reduce, return (*cut back, scale back, get back to*) |
| **on** | continue, base, depend (*follow up on, build on, rely on*) |
| **through** | complete (*see through, walk through, follow through*) |
| **in** | include (*factor in, loop in, fill in*) |

### Chunk by collocation, not by verb
Store full chunks, not single words (Lesson 3):
- *follow up on a request / an email / a lead*
- *rule out the possibility / the option / a delay*
- *account for the difference / the results / 40% of sales*
- *draw on experience / research / sources*
- *carry out research / a survey / an analysis*
- *sign off on a budget / a plan / a design*

### Email template memory
*"Thanks for your message. I'll look into it and get back to you by Friday. In the meantime, could you fill me in on the timeline?"* (four phrasal verbs, one useful template)

### Spaced retrieval cards
Front: *Could you ___ me ___ on the timeline?* → **fill / in**
Front: *We can't ___ ___ the possibility of a delay.* → **rule out**
Front: *Exports ___ ___ 40% of revenue.* → **account for**
Review days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30; then once a month. Say the sentence aloud.

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1:** **follow up on + [request/email/issue]** and **follow up with + [person].** *I'll follow up on that. / I'll follow up with Mark.* The preposition tells you the type of object.

> **Pattern 2:** **verb + someone + particle + on/through/by + thing** for communication verbs: *fill me in on, walk me through, run it by me, bring me up to speed on, brief me on.* The person goes first.

> **Pattern 3:** **X accounts for Y% of Z.** *Exports account for 40% of revenue.* Standard in data descriptions. Compare **make up** (*Women make up 60% of the staff*) and **represent**.

> **Pattern 4:** **rule out + [possibility/option/cause].** Used with *can't/can/cannot*: *We can't rule out a recession.* A key hedging phrase in analysis and journalism.

> **Pattern 5:** **build on / rest on / hinge on / rely on / draw on** — "structure" verbs for academic argument. Object = idea, evidence, assumption, study.

> **Pattern 6:** **scale up/down, ramp up, phase out, cut back, scale back** — a resource-management family. All are used with business objects: costs, production, staff, programs.

> **Pattern 7:** **be + verb-ed + out/up/off** in passive reports: *The program was rolled out in 2023. The contract was signed off. The meeting was called off.* Passive phrasal verbs are frequent in news and reports (Lesson 12).

> **Pattern 8:** **get back to + person + with/about + thing.** *I'll get back to you with the figures.* Never an infinitive.

> **Connection to Lesson 44 (spectrum):** *roll out*, *scale back*, *phase out* sit in the middle (partly literal, partly metaphorical); *iron out*, *pan out*, *touch base* are idiomatic; *hand over*, *back up*, *write down* are transparent.

> **Connection to Lesson 43 (dependent prepositions):** *rely on, depend on, account for, deal with, comply with* belong to both lists. Memorize the preposition with the verb.

> **Connection to Lesson 28 (discourse markers):** *to sum up, to wrap up, to touch on, to move on to* serve as spoken discourse markers in meetings and presentations.

> **Connection to Lesson 18–27 grammar:** phrasal verbs in the passive (Lesson 12), in reported speech (Lesson 22), and in relative clauses (Lesson 21: *the issue we ruled out*), still obey separability.

## 12. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1 — Basic**
- Please **fill in** this form.
- Let's **wrap up** the meeting.
- I'll **find out** and tell you.
- We **set up** a new office.
- Can you **back up** your files?

**Level 2 — Everyday work**
- I'll **follow up** with the client tomorrow.
- She **pointed out** a mistake in the report.
- The meeting was **called off**.
- We need to **work out** a schedule.
- I'll **get back to** you by Friday.

**Level 3 — Professional**
- The company **rolled out** the new system in March.
- We can't **rule out** a delay.
- Please **loop** me **in** on that email.
- Could you **walk** me **through** the budget?
- The director **signed off on** the final design.

**Level 4 — Analytical**
- Exports **account for** nearly half of the revenue.
- The results **bear out** our earlier hypothesis.
- We **narrowed down** the options to two.
- They had to **scale back** their plans after the funding was cut.
- The argument **rests on** one key assumption.

**Level 5 — Native-like**
- "I want to **touch on** one more point before we **wrap up**: we haven't **factored in** shipping costs."
- "She **drew on** a decade of field experience and **built on** earlier research to **work out** a new framework."
- "We **ironed out** most of the issues, but a few still need to be **sorted out** before we can **sign off on** the launch."
- "The pilot **panned out** better than expected, so we're **scaling up** and **phasing out** the old process."
- "The data **point to** a slowdown, but I wouldn't **rule out** a recovery. It all **hinges on** consumer confidence."

**Sample email (read it aloud)**
> Hi Laura,
> Thanks for **reaching out**. I'll **look into** the invoice issue and **get back to you** by Thursday. In the meantime, could you **fill me in on** what happened with the first payment? I'd also like to **run** a couple of ideas **by** you before we **sign off on** the new process.
> Best,
> Camila

**Contrast with similar verbs**
| Idea | Verb | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Solve | work out / figure out / sort out / solve | *work out* = find a solution step by step; *figure out* = understand; *sort out* = organize or fix; *solve* = formal for problems/puzzles |
| Examine | look into / go over / run through / review | *look into* = investigate; *go over* = review in detail; *run through* = quick review |
| Reduce | cut back / scale back / cut down / phase out | *cut back* = spending; *scale back* = ambition or size; *phase out* = gradual stop |
| Cancel | call off / cancel / scrap | *call off* = events/plans; *scrap* = abandon; *cancel* = formal and neutral |
| Contact | get back to / reach out / touch base / get in touch | *get back to* = reply; *reach out* = initiate; *touch base* = brief check-in |
| Support | back up / bear out / support / substantiate | *back up* neutral; *bear out* formal evidence; *substantiate* formal writing |
| Start | kick off / set up / launch / roll out | *kick off* = begin an event; *set up* = establish; *roll out* = gradual release |

**Mini Quiz**
1. *Follow up on* + thing (request, issue); *follow up with* + person.
2. Person in the middle: *walk me through*, *fill me in on*; for *run by*, the thing goes in the middle and the person after: *run it by me*.
3. *Carry out* → conduct; *account for* → explain; *draw on* → use; *point to* → indicate; *rely on* → depend upon.
4. Three-part: *sign off on, live up to, get back to.* (*Rule out* and *narrow down* are two-part.)
5. *Rule out* excludes a possibility; *rule over* means govern.
6. Because *to* is a preposition: *look forward to hearing*.
7. Verb: particle stress (*follow UP*). Noun: first-syllable stress (*FOLLOW-up*).
8. It makes up 40% of the total (a proportion); it can also mean "explains."
9. Too much jargon reduces clarity and sounds clichéd; clear phrasal verbs are better.
10. American: *touch base, circle back, reach out, figure out* (any three). British: *sort out*, *ring back*, *knock off* (any one).
