# Lesson 31 — MAKE vs. DO
### Phase 2: High-Frequency Verb System | Lesson 3 of 8

---

## 0. Where this fits
This lesson directly resolves the classic confusion most Spanish speakers face because Spanish uses a single verb, *hacer*, for both "make" and "do" — English splits this into two genuinely distinct verbs with different core meanings, and Lesson 30 already began building your take-vs-make instinct; this lesson extends that same discrimination skill to make-vs-do.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation

The core distinction: **"make" is about creation/construction** — bringing something into existence that didn't exist before (a product, a decision, a plan, a mess). **"do" is about performing an action/activity/task** — engaging in a process, especially one without a specific physical/tangible end-product. Picture "make" as a factory (producing an output) and "do" as a gym (performing an activity/exertion, the value is in the doing, not in producing a physical object).

---

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2a. MAKE — Creation, Construction, Causing
- **Creating something tangible:** "She made a cake." / "They made a new product."
- **Creating something abstract:** "make a decision," "make a plan," "make an effort," "make progress," "make a mistake," "make an excuse," "make a promise," "make a suggestion," "make an impression," "make a difference," "make sense," "make money," "make a noise/mess"
- **Causative meaning:** "This makes me happy." / "The movie made me cry." (causing a state/reaction — connects to Lesson 29's causative "get," a related but distinct causative structure)

### 2b. DO — Performing an Activity/Task
- **General/unspecified activities:** "What are you doing?" / "I'm not doing anything special."
- **Tasks, chores, work:** "do the dishes," "do the laundry," "do homework," "do a job," "do business," "do exercise," "do research," "do one's best," "do someone a favor," "do the shopping" (note: "go shopping" is also correct and extremely common — a related but distinct expression)
- **Activities/hobbies (often, though not universally — some take "play," covered later in this phase's related-verb notes):** "do yoga," "do karate," "do gymnastics"

---

## 3. The Key Diagnostic: Is There a Tangible/Specific End-Product, or Is It the Activity Itself That Matters?
- If the focus is on the **result/product/outcome** (even an abstract one like a decision or a plan) → **MAKE**
- If the focus is on the **process/activity/task itself**, without necessarily producing a specific tangible output → **DO**

This diagnostic resolves the vast majority of confusion cases, though as with all such heuristics, the ultimate correct answer for any specific collocation is fixed by convention (Lesson 30's core point about collocations being arbitrary/memorized), so the diagnostic is a helpful starting intuition, not an infallible derivation method.

---

## 4. Why Native Speakers Maintain This Split
The make/do distinction lets English communicate something Spanish's single *hacer* doesn't distinguish as clearly: whether an action is fundamentally about *producing/creating* something (make) or *performing/engaging in* an activity (do). This is a genuinely useful semantic distinction that native speakers rely on automatically, and getting it wrong (mixing up make/do) is one of the most immediately noticeable non-native markers in everyday speech, precisely because it's such high-frequency vocabulary.

---

## 5. When Each Matters Most
- **"Make" collocations** — describing decisions, plans, creative output, mistakes, progress, and causative reactions — extremely common in everyday and professional contexts.
- **"Do" collocations** — describing chores, general activities, work/tasks, and favors — equally extremely common, especially in daily routine descriptions.

---

## 6. Frequency & Register
Both "make" and "do" collocations are used constantly across every register — this is core, high-frequency everyday vocabulary, not a formal/informal distinction. Getting the specific collocation right (not "make homework," but "do homework") matters regardless of context.

---

## 7. American vs. British Note
No major systematic differences — make/do collocations function identically in American and British English.

---

## 8. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

| Mistake | Why | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "I need to make my homework." | Direct translation from *hacer la tarea* | "I need to do my homework." |
| "Can you do me a cake?" | Direct translation from *hacerme un pastel* | "Can you make me a cake?" |
| "I do a mistake every time." | Direct translation from *hago un error* | "I make a mistake every time." |
| "She does a lot of money." | Direct translation from *hace mucho dinero* | "She makes a lot of money." |
| "Let's do a decision." | Confusing "do" with "make" for this specific abstract collocation | "Let's make a decision." |
| "I need to make the dishes." | Direct translation from *hacer los platos* | "I need to do the dishes." |

---

## 9. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ "Make and do are basically synonyms with minor stylistic differences." — They encode a genuine semantic distinction (creation/production vs. activity/performance), not just stylistic variation.
- ❌ "You can predict every make/do collocation perfectly using the tangible-product diagnostic." — The diagnostic (Section 3) is a strong starting heuristic, but some collocations are simply fixed by convention and need direct memorization (e.g., "make progress" — an abstract, non-tangible "product," yet still "make," not "do").
- ❌ "Since Spanish uses one verb (*hacer*) for both, there's no real logical system in English either — it's totally random." — There IS a genuine underlying logical pattern (Section 3's diagnostic), even though individual exceptions need to be memorized as fixed chunks on top of that general pattern.

---

## 10. Pronunciation Note
"Make" and "do" collocations are stored and produced as complete chunks (per Lesson 3), typically with natural stress falling on the following noun rather than on "make/do" themselves: "make a DEcision," "do the DIshes" — training this natural stress pattern alongside the correct verb choice reinforces both pieces of the chunk together.

---

## 11. Memory Technique
**The "factory vs. gym" image** from Section 1: MAKE = factory, producing an output (even an abstract one like a decision or an impression). DO = gym, performing an activity/exertion where the value is in the doing, not in a tangible product. When uncertain, ask: "Am I producing/creating something here, or performing/engaging in an activity/task?"

**For memorizing the fixed exceptions:** build a dedicated "Make/Do Collocation List" as two parallel columns (directly extending Lesson 30's take/make contrast-list method) and drill them as a matched set, since the two verbs are so frequently confused specifically because they're learned in opposition to each other.

---

## 12. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern:** MAKE = create/produce (even abstractly: a decision, a plan, an impression, progress) or cause a reaction (make someone happy/angry/sad). DO = perform/engage in an activity, task, or chore, especially where the value lies in the process rather than a tangible resulting product.

---

## 13. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1:**
- "I need to do the laundry." (activity/chore)
- "I need to make dinner." (creating a tangible product)

**Level 2 — abstract "make," non-obvious at first glance:**
- "This news makes a huge difference to our plans." (abstract "creation" of a changed outcome — still MAKE, despite no physical product)

**Level 3 — do as a general, unspecified activity placeholder:**
- "I don't know what to do this weekend." (general, unspecified activity — the classic "do" default for vague/general activities)

**Level 4 — combining make and do correctly in one natural passage:**
- "Before I make any big decisions, I like to do some research, make a pros-and-cons list, and do my best to consider every angle — that way, whatever I decide, I've at least made an effort to do things properly." (three "make," two "do," all correctly matched to their specific collocations)

---

## 14. Related Expressions / Terms
- **Make vs. Do collocation pairs** — this lesson's central practical takeaway, worth building as a standing reference list.
- **Causative make** — "this makes me feel..." (connects to Lesson 29's causative "get" as a related but distinct causative structure).
- **"Do" as a general activity placeholder** — especially useful in vague, unspecified contexts ("what are you doing later?").
