# Lesson 51 — Chunks, Not Words
### Phase 5: Vocabulary as a System | Module 1 of 8

---

## 0. Where this fits
Lesson 3 introduced the big idea: native speakers retrieve **chunks**, not isolated words. Phase 2 (high-frequency verbs) and Phase 4 (phrasal verbs) were already chunk training in disguise. Phase 5 now turns that idea into a **complete vocabulary system**: how to choose what to learn, how to store it, how to retrieve it, and how to make it active.

This lesson is the **operating manual** for Phase 5. Lessons 52–58 supply the content (collocations, synonyms, register, academic and professional vocabulary, idioms). This lesson supplies the **method**, so that what you learn stays learned.

By the end you will be able to:
1. Explain what a "word" actually is, and why knowing a word is not one thing.
2. Recognize and record chunks in six categories.
3. Use a notebook and spaced-retrieval routine that fits your life.
4. Convert **passive** vocabulary (you recognize it) into **active** vocabulary (you produce it).

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
Imagine two people entering a kitchen. One has a list of 3,000 ingredients, each defined in a dictionary. The other has fifty **recipes** memorized, with the ingredients that go together and the order they go in. Who cooks dinner faster?

Your vocabulary works the same way. A learner with 8,000 isolated words still stumbles, because every sentence must be built from scratch. A native speaker with a similar number of *words* has tens of thousands of **ready-made combinations**: *make a decision*, *heavy traffic*, *as a matter of fact*, *it's not worth the hassle*, *I couldn't agree more*.

So the question changes:

- ❌ "How many words do I know?"
- ✅ "How many **usable chunks** can I retrieve, at speed, in the right situation?"

A useful picture: **a word is a LEGO brick; a chunk is a pre-assembled piece.** You can still build from bricks, but fluent speech is mostly assembled from pieces.

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 What is "a word"?
Linguists distinguish several things that learners lump together:

| Concept | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Word form** | the spelling/sound | *run* |
| **Lemma** | the base form with its inflections | run, runs, ran, running |
| **Word family** | lemma plus derived forms | run, runner, runny, rerun |
| **Lexical item** | any unit that works as a single vocabulary entry; may be several words | *run out of*, *a runny nose*, *in the long run* |

The last one is the key. The dictionary lists *words*, but the mental lexicon stores **lexical items**, and many have more than one word. *Run out of* is a single lexical item with its own meaning; it is not "run" + "out" + "of."

### 2.2 What does it mean to "know" a word?
Nation's well-known framework (building on earlier work) says knowing a word has many dimensions. For one word, such as **"commit"**, you may know some of these and not others:

| Dimension | Question | Example with *commit* |
|---|---|---|
| **Form (spoken)** | How is it pronounced? | /kəˈmɪt/, stress on 2nd syllable |
| **Form (written)** | How is it spelled? | commit → committed (double t) |
| **Parts** | What affixes does it take? | commitment, committee, uncommitted |
| **Meaning** | What does it mean? | promise / dedicate; also carry out (a crime) |
| **Concept & referents** | Where does it apply? | people, plans, crimes, resources |
| **Associations** | What words relate? | promise, dedicate, pledge, engage |
| **Grammar** | What patterns does it take? | *commit to + noun/-ing*; *commit a crime*; *be committed to* |
| **Collocations** | What goes with it? | *commit suicide / a crime / yourself / to memory* |
| **Constraints** | Register, frequency, taboo? | neutral-formal; "commit suicide" is increasingly replaced by "die by suicide" in sensitive contexts |

**Most learners stop at "meaning."** That explains why you can understand a text perfectly and still write *"I committed an error"* (✅ natural: *I made a mistake*) or *"commit to do"* (✅ *commit to doing*).

### 2.3 Receptive vs. productive knowledge
- **Receptive (passive):** you recognize the item when you read or hear it.
- **Productive (active):** you can retrieve and use it, with correct form, when you speak or write.

Typically, receptive vocabulary is **much larger** than productive. The distance between them is where most advanced learners live. Closing that gap is the goal of this phase. It does not close by reading more; it closes by **retrieving and producing** (see Section 10).

### 2.4 Types of chunks (a working taxonomy)
Lesson 3 gave five types. For daily use, expand to six working categories:

| # | Type | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Collocations** | words that habitually co-occur; flexible slots | *make progress, heavy rain, deeply concerned, a sharp decline* |
| 2 | **Fixed phrases / lexical bundles** | 3–5 words, high frequency, almost no variation | *as a result of, at the end of the day, in the case of, on the other hand* |
| 3 | **Phrasal & prepositional verbs** | verb + particle(s) | *carry out, look into, depend on, get away with* |
| 4 | **Idioms** | meaning not predictable from the parts | *break the ice, a blessing in disguise, the ball is in your court* |
| 5 | **Sentence frames / "slot" patterns** | a skeleton with a gap | *It's not so much X as Y; the more X, the more Y; I wouldn't go so far as to say…* |
| 6 | **Pragmatic routines** | fixed formulas for social functions | *How's it going? / No worries. / Would you mind if I…? / Sorry to bother you* |

**Why type 5 matters most for you:** frames are the highest-leverage chunks for advanced learners because they combine **fixed structure** with **free content**. One frame, learned well, produces hundreds of sentences. We will collect frames systematically in Lessons 52–58.

### 2.5 Where the evidence comes from
- **Corpus linguistics:** large databases of real text show that language is far less "free" than grammar books suggest. Words come with strong *preferences* for partners (collocational preference), for grammatical patterns (colligation), and for meanings (semantic prosody).
- **Processing studies:** native speakers read and recognize multi-word units faster than matched, non-formulaic strings of the same length.
- **Acquisition studies:** learners who notice and store chunks produce more fluent and more accurate speech than learners who learn single words and rules.

(You do not need to remember the names. You need to remember the practical consequences, which follow.)

### 2.6 Three hidden properties of words

**(a) Collocation:** which words come together. *Strong* tea, not *powerful* tea. *Powerful* engine, not *strong* engine (though *strong engine* is understandable). Native speakers feel the gap instantly.

**(b) Colligation:** which *grammar* a word prefers. *Suggest* takes *that*-clauses and *-ing*, but not *to + infinitive* with a person: ❌ *He suggested me to go* → ✅ *He suggested that I go / suggested going.*

**(c) Semantic prosody:** the "mood" a word picks up from its typical company. *Cause* is usually followed by negative things (*cause problems, cause damage, cause trouble*). *Provide* is neutral-to-positive (*provide support, provide an opportunity*). *Happen* collocates with unexpected or unwelcome events (*accidents happen*). *Set in* (Lesson 48) is used mainly with unpleasant things (*winter set in, doubt set in, rot set in*). Using a word against its prosody sounds subtly "off" even when grammatical: ❌ *This vitamin causes better health* → ✅ *This vitamin leads to / promotes better health.*

## 3. Why Native Speakers Do This
Because it is cheaper. Retrieval of a stored chunk costs less working memory than assembly, and working memory is the bottleneck of real-time speech. When a native speaker says *"I'll get back to you on that,"* no assembly takes place: the whole unit is retrieved and the speaker's attention is free for tone, timing, and listening.

There is a second reason: **social signalling**. Using the expected chunk tells listeners that you belong to the speech community. Using a technically correct but unexpected combination (*"I took a decision after a long reflection"*) marks you as an outsider, even if everyone understands you perfectly. Chunks carry membership.

The practical corollary for your learning plan: **study the unit that natives actually store.** When you meet a new word, do not ask "what does it mean?" but "what does it come with?"

## 4. When to Use / When Not To

**Use chunk-based learning when:**
- You want to speak or write more fluently and naturally.
- You keep making "grammatically fine but weird" sentences.
- You hesitate mid-sentence because you are assembling.
- You want vocabulary to stay in memory longer.

**Be careful when:**
- You memorize a chunk without understanding its **register** or **context** (a chunk that works in an email may be wrong in a toast). Always record context.
- You treat chunks as rigid. Many have **slots**: *make a [decision/choice/mistake/point]*. Learn the slot range.
- You overuse a favorite chunk. Natives vary expressions; rotate.
- You learn idioms before collocations. Idioms are low-frequency and high-risk; collocations are high-frequency and low-risk. Prioritize collocations (Lessons 52–53) before idioms (Lesson 58).

**When single words are fine:**
- Technical terms with a single precise meaning (*mitochondria, amortization*).
- Concrete nouns (*bridge, ladder*).
- Reading-only vocabulary you will never need to produce.

## 5. Frequency & Register

**Frequency strategy: learn by band.**
Word frequency follows a steep curve: a small number of words cover most text.

| Band | Approx. word families | Text coverage | Practical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Top 2,000 | high-frequency | ~80–85% of everyday text | You know these; now deepen them with chunks |
| 2,000–5,000 | mid-frequency | adds ~10% | The crucial band for B2→C1 |
| 5,000–9,000 | lower-mid | adds ~3–4% | Reading and professional growth |
| 9,000+ | low-frequency | small | Learn only as needed (specialist fields) |

**Key insight for you:** you probably *recognize* most of the top 5,000 families, but your **control of their chunks** is incomplete. That is where depth beats breadth. Studying *deeply* the 2,000 most frequent words (every collocation, every phrasal verb, every pattern) will improve your English more than learning 2,000 new rare words.

**Register of chunks:** chunks are strongly register-bound.
- Spoken/informal: *no worries, kind of, you know, to be honest, I mean*
- Neutral: *make a decision, take place, deal with*
- Formal/academic: *it should be noted that, in accordance with, with regard to, a growing body of evidence*

Record register every time (see the notebook template in Section 10).

## 6. American vs. British Notes
Chunks differ regionally more than single words do:

| Meaning | US | UK |
|---|---|---|
| Make a call | *give someone a call / make a call* | *give someone a ring / a bell / a buzz* |
| Wait a moment | *hold on* | *hang on* |
| Reasonably good | *pretty good* | *quite good / not bad / fair enough* |
| Quickly | *in a hurry* | *in a rush / flat out* |
| Used to describe a weekend day | *on the weekend* | *at the weekend* |
| Decide | *make a decision* | *take a decision* (also common) |
| Take a break | *take a break* | *have a break / take a breather* |
| Go to the university | *go to college / go to school* | *go to university / go up* |
| Spend a long time | *a ton of time* | *ages / yonks* |
| Agree | *You bet / Sure thing* | *Quite / Absolutely / Fair enough* |
| Thanks | *You're welcome / No problem / Sure* | *Cheers / No worries / You're alright* |
| I'm sorry / pardon | *Excuse me? / Sorry?* | *Pardon? / Sorry?* |
| Casual greeting | *What's up? / How's it going?* | *All right? / You alright?* |

**Practical advice:** pick one variety as your *active* model (say American, since you will probably work with US-oriented material) and treat the other as *receptive*. Mixing is fine in practice; just don't fear it. Record the label (US/UK) in your notebook when you notice it.

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

1. **Word-for-word collocations (the biggest source)**
   - ❌ *I did a mistake.* (hacer un error) → ✅ *I made a mistake.*
   - ❌ *She has a big fear.* (tiene mucho miedo) → ✅ *She's very afraid / She has a deep fear.*
   - ❌ *I want to take a decision.* (UK: acceptable) → ✅ *make a decision* (US).
   - ❌ *Make attention.* (prestar atención) → ✅ *Pay attention.*
   - ❌ *Take a photo of me* is fine, but ❌ *do a photo* → ✅ *take a photo.*
   - ❌ *Make a party / do a party* → ✅ *throw / have a party.*
   - ❌ *Do a question* → ✅ *ask a question.*
   - ❌ *Win money* (ganar dinero) → ✅ *earn / make money* (*win* = a prize).
   - ❌ *Pass the time* is fine; ❌ *pass a good time* → ✅ *have a good time.*
   - ❌ *Strong rain* → ✅ *heavy rain.*
   - ❌ *Big traffic* → ✅ *heavy traffic.*

2. **Over-reliance on direct translation of Spanish chunks**
   - ❌ *I have 25 years* → ✅ *I'm 25.*
   - ❌ *It's very cold today, no?* → ✅ *It's really cold today, isn't it?* (tag question).
   - ❌ *By the way of* / *in the way of* → ✅ *by way of* / *on the way to.*
   - ❌ *In the other hand* → ✅ *On the other hand.*
   - ❌ *Of course yes* → ✅ *Of course.* / *Yes, of course.*
   - ❌ *Be agree* → ✅ *agree* (no *be*). *I agree.*

3. **Learning a verb without its preposition**
   - ❌ *depend of*, *consist in* (meaning "be made of"), *interested on*, *married with*, *dream with* → ✅ *depend on, consist of, interested in, married to, dream of/about.* (see Lesson 43)

4. **Using a word in the wrong register**
   - ❌ *Hey professor, I demand an extension.* (translated directness) → ✅ *Hi Professor, would it be possible to have an extension?*
   - Writing *Dear Sir, I want to inform you that…* in an email to a colleague. Stiff.

5. **Collecting words in lists**
   - Spanish-medium courses often supply word lists with one-word translations. This builds *recognition* (word → translation) and weakens *production* (situation → chunk). The notebook method below corrects this.

6. **Trusting a bilingual dictionary alone**
   - Dictionary translations list equivalents but rarely show collocations. Use a **learner's dictionary** or collocation dictionary (e.g., Oxford Collocations, Longman, Cambridge, Macmillan) and check examples.

7. **Pronouncing chunks as separate words**
   - ❌ *a-s fa-r a-s I k-now* (each word stressed) → ✅ /əz ˈfɑːr əz aɪ ˈnoʊ/ with weak forms. A chunk has a **melody**; learn it as one.

8. **Fear of "wrong" chunks leading to avoidance**
   - Learners avoid an unknown collocation and use a simpler one. This keeps English "correct but plain." Take small risks, then check.

## 8. Common Misconceptions

- ❌ **"A big vocabulary means knowing many words."** — It means knowing the words you have in *depth* (collocations, patterns, register) and being able to retrieve them quickly.
- ❌ **"Synonyms are interchangeable."** — Almost never. *Big, large, great, huge, major* all differ in what they collocate with (*big mistake, large number, great importance, huge success, major problem*). Lesson 54 covers this.
- ❌ **"Memorizing idioms is the key to native-like speech."** — Idioms are the *tip*. Collocations and bundles are the *iceberg*. Natives use far more collocations than idioms.
- ❌ **"If I understand it when reading, I know it."** — Recognition is not production. Test yourself by producing.
- ❌ **"I should avoid translation completely."** — Lesson 4 said: *replace* the translation habit, don't *suppress* it. Spanish can be a bridge **if** you attach the English chunk, not a word, to the Spanish idea.
- ❌ **"Chunks are rigid and can't be adapted."** — Many chunks have *slots*, and natives adapt them constantly: *It's no big deal / It's no big surprise / It's no great loss.*
- ❌ **"I need to learn thousands of new words before I'm advanced."** — For B2+/C1, depth in the top 5,000 families is far more valuable than breadth.
- ❌ **"Flashcards with one word per card are the best method."** — Single-word cards test recognition. Cards with **chunks and gaps** test production.
- ❌ **"Reading a lot automatically fixes vocabulary."** — Reading builds receptive knowledge slowly; *noticing + retrieval + use* makes it productive.

## 9. Pronunciation Note
Chunks are pronounced as **single rhythmic units**. Three rules help.

**1. Weak forms (function words reduce).**
Function words in chunks lose their full vowels:
- *as far as I know* → /əz ˈfɑr əz aɪ ˈnoʊ/ (stressed: FAR, KNOW)
- *a lot of* → /ə ˈlɑɾ əv/ ("a lotta")
- *kind of* → /ˈkaɪndə/ ("kinda")
- *going to* → /ˈɡʌnə/ ("gonna")
- *want to* → "wanna", *have to* → "hafta", *got to* → "gotta"
These are normal speech, not sloppiness. Understand them for listening; produce them when speaking informally.

**2. Stress the content words, compress the rest (stress-timing, Lesson 1).**
In *"It's a matter of time,"* the stressed syllables are MAT and TIME. The other syllables are squeezed between them.

**3. Linking and elision.**
- Consonant + vowel links: *take it out* → "tay-ki-tout."
- Repeated consonants merge: *last time* → /læs taɪm/ (the /t/ at the end of *last* often disappears).
- /t/, /d/ + /j/ fuse: *did you* → "didja"; *would you* → "wouldja"; *what you* → "whatcha."

**Practical method: shadowing chunks.** Choose 5 chunks. Listen to a native model three times, then say the chunk *with* the recording (shadowing), copying stress, rhythm, and links. Record yourself and compare. Repeat daily for a week. This is the pronunciation equivalent of spaced repetition.

**Spanish-speaker traps:**
- Pronouncing every syllable with equal length (syllable-timed rhythm) breaks chunk melody.
- Adding /e/ before /s/ clusters (*"estudy"*, *"especial"*): say *study, special.*
- Pronouncing final consonants too weakly (or too strongly): *worked* /wɜːrkt/, not "work-ed."

## 10. Memory Technique — The Chunk System
This is the heart of the lesson. It has five parts: **Capture → Record → Review → Produce → Recycle.**

### 10.1 Capture: notice, don't just read
Your brain only stores what it notices. Train a **noticing habit**:
- When reading or listening, pause on any combination that sounds "natural" or surprises you.
- Use the question from Lesson 3: *"Is there a chunk I should have retrieved here?"*
- Set a daily quota: **5 chunks** (not 20). Quality over volume.

### 10.2 Record: the notebook template
For each chunk, write **one line** (or one spreadsheet row) with these fields:

| Field | What to write | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Chunk** | the whole unit, with slot if relevant | *come to a conclusion* |
| **Gist** | meaning in English (and Spanish only if needed) | reach a final opinion |
| **Context sentence** | a real sentence, ideally from your own life | *After weeks of research, we came to the conclusion that the product wasn't viable.* |
| **Register / variety** | informal / neutral / formal; US/UK | neutral-formal |
| **Related** | other chunks with the same slot | *reach a conclusion, draw a conclusion, jump to conclusions* |
| **Trap** | the Spanish-influenced error to avoid | ❌ *arrive to a conclusion* |

**Tips**
- Write the chunk *in a sentence you could really say.* Personal sentences are remembered better than textbook ones (self-reference effect).
- Include the **preposition**.
- Record **one meaning at a time**, never five.
- Organize by *topic* or *verb*, not alphabetically. Alphabetical order creates interference; topic clusters create networks (Section 10.5).

### 10.3 Review: spaced retrieval, not re-reading
The evidence is strong: **testing yourself** beats re-reading, and **spacing** beats cramming. Combine both.

**The Leitner box (paper or digital):**
- Box 1: new items, reviewed **daily**
- Box 2: reviewed every **3 days**
- Box 3: reviewed every **week**
- Box 4: reviewed every **2 weeks**
- Box 5: reviewed every **month**

Rule: **correct → move up one box; wrong → back to Box 1.**

**Flashcard design (this is critical):**
- ❌ Front: *come to a conclusion* / Back: *llegar a una conclusión* (tests recognition)
- ✅ Front: *After weeks of research, we ___ ___ ___ ___ that the product wasn't viable.* / Back: *came to the conclusion* (tests production in context)
- ✅ Front: (Spanish idea or a situation) *You've finished thinking about it and you have an opinion. Say it in English with "conclusion".* / Back: chunk.

Use an app that supports spacing (Anki and similar) or a simple spreadsheet with "next review" dates. Keep sessions short (10–15 minutes).

**The 24-hour rule:** the first review should happen the **next day**; the biggest forgetting happens in the first 24 hours.

### 10.4 Produce: turn passive into active
Retrieval from a card is not enough. Chunks become active only when **you use them in real communication**.

| Method | How |
|---|---|
| **Daily 3-sentence use** | At night, write three sentences using today's chunks about your own day. |
| **Speak-aloud** | Record a 60-second voice note using 3–5 chunks from this week. |
| **Chunk-of-the-day challenge** | Pick one chunk; use it in a real conversation, message, or email before bed. |
| **Retell** | After reading an article, retell it aloud using at least five chunks from it. |
| **Substitution drill** | Take a sentence you wrote and upgrade 3 plain words to chunks (see Section 14). |
| **Conversation partner / tutor** | Ask them to *prompt* you with situations that need the chunks. |

The **generation effect** says that producing an answer (even with errors, followed by feedback) beats re-reading the correct one.

### 10.5 Recycle: build networks
Isolated memories decay; connected memories survive. Link chunks into **networks**:

- **By verb:** make → *make a decision, make progress, make sense, make an effort, make a difference*
- **By noun:** decision → *make / reach / take / postpone / reverse a decision; a tough / snap / final decision*
- **By topic:** meetings → *call a meeting, chair a meeting, adjourn, minutes, agenda, item on the agenda*
- **By function:** disagreeing politely → *I see your point, but… / I'm not sure I'd go that far / With respect…*
- **By frame:** *the more X, the more Y* → *the more you practice, the better you get*

Draw a **word web** (mind map) for one hub per week.

### 10.6 Use learner tools
- **Collocation dictionaries** (e.g., Oxford Collocations) show what goes with a word.
- **Corpus tools** (free ones like the Corpus of Contemporary American English, British National Corpus interfaces, or Sketch Engine's free options) let you type a word and see real combinations. Search "make a decision" vs. "take a decision" and compare frequency.
- **Subtitles + shadowing:** watch a series in English with English subtitles; pause on chunks.
- **Search engine trick:** put a phrase in quotation marks and compare result counts or examples to check whether it is idiomatic. Treat this as a hint, not proof.

### 10.7 The 5-minute daily routine (sustainable version)
1. **Capture** (during the day): 5 chunks in your notes app.
2. **Review** (5 min): Leitner/Anki cards due today.
3. **Produce** (5 min): 3 sentences or a 60-second recording.
4. **Weekly** (20 min): review the week's chunks, draw one word web, throw away items you already know.

Consistency beats intensity. **Fifteen minutes a day beats two hours on Sunday.**

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1: Learn the unit, not the part.** When you meet a word, record at least a 2–4 word unit containing it. *"Decision"* → *make a decision.* *"Argue"* → *argue about / argue with.*

> **Pattern 2: Verb + noun collocations are your biggest error zone.** Check every verb that Spanish translates with *hacer, tener, dar, tomar, poner, pasar* (Phase 2). Each maps to many English verbs (*make/do/have/take/give/put/spend/pass*).

> **Pattern 3: Slot + frame learning.** Learn frames with blanks, then fill them with different content: *It turns out that ___. / The thing is, ___. / What I'm getting at is ___.*

> **Pattern 4: Prosody check.** Before using a verb with a noun, ask: does this verb usually go with good or bad things? (*cause, bring about, suffer from, face* → negative; *enjoy, benefit from, achieve* → positive.)

> **Pattern 5: Same meaning, different company.** For a concept such as "very," collect the adverbs that go with each adjective: *deeply concerned, highly unlikely, bitterly cold, fully aware, utterly ridiculous, widely known* (Lesson 53).

> **Pattern 6: Retrieve before you re-read.** Always try to produce a chunk *before* you check it. The struggle is the learning.

> **Pattern 7: One context → many chunks.** Organize by real situations: ordering food, giving feedback, disagreeing, apologizing, small talk, negotiating. A situation cues retrieval later when you need it.

> **Connection to Lesson 3:** Lesson 3 gave the *why* (cognitive load, formulaic language). This lesson gives the *how* (capture, record, review, produce, recycle).

> **Connection to Lesson 4:** The Spanish-to-English "bridge" is allowed if the bridge ends in an English *chunk*, not a word. *Tomar una decisión* → *make a decision* (store as a unit).

> **Connection to Phase 2 (core verbs) and Phase 4 (phrasal verbs):** These were the first two big chunk families. Phase 5 extends the method to all of vocabulary.

> **Connection to Lesson 43 (dependent prepositions):** A dependent preposition is the simplest chunk: *depend on, interested in, good at.* Treat each as one item.

> **Connection to Lesson 28 (discourse markers):** discourse markers are fixed phrases that organize speech. They are chunks of type 2/6 and benefit from the same system.

## 12. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1 — Replace the word-by-word version with a chunk**
- ❌ *I did a mistake.* → ✅ *I made a mistake.*
- ❌ *Make attention, please.* → ✅ *Pay attention, please.*
- ❌ *Strong rain* → ✅ *Heavy rain*
- ❌ *We have to take a decision.* → ✅ *We have to make a decision.*

**Level 2 — Everyday chunks**
- *It's up to you.*
- *I'll give it a try.*
- *That makes sense.*
- *Take your time.*
- *I couldn't agree more.*

**Level 3 — Work and study**
- *Let's touch base next week.*
- *I'd like to draw your attention to…*
- *We're running out of time.*
- *It's worth bearing in mind that…*
- *As far as I'm concerned, the deadline is realistic.*

**Level 4 — Frames**
- *It's not so much that he's lazy as that he's afraid of failing.*
- *The more I think about it, the less sure I am.*
- *I wouldn't go so far as to say it's a failure, but it's certainly a setback.*
- *What strikes me is how quickly things have changed.*

**Level 5 — Native-like control (collocation + prosody + register)**
- "There is mounting evidence that the policy has had **unintended consequences**."
- "The report **raises serious concerns** about data privacy."
- "She **holds strong views** on the issue, but she's **open to persuasion**."
- "The company is **under growing pressure** to **come clean** about the breach."
- "Talks **broke down** after both sides **dug in their heels**."

**The same message: word-by-word vs. chunked**
> ❌ *"I want to inform you that I took the decision of not to continue with the project because I have a big preoccupation about the time."*
> ✅ *"I wanted to let you know that I've decided not to go ahead with the project. I'm concerned about the timeline."*

Notice the upgrades: *let you know* (not *inform you that I want*), *decided* (verb, not *took the decision*), *go ahead with* (phrasal), *concerned about* (not *preoccupation*, a false friend), *timeline* (compact noun).

**Sample notebook entries (use this style)**

| Chunk | Gist | Context sentence | Register | Related | Trap |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *run out of* + noun | have none left | *We ran out of coffee this morning.* | neutral | *run low on, use up* | ❌ *finish the coffee* (when none remains) |
| *come to a conclusion* | reach an opinion | *We came to the conclusion that it wasn't viable.* | neutral-formal | *draw a conclusion* | ❌ *arrive to a conclusion* |
| *be on the same page* | share understanding | *Let's make sure we're all on the same page.* | informal-business | *see eye to eye* | ❌ *in the same page* |
| *the more…, the more…* | proportional frame | *The more I practice, the more confident I feel.* | neutral | *the sooner, the better* | ❌ *more… more…* (missing *the*) |

## 13. Collocations & Related Expressions

**Core vocabulary of vocabulary-learning (useful chunks about this lesson itself)**
- **build / expand / enlarge** your vocabulary
- **pick up** words and phrases
- **come across** a new expression
- **look up** a word / **check** a collocation
- **commit** something to memory / **learn** by heart
- **have a good command of** English / **have a wide range of** vocabulary
- **make a note of** a phrase
- **go over / review** your notes
- **retain / retrieve** information
- **it slipped my mind** / **it's on the tip of my tongue**
- **rack your brains**
- **ring a bell** (sound familiar)
- **learn the hard way**
- **a steep learning curve**
- **bear in mind / keep in mind**
- **get the hang of** (learn how to do)
- **pick it up quickly / slowly**

**Related terms (linguistics)**
- **Lexical chunk / formulaic sequence:** multi-word unit stored as one.
- **Collocation:** habitual word partnership.
- **Colligation:** word + preferred grammar pattern.
- **Semantic prosody:** the positive/negative "aura" a word acquires from its company.
- **Lexical bundle:** frequent 3–5 word sequence.
- **Word family:** base + derived forms.
- **Receptive / productive vocabulary:** recognition vs. use.
- **Depth vs. breadth:** how well vs. how many.
- **Noticing:** consciously registering a form (Schmidt).
- **Retrieval practice / testing effect:** recall strengthens memory more than re-study.
- **Spacing effect:** distributed review beats massed review.
- **Generation effect:** self-produced items are remembered better.
- **Interleaving:** mixing topics during practice improves discrimination.

**Contrast: ways to say "learn a word"**
| Expression | Nuance |
|---|---|
| *pick up* | acquire casually, without trying |
| *learn by heart / memorize* | deliberately commit to memory |
| *get the hang of* | learn how to use something |
| *master* | reach high skill |
| *brush up on* | refresh knowledge you once had |
| *come across* | meet by chance |
