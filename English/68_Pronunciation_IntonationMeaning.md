# Lesson 68 — Intonation and Meaning
### Phase 7: Pronunciation & the Sound System | Module 5 of 5 (Phase Capstone)

---

## 0. Where this fits
You now have the sounds (Lesson 64), the stress (65), the rhythm (66), and the glue (67). The last layer is the **melody**: the rise and fall of your voice across a sentence.

Intonation is not decoration. In English it carries **meaning that words do not**: whether you are asking or telling, finished or continuing, certain or doubtful, warm or cold, polite or abrupt. The same words, *Really*, can express interest, doubt, boredom, or shock, depending only on the pitch.

Connections:
- **Lesson 65:** the *nuclear stress* (the main stressed syllable) is exactly where the pitch movement happens.
- **Lesson 66:** thought groups and pauses; each group has its own tune.
- **Lesson 67:** the chunks that the melody sits on.
- **Lessons 59–63:** hedging, agreement, small talk, and turn-taking all depend on tune (a polite *Well, I'm not sure* with the wrong melody can sound rude).
- **Phase 8:** listening and speaking fluency, where you will use all five layers together.

> **Core idea:** English intonation is built from a small number of **tunes** attached to the **nuclear syllable** of each thought group. Choose the right tune and you control how your sentence is received.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
Say "Yes" five ways:
1. **Yes.** ↘ (a plain answer, finished)
2. **Yes?** ↗ (Are you sure? / Is that you?)
3. **Yes!** ↗↘ (excited, delighted)
4. **Yes...** ↘↗ (well, yes, but...)
5. **Yeeees.** → (bored, reluctant, flat)

The word never changed. The **melody** did, and so did the message.

A second image: **words are the lyrics; intonation is the tune.** A song with the right lyrics and the wrong tune sounds odd. A speaker with the right words and a flat or mismatched tune may sound bored, unfriendly, or confusing, even when the grammar is perfect.

A third image: **the voice has a "home" level**. Falling means "landing": I am done, certain, closing. Rising means "still in the air": I am asking, continuing, or inviting you to respond.

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 What intonation is
Intonation is the **pattern of pitch change** (high to low, low to high) across an utterance. Pitch is produced by the speed of vibration of the vocal folds. Speakers use:
- **Pitch direction:** falls, rises, combinations.
- **Pitch range (span):** narrow (calm, bored, serious) or wide (excited, engaged, surprised).
- **Pitch level (key):** high (new topic, surprise) or low (afterthought, finality).

### 2.2 The structure of an intonation unit
Each **thought group** (Lesson 65) has a shape:

| Part | What it is | Example (CAPS = stress) |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-head** | unstressed syllables at the start | *I'd* |
| **Head** | first stressed syllable up to the nucleus | *LIKE a CUP of* |
| **Nucleus** | the **last major stressed syllable** — where the pitch moves | **COF**- |
| **Tail** | anything after the nucleus | *-fee* |

> I'd **LIKE** a **CUP** of **COF**fee.
> The nucleus is **COF-**; the pitch falls from there and runs down the tail *-fee*.

**Rule of thumb:** the pitch movement starts on the nucleus, and the tail follows its direction (falling tail after a fall, rising tail after a rise).

### 2.3 The five basic tunes

| Tune | Symbol | Shape | Basic meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fall** | ↘ | high → low | finished, certain, complete, definite |
| **Rise** | ↗ | low → high | open, unfinished, a question, inviting a response |
| **Fall-rise** | ↘↗ | down then up | reservation, limited agreement, "there's more", tentativeness |
| **Rise-fall** | ↗↘ | up then down | strong feeling: impressed, surprised, enthusiastic, sarcastic |
| **Level** | → | flat | routine, bored, reciting, "I'm not engaged" |

These are tendencies, not laws. Context and facial expression add to the meaning. But learning to **produce the first four on purpose** will transform your English.

### 2.4 Falls: the default for finished information
Use a **fall** for:
- **Statements:** *I live in Bogotá.* ↘
- **Wh-questions (neutral):** *Where do you live?* ↘
- **Commands:** *Sit down.* ↘
- **Exclamations:** *What a day!* ↘
- **Final item in a list:** *…and bread.* ↘
- **Tag confirming what you already believe:** *It's cold, isn't it?* ↘
- **Greetings and goodbyes:** *Good morning.* ↘ *See you tomorrow.* ↘
- **"Thank you"** (sincere): *Thank you.* ↘

A fall signals confidence and closure. A fall on a question can sound confident, businesslike, or direct.

### 2.5 Rises: open, continuing, or asking
Use a **rise** for:
- **Yes/no questions:** *Are you ready?* ↗ *Do you like coffee?* ↗
- **Non-final items in a list:** *I need eggs ↗, milk ↗, and bread ↘.*
- **Continuation within a longer sentence:** *When I got home ↗, I made dinner ↘.*
- **Checking or confirming something you are not sure about:** *You're Anna's brother?* ↗
- **Echo questions** (repeating to confirm or show surprise): *You lost your keys?* ↗
- **Tag questions asking a real question:** *You're coming, aren't you?* ↗
- **Friendly, open wh-questions** (softening): *What's your name?* ↗ (can sound warm or like a repeated check)
- **Polite requests:** *Could you help me?* ↗

A rise says, "I'm not finished" or "your turn."

### 2.6 Fall-rise: "yes, but..."
The fall-rise ↘↗ is one of English's most useful (and, for learners, least used) tunes. It means:
- **Reservation / limited agreement:** *It's a nice car ↘↗ (but expensive).*
- **Contrast or implication:** *I like him ↘↗ (but I don't trust him).*
- **Politeness / softening a refusal:** *I'm not sure ↘↗.*
- **Warning or reminder:** *Be careful ↘↗.*
- **Gentle correction:** *Well, that's one way to do it ↘↗.*
- **Tentative answer:** *Maybe ↘↗.*

If you say *It's a nice car* with a fall, you have finished: you like it. With a fall-rise, you have **left something unsaid**, and the listener hears the "but."

### 2.7 Rise-fall: strong attitude
The rise-fall ↗↘ expresses:
- **Strong enthusiasm:** *Wonderful!* ↗↘
- **Surprise or being impressed:** *Really? ↗↘ I had no idea.*
- **Sarcasm or irony:** *Oh, great ↗↘.*
- **Certainty with feeling:** *Absolutely ↗↘.*

Use sparingly; too much sounds theatrical.

### 2.8 Level: flat pitch
Level tunes appear when:
- the speaker is bored or tired;
- someone is reading something mechanically;
- a speaker is hesitating (*um...*);
- reciting a known list (counting, numbers).

Flat speech from a learner is often read as "uninterested" or "cold," even when it is just caution. If your pitch range is narrow, you may be sending messages you do not intend.

### 2.9 The same words, different meanings
| Words | Tune | Typical meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Really.** | ↘ | I agree / that's true |
| **Really?** | ↗ | I'm asking for confirmation |
| **Really?!** | ↗↘ (wide) | I'm shocked or amazed |
| **Really...** | ↘↗ | I'm not convinced |
| **Fine.** | ↘ | all right |
| **Fine!** | ↘ (sharp, low) | irritated, ending the discussion |
| **Fine?** | ↗ | Is that okay with you? |
| **Sorry.** | ↘ | apology |
| **Sorry?** | ↗ | Pardon? I didn't hear |
| **Sure.** | ↘ | yes, of course |
| **Sure?** | ↗ | Are you certain? |
| **Okay.** | ↘ | agreed / that's all |
| **Okay?** | ↗ | Do you agree? Are you fine? |
| **Oh.** | ↘ | I see (neutral) |
| **Oh!** | ↗↘ | surprise |
| **Oh...** | ↘↗ | uncertainty / disappointment |

### 2.10 Intonation and grammar
Sometimes intonation alone distinguishes sentence types:
- *You're coming.* ↘ (statement) vs. *You're coming?* ↗ (question)
- *She's late.* ↘ vs. *She's late?* ↗
- *Hello?* ↗ (checking someone's there) vs. *Hello.* ↘ (greeting)

It also marks **boundaries and grouping**:
- *My brother ↗, who lives in Cali ↗, is a doctor ↘.* (extra information, non-restrictive)
- *My brother who lives in Cali is a doctor.* (one tune: restrictive; you may have other brothers)
- *Let's eat, Grandma!* vs. *Let's eat Grandma!* (the pause and tune show the comma)

### 2.11 Intonation and focus
The **nucleus** shows the most important new information (Lesson 65):
- *I didn't take your **BOOK**.* ↘ (not the book, something else)
- *I didn't **TAKE** your book.* ↘ (I borrowed it, perhaps)
- ***I** didn't take your book.* ↘ (someone else did)

English uses **pitch** to mark focus. Spanish tends to use **word order** or special constructions (*Fui yo*, *Fue eso lo que dije*). When you translate that habit, you leave out the pitch and your focus disappears. Practise putting the movement on the word you mean.

### 2.12 Wh-questions and yes/no questions
| Type | Neutral tune | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Wh-question | fall ↘ | normal, direct: *Where are you from?* |
| Wh-question | rise ↗ | friendlier, softer, or repeating: *Where are you from?* |
| Yes/no | rise ↗ | standard: *Are you hungry?* |
| Yes/no | fall ↘ | firm, businesslike, rhetorical, or checking a known fact: *Are you coming.* |
| Alternative question | rise on first, fall on last | *Tea ↗ or coffee ↘?* |
| Question tag (real question) | rise ↗ | *It's open, isn't it?* |
| Question tag (confirming) | fall ↘ | *It's lovely, isn't it?* |

### 2.13 Lists, numbers, and addresses
- **Lists:** rise on each non-final item, fall on the last: *Apples ↗, bananas ↗, oranges ↗, and grapes ↘.*
- **Phone numbers:** rise at the end of each group, fall on the last group: *three-one-oh ↗ five-five-five ↗ one-two-three-four ↘.*
- **Alternatives:** *Do you want to eat in ↗ or take it away ↘?*

### 2.14 Discourse-level intonation
- **New topic:** a higher pitch start ("paragraph beginning").
- **Same topic, continuing:** pitch gradually declines (declination).
- **End of a turn:** low fall — signals the floor is open (Lesson 63).
- **Mid-turn pause with a rise or level tone:** "I'm not done."
- **Parenthetical or aside:** lower and faster.
- **Quoting someone:** often a different pitch range for the voice of the quoted person.

### 2.15 Intonation and politeness
In English, politeness is shaped by tune as much as by words.
- *Could you pass the salt?* ↗ polite, open.
- *Could you pass the salt?* ↘ may sound like an instruction or a complaint.
- *Thanks.* ↘ ordinary; *Thanks!* ↗↘ warm and genuine.
- *Excuse me?* ↗ polite attention-getting; *Excuse me.* ↘ firm, may be irritated.
- *I'm sorry, but I can't.* with a falling-rising *sorry* sounds regretful; a flat *sorry* sounds like a formula.

**Rule:** if you want to be warm, use a **wider range** and a gentle **rise or fall-rise**. If you want to be firm, use a **narrow range** and a **low fall**.

### 2.16 Uptalk (high rising terminal)
Many speakers, especially in the US, Australia, New Zealand, Canada, and some UK regions, end statements with a rise: *I'm from Denver? And I work in marketing?* This is called **uptalk** or **high rising terminal**.
- It can invite the listener's attention ("are you following?") or soften a statement.
- In some contexts it can also sound unsure.
- Some varieties (for example parts of Northern Ireland, Glasgow, and Australia) use rising statements as an ordinary default.
- **For you:** recognize it, don't adopt it as your default. Use falls for statements when you want to sound confident, particularly in interviews and presentations.

### 2.17 Variation by region and speaker
| Feature | US | UK |
|---|---|---|
| Typical pitch range | moderate, more level in many speakers | often wider, with more noticeable falls and fall-rises |
| Fall-rise | less frequent | frequent in polite or tentative speech |
| Uptalk | common in some groups | varies by region |
| Tag questions | used, often falling | very frequent, rising or falling |
| *Right?* / *Yeah?* as tags | frequent | frequent |
| Politeness tunes | more rising on requests | more fall-rise and rise-fall |

Individuals vary widely; treat these as tendencies.

## 3. Why Native Speakers Do This
1. **Grammar is not enough for speech.** Written English has punctuation; spoken English has melody (a question mark you can hear).
2. **Efficiency.** A tune can carry an attitude that would take a whole sentence to say.
3. **Turn-taking.** Falls and rises tell listeners when you are finished (Lesson 63).
4. **Social signaling.** Warm voices have wide ranges; cold ones are flat. Listeners read these cues in a second.
5. **Information structure.** The nuclear tone signals what is new, what is contrasted, what is finished.
6. **Politeness and face.** Tune can soften a refusal or make a request easier to accept without extra words.
7. **Learned unconsciously.** Children acquire the tunes before they learn grammar, so natives hear a wrong tune as "odd" or "rude" more than a wrong word.

## 4. When to Use / When Not To

**Use rich intonation when**
- chatting, small talk, giving opinions, telling stories;
- making requests, apologizing, thanking;
- you want to sound warm, interested, or polite;
- making a point in a meeting or presentation (fall for conclusions, rise to hold attention).

**Use more restrained intonation when**
- giving instructions where clarity matters most;
- speaking formally (legal, technical, ceremonial);
- giving critical or serious news;
- delivering precise information such as numbers, names, addresses.

**Avoid**
- exaggerated "sing-song" tunes for everything;
- rising on every statement (sounds unsure);
- falling on every question (sounds abrupt);
- a flat monotone throughout (sounds bored or robotic);
- copying a regional contour you cannot sustain naturally.

**Rule of thumb:** *statements fall, yes/no questions rise, lists rise-rise-fall, "yes, but" fall-rise, and strong feelings get a wider range.*

---

## 5. Frequency & Priority

**What matters most for you**
1. **Falls on statements and wh-questions** (finished, confident).
2. **Rises on yes/no questions and non-final list items.**
3. **Nuclear stress in the right place** (Lesson 65).
4. **Wider pitch range** in friendly conversation.
5. **Fall-rise** for polite reservation and "yes, but."
6. **Rise-fall** for strong feeling, used sparingly.
7. **Recognition** (not production) of uptalk and regional contours.

**Register**
| Context | Intonation |
|---|---|
| Casual with friends | wide range, many rises and rise-falls |
| Workplace | moderate range, clear falls on conclusions |
| Formal presentation | controlled, clear falls, rises to hold attention |
| Customer service | wide, warm, rising on offers (*Anything else?*) |
| Phone | slightly exaggerated range to compensate for no visual cues |
| Interview | confident falls, pauses, avoid uptalk |

## 6. American vs. British Notes
See 2.17. Additional points:
- British speakers use the fall-rise a lot in polite refusals: *I'm afraid I can't* ↘↗.
- American speakers tend to use rising pitch on requests (*Can you help?* ↗) and a falling tune on "Thanks" to be efficient.
- In both, a **wide range** signals warmth; a **narrow** range signals seriousness or boredom.
- In service encounters, a rising tune on greetings (*Hi, how are you?* ↗) is common and friendly in both.
- **Australian, New Zealand, Canadian, and some US speakers** use uptalk commonly; do not treat it as a mistake in them.
- **Indian, Singaporean, Jamaican, and other World Englishes** have their own intonation systems that work well in local communication; your target variety just needs to be consistent and clear.

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

### 7.1 Tendencies (not every speaker)
| ❌ Habit | Effect | ✅ Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Narrow pitch range (monotone) | sounds bored or cold | widen your range, especially on the nucleus |
| Little or no fall-rise | sounds blunt in refusals and disagreements | add ↘↗ to *I'm not sure*, *maybe*, *that's true, but...* |
| Rising on statements at the end of every sentence | sounds unsure or asking permission | finish with a clear fall |
| Falling on yes/no questions | sounds abrupt or like a demand | rise on the nucleus |
| Using word order or extra words to show focus instead of pitch | focus is lost | put the pitch peak on the focus word |
| Falling on non-final list items | the listener thinks the list has ended | rise on each non-final item |
| Pitch peak on every stressed syllable | sounds like a "chant" | one main movement per thought group |
| Using Spanish tag habit (*¿no?*, *¿verdad?*) with an unchanged tune | tag sounds off | learn *isn't it? / right?* with the right tune |
| Flat "Thank you" | sounds insincere | fall with a little extra range: *Thank you* ↘ |
| Saying "Sorry?" with a fall | sounds like an apology, not a request to repeat | rise |
| Not marking the end of a turn | listeners don't know you're finished | low fall at the end |

(Spanish intonation has its own rich patterns and varies by region. Colombian Spanish, for instance, is often described as melodic. The problem is usually not a lack of melody but that the **English tunes are different** and are tied to **nuclear stress** rather than to the Spanish positions.)

### 7.2 Listening mistakes
| Problem | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| You can't tell if someone is asking or telling | you ignore the tune | listen for the final rise or fall |
| You miss sarcasm | you don't hear rise-fall or flat tune | notice exaggerated range |
| You miss politeness | you don't hear fall-rise | listen for the "but" implied |
| You think a native speaker is angry | low, narrow fall (firm) | check facial expression and context |
| You think a native speaker is unsure | uptalk | consider the speaker's variety |

### 7.3 Attitude and habits
- **"English intonation is too dramatic."** A moderate widening is enough; you don't have to sing.
- **"Intonation can't be learned as an adult."** It can; tunes are patterns like any others.
- **"If the grammar is right, I'm fine."** Grammar is only half the message.
- **"I'll only copy the words."** Copy the melody too (shadowing).

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ **"Questions always go up."** — Wh-questions often fall; yes/no questions usually rise; tags can do either.
- ❌ **"Statements always go down."** — Many end with rises in uptalk varieties; mid-sentence rises are normal.
- ❌ **"Rising intonation means uncertainty."** — It can mean friendliness, openness, or "I'm continuing."
- ❌ **"A flat tone is neutral."** — To native ears it often sounds bored, unfriendly, or reading.
- ❌ **"Intonation is the same in every language."** — The basic ideas overlap, but the meanings of specific tunes differ.
- ❌ **"Intonation is just emotion."** — It also marks grammar, focus, and structure.
- ❌ **"British and American intonation are the same."** — Similar, but with different frequencies and contours.
- ❌ **"Uptalk is wrong."** — It is a common pattern in many varieties.
- ❌ **"A big range is always better."** — Too much sounds theatrical.
- ❌ **"Exact pitch numbers matter."** — Direction and relative height matter.
- ❌ **"It's too late for me to change my intonation."** — Listeners respond to even modest changes.
- ❌ **"Intonation is separate from stress."** — The tune attaches to the stressed nuclear syllable.

## 9. Pronunciation Note: Training Method

### 9.1 Hear the tunes first
Before producing, **listen**: pick a short clip and write an arrow (↗ ↘ ↘↗ ↗↘ →) above every thought group. Compare with a friend or teacher.

### 9.2 Hum it
Replace the words with "mm" or "da-da-DA-da" and reproduce only the melody. Then add the words back.

### 9.3 Exaggerate, then relax
Practice the tunes with a **very wide range** (like reading to a child). Then reduce to 60% for real conversation.

### 9.4 Arrow notation
Mark scripts:
> Are you **READ**y? ↗
> I'm **FINE**. ↘
> It's a **NICE** car ↘↗, but it's **EX**pensive. ↘

### 9.5 Hand movement
Move your hand up or down with the pitch. This uses the body to remember the shape.

### 9.6 Minimal pairs of meaning
Say each pair, changing only the tune:
- *Sure.* ↘ / *Sure?* ↗
- *Really.* ↘ / *Really?* ↗ / *Really?!* ↗↘
- *Sorry.* ↘ / *Sorry?* ↗
- *Fine.* ↘ / *Fine?* ↗
- *Maybe.* ↘ / *Maybe...* ↘↗

### 9.7 Shadowing with tune
Shadow 30 seconds of natural speech. Imitate not only the words but the **melody and pitch range**. Record and compare.

### 9.8 Recording for tone
Record a 60-second self-introduction. Listen for: Is my pitch range narrow? Do I fall at the ends of statements? Do I rise on yes/no questions? Redo, with a wider range.

### 9.9 The "smile" trick
Smiling slightly raises pitch and widens range. It helps you sound warm, especially on the phone.

### 9.10 Trouble spots
- Rising at the end of every statement.
- Falling on every yes/no question.
- Using the same tune for refusals and acceptances.
- Forgetting the fall-rise.
- Putting the movement on the wrong word.

## 10. Memory Technique

### 10.1 The landing metaphor
Falls = landing (done). Rises = still flying (open). Fall-rise = landing with a "but" (the plane takes off again). Rise-fall = fireworks (strong feeling). Level = taxiing (nothing happening).

### 10.2 Question mark you can hear
Imagine you can hear the punctuation: ↘ is a period, ↗ is a question mark, ↘↗ is a comma-with-"but," ↗↘ is an exclamation mark.

### 10.3 Shape a word with your hand
Say *Yes* five ways, moving your hand each time. Keep the five shapes in mind.

### 10.4 List song
*Apples ↗, bananas ↗, oranges ↗, and grapes ↘.* Sing it once and remember the pattern of rise-rise-rise-fall.

### 10.5 The "but" test
After a fall-rise, you can almost always add "but...": *It's nice ↘↗ (but...).* If you can't, you probably need a fall.

### 10.6 Film and series
Watch a favorite scene and notice the arrows. Pick one line a day and copy it in tone, not just in words.

### 10.7 Spaced retrieval cards
- *Statement tune?* → **fall ↘**
- *Yes/no question tune?* → **rise ↗**
- *Neutral wh-question tune?* → **fall ↘**
- *List tune?* → **rise, rise, ... fall**
- *"Yes, but" tune?* → **fall-rise ↘↗**
- *Strong surprise tune?* → **rise-fall ↗↘ (wide)**
- *Flat tune sounds...?* → **bored or cold**
- *Uptalk is...?* → **rising end on statements; recognize it, don't default to it**
Review on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30.

### 10.8 Weekly tune log
Every week, write down five lines you heard that surprised you ("they sounded angry but weren't"), mark the tune with arrows, and note the context.

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1: Finished information → fall.**

> **Pattern 2: Open/asking/continuing → rise.**

> **Pattern 3: "Yes, but" or tentative → fall-rise.**

> **Pattern 4: Strong feeling → rise-fall with wide range.**

> **Pattern 5: Bored or reading → level, narrow range.**

> **Pattern 6: The tune is attached to the nucleus (the last major stressed syllable of the thought group).**

> **Pattern 7: Lists: rise ... rise ... fall.**

> **Pattern 8: Wh-questions fall (neutral); yes/no questions rise.**

> **Pattern 9: Wider range = warmer, more engaged; narrower = serious, formal, or flat.**

> **Pattern 10: Politeness often comes from the tune, not the words.**

> **Pattern 11: Focus is marked by pitch (English), not only by word order (Spanish).**

> **Pattern 12: Each thought group has one main movement.**

## 12. Real-Life Examples

### 12.1 Everyday tunes
| Utterance | Tune | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| I'm from Colombia. | ↘ | statement |
| Are you from Colombia? | ↗ | yes/no question |
| Where in Colombia? | ↘ | neutral wh-question |
| Where in Colombia? | ↗ | friendly interest |
| You're from Colombia? | ↗ | surprise or confirmation |
| It's a good idea ↘↗ | fall-rise | ...but I have concerns |
| That's brilliant! | ↗↘ | strong approval |
| Thanks. | ↘ | ordinary |
| Thanks! | ↗↘ | warm |
| Right. | ↘ | I understand |
| Right? | ↗ | Don't you agree? |
| Okay... | ↘↗ | I'm not sure I like it |

### 12.2 Mini-dialogues with tune marks
**1. Café**
> **A:** Hi, can I help you? ↗
> **B:** Yes, I'd like a large coffee ↘, please.
> **A:** For here ↗ or to go ↘?
> **B:** To go ↘.
> **A:** Anything else ↘?
> **B:** No, that's all ↘. Thanks!

**2. Polite disagreement**
> **A:** I think we should move the deadline.
> **B:** That's an interesting idea ↘↗... but I'm worried about the budget ↘.

**3. Surprise**
> **A:** I got the job.
> **B:** Really?! ↗↘ That's fantastic ↗↘!

**4. Checking**
> **A:** The meeting is at three.
> **B:** At three ↗? I thought it was at four.
> **A:** No, three ↘.

**5. Tag questions**
> **A:** Nice weather, isn't it ↘? (confirming)
> **B:** Yes, lovely.
> **A:** You're coming tonight, aren't you ↗? (real question)
> **B:** I think so.

**6. Customer service**
> **A:** Thank you for calling ↘. How can I help you today ↗?
> **B:** I'd like to change my flight.
> **A:** Of course ↗↘. Let me check that for you ↘.

### 12.3 Focus shifts
Same sentence, different nucleus (CAPS = nuclear syllable):
- **I** asked her to dinner. (not someone else)
- I **ASKED** her to dinner. (I didn't "force" or "tell" her)
- I asked **HER** to dinner. (not someone else)
- I asked her to **DIN**ner. (not lunch)

### 12.4 Reading passage with tunes
> When I **MOVED** to **Bos**ton ↗, I didn't **KNOW** anyone ↘. The first **WEEK** was **HARD** ↘↗, but then I **MET** a **GROUP** of **STU**dents ↘. We **BECAME** friends ↘. And **now** ↗, **I** can't **IM**agine **LIV**ing any**WHERE** else ↘.

### 12.5 Before and after
> ❌ A flat, narrow voice: "Yes. I. Like. It." (reads as unconvinced)
> ✅ "I **REALLY** like it ↗↘!" (warm, wide range)

> ❌ Rising at the end of every sentence in an interview: "I worked in sales? For three years? Then I moved to marketing?"
> ✅ "I worked in sales for three years ↘. Then I moved to marketing ↘."
