# Lesson 75 — Tone, Style and Clarity Editing
### Phase 9: Writing | Module 4 of 4 (Phase Capstone)

---

## 0. Where this fits
Lessons 72–74 taught you three registers: professional email, academic prose, and casual/personal writing. This lesson teaches the skill that makes all three work: **editing**.

Good writers are not people who write perfect first drafts. They are people who **revise well**: they know what to look for, in what order, and how to fix it. For a learner at your level, editing is also where you can catch the "translated" patterns from Lesson 71 and where you have the time that speaking never gives you.

Editing has three targets:
1. **Tone:** how the text *feels* to the reader (polite, cold, confident, pushy, warm).
2. **Style:** how the text *reads* (concise, vivid, heavy, wordy, natural).
3. **Clarity:** whether the reader can understand it *the first time* (no ambiguity, no overload).

Connections:
- **Lesson 59:** hedging and softening are the main tone tools.
- **Lesson 61:** diplomatic disagreement shows up as tone edits.
- **Lesson 71:** the five levels of "translated" are your editing checklist.
- **Lessons 72–74:** email, academic, and personal writing each need different tone and style choices.
- **Lessons 66–68:** reading aloud brings rhythm and intonation into the editing process.
- **Lesson 76 (Phase 10):** cultural pragmatics explains why tone judgments differ across cultures.

> **Core idea:** Write first, edit second, and edit in layers: **purpose → structure → sentences → words → mechanics.** Fix the big things before the small ones, and read as your reader, not as the writer.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
Writing is like **sculpting**. The first draft is a block of stone: the shape is in there somewhere. Editing removes what isn't the sculpture (extra words), adjusts the proportions (structure), and polishes the surface (mechanics).

A second image: **a radio dial.** Every text is tuned to a station: formal or friendly, blunt or gentle, dense or light. Editing tones means *adjusting the dial* until the signal matches the reader and the situation.

A third image: **the reader as a tired stranger.** They don't know what is in your head. Every unclear pronoun, long sentence, or missing link makes them work harder. Editing is making their job easy.

Compare:
> ❌ *In relation to the issue of the delay in the delivery of the documents, it would be necessary that the necessary actions be taken in order to guarantee that the problem does not repeat itself in the future.*
> ✅ *The delivery was late. We need to prevent that from happening again.*

Same message. The second is shorter, clearer, and more direct, and it sounds like a person.

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 The editing process: from big to small
| Pass | Question | Fixes |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Purpose and audience** | What do I want the reader to think/do? Who are they? | goal, scope, level of detail, register |
| **2. Structure** | Is the order logical? Is the main point easy to find? | reorder, merge, split, add headings, front-load |
| **3. Paragraphs** | Does each paragraph have one idea and a clear link? | topic sentences, transitions, cuts |
| **4. Sentences** | Is each sentence clear, varied, and the right length? | split, combine, active voice, subject-verb closeness |
| **5. Words** | Are the words precise, natural, and necessary? | collocations, wordiness, register, false friends |
| **6. Mechanics** | Spelling, grammar, punctuation, formatting, consistency | proofreading |

**Do one pass at a time.** If you fix commas in a paragraph you will later delete, you've wasted effort.

**Practical helpers**
- **Take a break** (even 20 minutes) before editing; you'll see more.
- **Change the view** (print, enlarge the font, change the font): the text looks new.
- **Read aloud** (Lesson 66): your ear catches awkward rhythm and overlong sentences.
- **Reverse outline:** write one phrase per paragraph and check logic.
- **Read backwards**, sentence by sentence, for mechanics.
- **Use a checklist** based on your own recurring errors.

### 2.2 Tone: the emotional temperature of a text
Tone is created by **word choice, sentence structure, directness, politeness strategies, and punctuation.** Readers decide quickly whether a text sounds friendly, neutral, demanding, or hostile, and they rarely give you the benefit of the doubt (no face, no voice).

**The tone dial**
| Dimension | One end | Other end |
|---|---|---|
| Formality | formal | casual |
| Warmth | cold/distant | warm/friendly |
| Directness | blunt | indirect |
| Confidence | tentative | assertive |
| Emotion | neutral | emotional |
| Power | deferential | authoritative |

**Tone tools**
| Tool | Effect | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Modal verbs** | soften requests | *Could you…?* vs. *You must…* |
| **Hedges** | reduce force | *a bit, perhaps, seems, tends to* |
| **Positive framing** | focus on what can be done | *We can finish by Friday* vs. *We can't finish before Friday* |
| **Personal vs. impersonal** | closeness vs. distance | *I'd suggest…* vs. *It is suggested that…* |
| **"We" vs. "you"** | shared responsibility vs. blame | *We missed the deadline* vs. *You missed the deadline* |
| **Appreciation** | builds goodwill | *Thanks for flagging this.* |
| **Imperatives** | commanding | *Send the file.* vs. *Could you send the file?* |
| **Exclamation marks** | enthusiasm or pressure | *Thanks!* (warm) vs. *Please reply!!!* (pushy) |
| **Questions** | invite vs. accuse | *Could you help me understand?* vs. *Why didn't you…?* |
| **Details and specifics** | clarity and respect | *by Friday at 5 pm* |

### 2.3 Common tone problems and fixes
| Problem | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Blunt** | *Send me the report today.* | *Could you send me the report today? Thanks.* |
| **Accusatory** | *You didn't follow the instructions.* | *It looks like a step was missed. Could we go through it together?* |
| **Passive-aggressive** | *As per my last email…* / *Just a gentle reminder that I already asked…* | *Following up on my earlier email, could you let me know…?* |
| **Overly apologetic** | *Sorry, sorry, I'm so sorry to bother you, if it's not too much trouble…* | *Sorry to bother you. Could you…?* |
| **Cold** | *Noted.* | *Thanks, noted. I'll update the plan.* |
| **Pushy** | *I need this ASAP!!!* | *Could you send this by 3 pm today? It's blocking the next step.* |
| **Vague/indirect to the point of confusing** | *It might perhaps be possible that we could look at the issue.* | *Let's review the issue on Monday.* |
| **Overconfident** | *Obviously this is the best option.* | *I think this is the strongest option because…* |
| **Sarcastic** | *Great job, as usual.* | (avoid in writing) |
| **Condescending** | *As you should know…* / *Simply do X.* | *One option is to X.* |
| **Overly emotional** | *I'm absolutely furious!* | *I'm concerned about the delay and would like to discuss it.* |
| **Too casual for context** | *hey, send the contract asap* | *Hi Laura, could you send the contract when you have a moment?* |

**Words and phrases that often carry hidden tone**
| Phrase | Possible reading |
|---|---|
| *As per my last email* | irritation (you didn't read it) |
| *Per our conversation* | neutral-formal; can feel stiff |
| *Just circling back…* | polite chase; can seem passive-aggressive if repeated |
| *No worries* | friendly; can sound sarcastic in context |
| *Thanks in advance* | polite, but presumes compliance |
| *Kindly (send/confirm)* | formal; can sound like an order in some contexts |
| *Please advise* | formal; may sound distant |
| *Going forward…* | can sound like criticism of the past |
| *With all due respect…* | often introduces disrespect |
| *To be honest…* | can imply earlier dishonesty |
| *Obviously / clearly / simply / just* | can sound condescending |
| *Interesting.* | neutral or critical, depending on tone |
| *Fine.* | acceptance, resignation, or irritation |

(Tone is context-dependent; these are risks, not rules.)

### 2.4 Style: concision and strong writing
Style editing removes **clutter** and strengthens **the core message.**

**(a) Cut wordy phrases**
| Wordy | Concise |
|---|---|
| in order to | to |
| due to the fact that | because |
| in spite of the fact that | although |
| at this point in time | now |
| in the event that | if |
| with regard to / in relation to / with reference to | about |
| for the purpose of | for / to |
| it is necessary that | must / should / need to |
| in the near future | soon |
| a large number of | many |
| the majority of | most |
| has the ability to | can |
| make a decision | decide |
| conduct an investigation of | investigate |
| give consideration to | consider |
| take into consideration | consider |
| in the absence of | without |
| prior to / subsequent to | before / after |
| is able to | can |
| at the present time | now / currently |
| on a daily basis | daily |
| each and every | each / every |
| basic fundamentals | fundamentals |
| end result | result |
| completely finished | finished |

**(b) Replace nominalizations ("zombie nouns") with verbs**
| Nominalization | Verb version |
|---|---|
| We made an assessment of the risks. | We assessed the risks. |
| There was an increase in sales. | Sales increased. |
| The implementation of the plan was carried out by the team. | The team implemented the plan. |
| We will provide you with an explanation. | We will explain. |
| The reduction of costs is our objective. | We aim to reduce costs. |

Nominalizations are not forbidden (Lesson 73: they can sound academic), but chains of them are heavy.

**(c) Prefer active voice when the agent matters**
| Passive | Active |
|---|---|
| The report was written by the team. | The team wrote the report. |
| Mistakes were made. | We made mistakes. |
| It was decided that the budget would be cut. | The board decided to cut the budget. |

The passive is right when the agent is unknown, unimportant, or when you want to focus on the object (*The samples were heated to 80°C.*). It can also hide responsibility, which readers notice.

**(d) Choose strong verbs and concrete nouns**
| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| make an improvement | improve |
| is in agreement with | agrees with |
| has an effect on | affects |
| a lot of problems | several problems / many problems / three major problems |
| a thing that helps | a tool, a method, a resource |
| very important | essential / crucial |
| very big | huge / major |

**(e) Eliminate filler and weak intensifiers**
*very, really, quite, rather, basically, essentially, actually, in fact, just, simply, of course, obviously*: remove them unless they add meaning.

**(f) Vary sentence length and structure**
- Short sentences create emphasis.
- Longer sentences develop ideas.
- A run of similar sentences sounds monotonous.

**(g) Parallelism**
| ❌ | ✅ |
|---|---|
| She likes reading, to write, and painting. | She likes reading, writing, and painting. |
| The plan is to reduce costs, increase quality, and customers will be happier. | The plan is to reduce costs, increase quality, and delight customers. |

**(h) Old before new, and end-weight**
Start sentences with information the reader already knows, and end with the new or important idea:
> ❌ *A decline in sales caused the closure of the store.* (important point buried)
> ✅ *The store closed because sales declined.* (or ... *because of a decline in sales*)

### 2.5 Clarity: making it impossible to misunderstand
**(a) Ambiguous pronouns**
> ❌ *When Ana met Sofia, she was nervous.* (who?)
> ✅ *When Ana met Sofia, Ana was nervous.*

> ❌ *The system sends reports to the manager, which is useful.* (what is useful?)
> ✅ *The system sends reports to the manager. This feature is useful.*

**(b) Misplaced and dangling modifiers**
> ❌ *Walking down the street, the building looked impressive.* (the building wasn't walking)
> ✅ *Walking down the street, I thought the building looked impressive.*

> ❌ *He only ate the salad.* (ambiguous placement)
> ✅ *He ate only the salad.* / *Only he ate the salad.*

**(c) Overloaded sentences**
Put one main idea in each sentence; use bullets for lists of three or more.

**(d) Vague words**
*thing, stuff, aspect, issue, factor, situation, process*: replace with specific words when possible.

**(e) Unclear logical connections**
Check each *however, therefore, because, so* matches the actual relationship.

**(f) Undefined jargon and abbreviations**
Define on first use.

**(g) Numbers, dates, and units**
Be exact; include time zones, currencies, and units; use consistent formats.

**(h) Instructions**
Use numbered steps; put the action first; one action per step.

**(i) Headings and formatting**
Use headings, short paragraphs, bullets, and bold for scanning. Don't overformat.

**(j) Consistency**
Same term for the same concept (don't alternate *client*, *customer*, *user* for the same group); same spelling variety; same tense logic; same capitalization and number style.

### 2.7 Punctuation and mechanics checklist
| Area | Check |
|---|---|
| **Commas** | after introductory phrases; around non-restrictive clauses; no comma between subject and verb; no comma splices |
| **Apostrophes** | *it's* (it is) vs. *its* (possessive); *you're* vs. *your*; *they're/their/there* |
| **Capitalization** | titles, names, start of sentences; not all nouns |
| **Hyphens** | compound modifiers before nouns (*well-known author*, *a 10-minute break*) |
| **Numbers** | spell out one–nine in many styles; use digits for larger numbers and for measurements |
| **Lists** | parallel structure, consistent punctuation |
| **Quotation marks** | US/UK conventions (Lesson 73) |
| **Articles** | *a/an/the/none*; general plurals and uncountables without articles |
| **Prepositions** | verify with a dictionary or corpus |
| **Verb forms** | agreement, tense consistency, irregular forms |
| **Spelling** | one variety; names |

**Commonly confused words (spell checkers often miss)**
| Pair | Difference |
|---|---|
| affect / effect | verb (influence) / noun (result) |
| then / than | time or sequence / comparison |
| lose / loose | misplace, fail to win / not tight |
| advice / advise | noun / verb |
| principal / principle | main (or school head) / rule |
| complement / compliment | complete / praise |
| it's / its | it is / possessive |
| their / there / they're | possessive / place / they are |
| whose / who's | possessive / who is |
| accept / except | receive / excluding |
| quite / quiet | fairly / silent |
| later / latter | after / the second of two |
| desert / dessert | arid land / sweet course |
| ensure / insure / assure | make certain / buy insurance / reassure |
| fewer / less | countable / uncountable |

### 2.8 Using tools: spell-check, grammar-check, and AI
**Tools can**
- catch spelling, basic grammar, and repeated words;
- suggest wording and alternatives;
- explain rules;
- reformat, summarize, and give feedback on clarity and tone.

**Tools cannot (reliably)**
- know your audience, purpose, and relationship;
- verify facts, sources, or quotations;
- decide what is natural in your specific context;
- guarantee accuracy of terms or collocations;
- keep your voice unless you ask for that and check the result.

**Good practice**
1. **Write your own draft first** so the ideas and voice are yours.
2. **Ask specific questions** (*Is this too blunt for a client email? Can you suggest a warmer version?*).
3. **Compare** the suggestion with your original and keep only improvements.
4. **Check** names, numbers, dates, citations, and meaning changes.
5. **Learn from corrections:** record patterns in your error log.
6. **Follow rules** of your school or workplace on AI use and disclosure.

### 2.9 Giving and receiving feedback
**Receiving**
- Separate the text from yourself.
- Ask for specific feedback (*What was unclear? Where did you lose interest?*).
- Look for patterns, not just individual corrections.

**Giving (peer review)**
- Start with something that works.
- Focus on a few key issues, not every error.
- Be specific (*In paragraph 2, I couldn't tell which source you mean*).
- Use suggestions (*You might consider…*) rather than commands.
- Separate global issues (structure, argument) from local ones (commas).

### 2.10 Building a personal editing system
1. **Error log:** categories (articles, prepositions, collocations, word order, punctuation, wordiness, tone). Update after each text.
2. **Top-5 checklist:** the five errors you make most; check them on every draft.
3. **Phrase bank:** natural chunks for your most frequent writing types.
4. **Style guide:** your own rules (spelling variety, number style, preferred terms).
5. **Templates:** for recurring documents.
6. **Review cycle:** revisit old texts after a month and edit them again.

## 3. Why Native Speakers Do This
1. **Everyone revises.** Professional writers expect to rewrite; first drafts are rarely good.
2. **Readers are busy.** In English-speaking workplaces and schools, concise, clear writing is a sign of respect for the reader's time.
3. **Plain-language movement.** Government, law, medicine, and business increasingly promote plain English to reduce errors and misunderstandings.
4. **Tone sensitivity.** Because writing lacks voice and face, readers interpret tiny cues (a period, a *Noted.*, a *Per my last email*) as signals of attitude.
5. **Public and permanent.** Emails, posts, and documents can be forwarded or archived; careful editing protects reputation.
6. **Style guides and editors.** Publishing, journalism, and organizations use editors and guides to ensure consistent quality.
7. **Culture of feedback.** Peer review, workshops, and editing are normal in education and the workplace.

## 4. When to Use / When Not To

**Edit thoroughly when**
- the text is important (job applications, contracts, publications, major emails);
- the reader is senior, external, or unfamiliar;
- the topic is sensitive (complaints, refusals, bad news);
- the text will be reused or archived;
- you're submitting it for assessment.

**Edit lightly when**
- the message is short and routine (a quick chat);
- speed matters more than polish;
- the relationship is close and the context informal.

**Don't**
- over-edit until the text loses your voice;
- polish sentences before fixing structure;
- replace plain words with fancy ones;
- accept every automated suggestion;
- edit when you're angry and send immediately (wait, then edit).

**Quick edit (2 minutes):** subject/headline → first sentence → ask/deadline → tone of the opening and closing → names, numbers, dates, attachments → spell-check.

**Full edit (30–60 minutes):** all six passes in 2.1, with a break between draft and revision.

## 5. Frequency & Priority

**Priorities for you (in order)**
1. **Purpose and structure**: Is the main point obvious?
2. **Concision**: cut wordy phrases and filler.
3. **Tone check**: bluntness, accusation, passive-aggressive phrases.
4. **Sentence clarity**: split long sentences; use active voice.
5. **Spanish-influenced patterns**: check against 2.6.
6. **Collocations, prepositions, articles**: check by chunk (Phases 3, 5).
7. **Consistency**: terms, spelling variety, tense, formatting.
8. **Proofreading**: names, numbers, dates, homophones.
9. **Read aloud** before sending anything important.
10. **Keep an error log** and review it monthly.

**Where the biggest gains usually are for advanced Spanish speakers**
- shortening sentences;
- removing redundancy;
- softening bluntness and removing translated formulas;
- using stronger verbs and fewer abstract noun chains;
- article and preposition accuracy.

## 6. American vs. British Notes
| Feature | US | UK |
|---|---|---|
| Spelling | *organize, color, program, check* | *organise, colour, programme, cheque* |
| Punctuation with quotes | periods/commas inside quotes | logical placement |
| Serial comma | often used | often omitted |
| Dates | *Nov. 14, 2026* | *14 November 2026* |
| Tone | direct and friendly; "Hi," first names | slightly more reserved; understatement; irony more common |
| Politeness phrases | *Thanks! / I appreciate it.* | *Many thanks / Cheers / I'd be grateful if…* |
| Style guides | AP, Chicago, APA | Guardian, Oxford, Economist (and others) |
| Plain-language movement | Plain Writing Act (federal government) | Plain English Campaign |
| Words | *fall, apartment, truck, cell phone* | *autumn, flat, lorry, mobile* |
| Collective nouns | singular (*The team is…*) | singular or plural (*The team are…*) |

Be consistent within a document. Set your tools' language to match your choice.

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

### 7.1 Style and clarity
| ❌ Habit | Effect | ✅ Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Very long sentences with many clauses | hard to follow | split; one idea each |
| Starting with long background | hides the point | front-load the main message |
| Using formal phrases for everything (*it is necessary to carry out the realization of*) | heavy, translated | plain verbs |
| Repeating the same idea with synonyms | redundant | say it once |
| Overusing the passive | evasive, heavy | name the agent |
| Abstract noun chains | dense | verbs |
| Vague words (*thing, aspect, situation*) | unclear | specific nouns |
| Ambiguous pronouns (*this, it, which*) | confusion | repeat the noun |
| Inconsistent terms | confusion | choose one |
| Overuse of connectors (*moreover, furthermore*) | stiff | vary or omit |
| Missing subjects (*Is important.*) | ungrammatical | *It is important.* |
| Gerund chains (*Being that…*) | awkward | simple clauses |
| Wrong register (too formal or too casual) | mismatch | match the audience |

### 7.2 Tone
| ❌ Habit | Effect | ✅ Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Direct translation of Spanish imperatives (*Send me…*) | blunt | *Could you send…?* |
| Starting with a criticism (*You didn't…*) | defensive reaction | describe the situation neutrally, propose a next step |
| Excessive politeness formulas | insincere or long | short, specific courtesy |
| Overusing "please" and "kindly" | pushy or stiff | one polite frame |
| Using "must" for requests | commanding | *could, would, need to, it would help if* |
| Too many exclamation marks | unprofessional | one at most |
| Sarcasm in writing | misunderstood | plain statement |
| Using *as per my last email* | passive-aggressive | *Following up on my earlier email…* |
| Writing while angry | regretted tone | draft, wait, edit |

### 7.3 Typical edits (examples)
| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I am writing this email with the objective of informing you about the change in relation to the schedule. | I'm writing to let you know about a schedule change. |
| It is a fact that the majority of the persons consider that the prices are high. | Most people think prices are high. |
| Due to the fact that the delivery had a delay, we proceeded to make the cancellation of the order. | The delivery was late, so we cancelled the order. |
| The realization of the audit was carried out by the external team. | The external team conducted the audit. |
| You didn't send the file, and I told you twice. | I haven't received the file yet. Could you send it by Friday? |

### 7.4 Attitude traps
- **"If it's grammatically correct, it's done."** Correct is the minimum.
- **"Longer and more formal looks smarter."** Clear and concise looks smarter.
- **"The tool corrected it, so it must be right."** Tools make mistakes and can change your meaning.
- **"I can't edit my own writing."** You can, with a break, a checklist, and reading aloud.
- **"Editing means fixing mistakes."** It means improving the reader's experience.
- **"I'll lose my voice if I edit too much."** Editing clarifies your voice.

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ **"Good writers don't need to edit."** — Most good writing is heavily revised.
- ❌ **"Editing = proofreading."** — Proofreading is the last, smallest pass.
- ❌ **"Never use the passive."** — It has legitimate uses; choose it deliberately.
- ❌ **"Never use 'I'."** — Depends on genre; clear ownership is often better.
- ❌ **"Short sentences are childish."** — They give clarity and emphasis.
- ❌ **"Formal means no contractions in every text."** — Depends on context (Lessons 72–74).
- ❌ **"A thesaurus improves style."** — Precise, familiar words usually do.
- ❌ **"Readability scores tell me if my writing is good."** — They measure sentence and word length, not clarity, tone, or accuracy.
- ❌ **"Tone is only about politeness words."** — Structure, directness, and framing matter as much.
- ❌ **"If the reader misunderstood, it's their fault."** — In English writing culture, the writer is responsible for clarity.
- ❌ **"AI polishing means no need to learn."** — You still need judgment to decide what is right for your reader.
- ❌ **"Passive-aggressive phrases are standard business English."** — They're common, but they often irritate readers.

## 9. Pronunciation Note → Training Method (for writing)

### 9.1 The six-pass edit (practice on a 300-word draft)
1. **Purpose pass:** write the goal and audience on top of the page.
2. **Structure pass:** reverse outline; reorder.
3. **Paragraph pass:** topic sentences, transitions, cuts.
4. **Sentence pass:** split long sentences; make passives active where appropriate.
5. **Word pass:** collocations, wordiness, Spanish-influenced patterns.
6. **Mechanics pass:** read backwards; check names, numbers, homophones.

### 9.2 Cut 20%
Take a draft and reduce it by 20% without losing information. This forces you to find filler and redundancy.

### 9.3 The "so what?" and "who cares?" test
For each paragraph: *So what?* (is the point clear?) and *Who cares?* (does the reader need it?).

### 9.4 The tone read
Read the text as the recipient: tired, busy, unsure of your intentions. Underline any phrase that could sound cold, bossy, or irritated. Rewrite it.

### 9.5 Read aloud and record
Read aloud (Lesson 66). Where you run out of breath, the sentence is too long. Where you stumble, the word choice or grammar is awkward. Where it sounds like a speech, simplify.

### 9.6 Before/after file
Keep a document of drafts and edits. Review it monthly to see your progress and recurring problems.

### 9.7 Peer exchange
Swap texts with another learner or friend. Mark only three issues per text: one structure, one tone, one language.

### 9.8 Edit someone else's writing
Practice by editing published emails, announcements, or product descriptions that are poorly written. Rewrite them clearly.

### 9.9 Checklist card (keep next to your computer)
- [ ] Purpose and reader clear?
- [ ] Main point in the first two lines?
- [ ] One idea per paragraph?
- [ ] Sentences under ~25 words on average?
- [ ] Active verbs, concrete nouns?
- [ ] Wordy phrases cut?
- [ ] Tone checked (blunt? cold? pushy?)?
- [ ] Spanish-influenced patterns checked?
- [ ] Collocations, prepositions, articles checked?
- [ ] Names, numbers, dates, attachments checked?
- [ ] Spelling variety consistent?
- [ ] Read aloud?

### 9.10 Trouble spots
- Editing too early, before the draft is complete.
- Fixing commas before structure.
- Over-trusting tools.
- Not keeping an error log.
- Sending without a final tone read.

## 10. Memory Technique

### 10.1 Big to small
Purpose → structure → paragraph → sentence → word → mechanics. Never fix small things first.

### 10.2 CCC: Clear, Concise, Courteous
Three words to check any text (add *Correct* for four).

### 10.3 The doorway test (Lesson 72)
Can the reader tell in five seconds what you want?

### 10.4 Cut the clutter
Delete *very, really, basically, in order, due to the fact that*. If the sentence still works, it stays deleted.

### 10.5 Verb first
Find the real action hidden in a noun (*make an assessment → assess*).

### 10.6 Three tone questions
1. Would I say this to their face?
2. Does it blame or help?
3. Does it sound like a request or an order?

### 10.7 Spaced retrieval cards
- *Order of editing passes?* → **purpose, structure, paragraphs, sentences, words, mechanics**
- *"In order to"?* → **to**
- *"Due to the fact that"?* → **because**
- *"Make a decision"?* → **decide**
- *"Per my last email"?* → **may sound passive-aggressive; rephrase**
- *Passive vs. active?* → **active when the agent matters**
- *"its" vs. "it's"?* → **possessive vs. it is**
- *Best way to catch awkward sentences?* → **read aloud**
Review on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30.

### 10.8 Monthly audit
Once a month, choose one old text, edit it, and compare. Record the three biggest improvements.

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1: Edit from big to small: purpose, structure, sentences, words, mechanics.**

> **Pattern 2: Clear before clever; concise before complete.**

> **Pattern 3: Tone is built from word choice, directness, and framing.**

> **Pattern 4: Requests soften with modals; blame softens with "we" and neutral description.**

> **Pattern 5: Replace nominalizations with verbs.**

> **Pattern 6: Cut filler and wordy phrases.**

> **Pattern 7: Put important information at the end of the sentence and the start of the paragraph.**

> **Pattern 8: One idea per sentence; vary length.**

> **Pattern 9: Make pronouns and references unambiguous.**

> **Pattern 10: Use parallel structure in lists.**

> **Pattern 11: Read aloud; take a break; use a checklist.**

> **Pattern 12: Use tools as assistants, not as authors.**

## 12. Real-Life Examples

### 12.1 Tone: before and after
| Before | After |
|---|---|
| You are late again. | I noticed the report hasn't arrived yet. Is there anything I can do to help? |
| This is wrong. | I think there may be an error in the third column. Could you check it? |
| I told you this already. | Just to make sure it's clear: the deadline is Friday. |
| Do it today. | Could you finish this today? It's blocking the next step. |
| I'm very disappointed in your work. | I'd like to talk about the last report. A few areas need improvement. |
| Whatever. | (avoid; say what you mean) |
| Noted. | Thanks, noted. I'll update the plan. |
| As per my last email, the deadline is Monday. | Quick reminder that the deadline is Monday. |

### 12.2 Style: before and after
> ❌ *In the event that you are unable to participate in the meeting that will be held on Thursday, it would be appreciated if you could make us aware of this fact at your earliest convenience.*
> ✅ *If you can't attend Thursday's meeting, please let us know.*

> ❌ *The implementation of the new software system was conducted by the IT department in the course of the last quarter.*
> ✅ *The IT department implemented the new software last quarter.*

> ❌ *It is of great importance that all employees are in possession of a valid ID badge at all times.*
> ✅ *All employees must carry a valid ID badge at all times.*

### 12.3 Clarity: before and after
> ❌ *The manager told the assistant that he needed to send the report.* (who needs to send it?)
> ✅ *The manager told the assistant, "You need to send the report."* / *The manager said he needed to send the report.* (clarify who)

> ❌ *Please find the invoices attached, which should be paid by March 15 or a late fee will be charged, and call us if you have questions about this or any other matter.*
> ✅ *The invoices are attached. Please pay by March 15 to avoid a late fee. If you have questions, call us.*

### 12.4 Full email edit
> **Draft:**
> *Dear Mr. Ortiz, I am writing to you in relation to the matter of the delay that has occurred in the delivery of your order. I want to inform you that, due to the fact that there were unforeseen problems with our supplier, it was not possible for us to make the delivery on the date that was agreed. We apologize for any inconvenience this may have caused you. We proceeded to make contact with the supplier in order to solve the problem and we think that the order will be delivered at the end of next week. Without more, I say goodbye. Attentively, Ana Ruiz*
>
> **Edited:**
> *Dear Mr. Ortiz, I'm sorry your order is late. Our supplier had an unexpected problem, so we couldn't deliver on the agreed date. We've contacted them, and we expect to deliver by Friday, 20 November. I'll confirm as soon as it ships. Thank you for your patience. Best regards, Ana Ruiz*

(From 100 words to about 50, with a specific date and a next step.)

### 12.5 Academic edit
> **Draft:** *In the last years, a lot of researches have been done about the topic of social media and its influence in the life of young persons, and the majority of them conclude that it has a negative effect.*
> **Edited:** *In recent years, many studies have examined the influence of social media on young people, and most report negative effects.*

### 12.6 Personal edit
> **Draft:** *Hello my friend, I hope you are very well. I am writing to tell you that I am very happy because I got the job that I wanted and I wanted to share this news with you.*
> **Edited:** *Hey! Guess what? I got the job! Wanted you to be the first to know.*

### 12.7 Homophone catch
> *Their going to loose the game if they don't practice more than there coach says.* → *They're going to lose the game if they don't practice more than their coach says.*

17. "Laura told Carmen that she should apply." 18. "Walking into the room, the lights were off." 19. "He only wants the red one." (he wants no other color) 20. "The team reviewed the report and the plan, which was approved."
