# Lesson 21 — Relative Clauses: Defining vs. Non-Defining, and When to Drop "That/Which/Who"
### Phase 1: Grammar | Block C (Nouns, Determiners, Reference) | Lesson 4 of 4 (Final Lesson of Block C)

---

## 0. Where this fits
This closes Block C. Relative clauses attach extra information to a noun using a relative pronoun (who, which, that, whose, where, when) — and English has a genuinely distinctive feature here that Spanish lacks entirely: the ability to **omit** the relative pronoun in certain structures. This "invisible" grammar is common in natural spoken English and often confuses learners who expect every relative clause to have a visible connector, the way Spanish always does (*que* is essentially never optional).

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation

A relative clause is a mini-sentence glued onto a noun to describe it further: "the book **that I bought**," "the man **who called you**." English splits these into two genuinely different types with different punctuation and grammar rules:
- **Defining (restrictive)** relative clauses — essential information; without it, you don't know which one is meant. No commas.
- **Non-defining (non-restrictive)** relative clauses — extra, non-essential information; the noun is already identified without it. Commas required.

And uniquely, in defining relative clauses (only), the relative pronoun can often be **dropped entirely** when it's the object of the clause — something Spanish never allows.

---

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2a. Defining (Restrictive) Relative Clauses
**No commas.** The clause is essential — removing it changes or loses the meaning (you no longer know which specific one is meant).
- "The woman **who called you** is my sister." (Without the clause: "The woman is my sister" — which woman? Unclear. The clause is essential.)
- "I lost the keys **that I bought yesterday**." (Specifies exactly which keys — essential.)

**Relative pronoun choice for defining clauses:**
| Referring to | Subject of clause | Object of clause |
|---|---|---|
| People | who / that | who(m) / that / (nothing) |
| Things | which / that | which / that / (nothing) |
| Possession | whose | whose |
| Place | where | where |
| Time | when | when |

### 2b. Non-Defining (Non-Restrictive) Relative Clauses
**Commas required.** The clause adds extra, non-essential information — the noun is already fully identified without it (often because it's a proper noun, or already specified elsewhere).
- "My sister, **who lives in Madrid**, is visiting next week." (I have one sister — the clause adds extra info, doesn't narrow down which sister.)
- "The Eiffel Tower, **which was built in 1889**, attracts millions of visitors." (There's only one Eiffel Tower — the clause is just additional information, not identifying which tower.)

**Critical restriction:** non-defining clauses can ONLY use who/which/whose/where/when — **never "that,"** and the relative pronoun can **never** be dropped. This is a hard rule, not a stylistic preference.

### 2c. THE Key Feature: Dropping the Relative Pronoun (Object Relative Clauses Only)
When the relative pronoun is the **object** of the relative clause (not the subject) in a **defining** clause, it can be omitted entirely — extremely common in natural spoken and written English:
- "The book **(that) I bought** was expensive." (that = object of "bought"; can be dropped)
- "The man **(who) I met yesterday** was very kind." (who = object of "met"; can be dropped)

**When it CANNOT be dropped — subject relative clauses:**
- "The woman **who called you** is my sister." (who = subject of "called" — CANNOT be dropped: ❌ "The woman called you is my sister" is not a valid sentence)

**Quick test:** if you can mentally insert a subject pronoun right after where the relative pronoun would go and the sentence still makes sense as two separate clauses, it's an object relative clause and can likely be dropped. "The book (that) I bought" → "I bought **it**" (it = object) → droppable. "The woman who called you" → "**She** called you" (she = subject) → not droppable.

---

## 3. Why Native Speakers Drop the Pronoun So Often
Dropping the object relative pronoun is extremely natural and common in everyday spoken English — "the book I bought" (no "that") sounds completely normal and is, if anything, more common in casual speech than including it. This is precisely why beginner/intermediate learners often perceive native speech as having "missing words" — they're not missing, they're grammatically optional and routinely omitted, a genuine feature of the language, not sloppiness.

---

## 4. When Each Type Matters Most
- **Defining clauses** — used constantly, in every register, for identifying/specifying which noun you mean.
- **Non-defining clauses** — very common in writing and more formal/explanatory speech, for adding background information about something already identified.
- **Dropping the object pronoun** — extremely natural in casual speech; keeping it in is not wrong, just slightly more formal/deliberate-sounding, and sometimes clearer in complex written sentences.

---

## 5. Frequency & Register
Defining clauses dominate everyday speech (identifying which thing you mean is a constant communicative need). Non-defining clauses are more common in writing and explanatory/descriptive speech (biographies, news, formal descriptions). Dropping the object relative pronoun is a hallmark of natural, native-sounding casual speech — including it every time (when it's grammatically optional) can sound slightly stiff or overly careful, though it's never wrong.

---

## 6. American vs. British Note
No major systematic difference in the defining/non-defining system. One stylistic note some style guides teach (particularly some American style guides): a soft preference for using "that" in defining clauses and reserving "which" for non-defining clauses exclusively — but in actual native usage (both American and British), "which" is commonly used in defining clauses too ("the book which I bought" is completely natural and used by native speakers in both varieties), so don't treat this "rule" as a hard requirement — it's a style preference in some formal/edited writing contexts, not a universal grammar rule.

---

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

| Mistake | Why | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Always including "that/who/which," never dropping it, even in casual speech | Spanish *que* is essentially always required, so learners default to always including the English equivalent too | Practice dropping the object relative pronoun in casual contexts: "the book I bought," not always "the book that I bought" — both are correct, but the dropped version sounds more natural in casual speech |
| Using "that" in a non-defining clause: "My sister, that lives in Madrid, ..." | Not distinguishing defining/non-defining, and not knowing "that" is banned specifically in non-defining clauses | "My sister, who lives in Madrid, ..." (who/which only, never "that," in non-defining clauses) |
| Missing commas around non-defining clauses, or adding commas around defining clauses | Comma rules for restrictive/non-restrictive clauses don't map directly onto Spanish punctuation conventions | Defining = no commas; non-defining = commas required, both before and after the clause (unless it ends the sentence) |
| Dropping the relative pronoun in a SUBJECT relative clause (not allowed) | Overgeneralizing "the pronoun can be dropped" without the subject/object restriction | "The woman who called you is my sister" — the "who" here is the subject of "called" and cannot be dropped |

---

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ "Dropping the relative pronoun is just casual/lazy speech." — It's fully grammatical, natural, and extremely common at every level of native fluency, not a marker of carelessness.
- ❌ "Which and that are always interchangeable everywhere." — They ARE interchangeable in defining clauses (with some style-guide soft preferences, Section 6), but "that" is categorically banned from non-defining clauses — a hard rule, not a preference.
- ❌ "The comma is just a stylistic choice around relative clauses." — The presence or absence of commas can genuinely change meaning: "My brother who lives in Madrid is a doctor" (defining — implies I have more than one brother, this specifies which one) vs. "My brother, who lives in Madrid, is a doctor" (non-defining — implies I have only one brother, and the Madrid detail is just extra info).

---

## 9. Pronunciation Note
Non-defining relative clauses are typically set off with a slight pause and a distinct intonation dip in speech (mirroring the comma in writing) — this is a real, audible cue that helps native listeners immediately parse whether a clause is essential or extra information, even without seeing punctuation.

---

## 10. Memory Technique
**The "does removing it lose essential identifying information?" test** for defining vs. non-defining: if removing the clause makes you unable to identify who/what is meant, it's defining (no commas). If the noun is already clear without it, it's non-defining (commas required).

**For droppability:** the "insert a subject pronoun" test from Section 2c — if the relative pronoun's slot could be replaced by a subject pronoun in an independent sentence, it's NOT droppable (it's the subject); if it would be replaced by an object pronoun, it IS droppable (it's the object).

---

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern:** Non-defining clauses = commas + who/which/whose/where/when only (never "that," never droppable). Defining clauses = no commas + who/which/that (subject, never droppable) OR who/which/that/nothing (object, droppable). This two-branch structure covers the entire relative clause system.

---

## 12. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1:**
- Defining: "The restaurant that we went to last night was amazing." (specifies which restaurant)
- Non-defining: "That restaurant, which just opened last month, is already fully booked." (adds extra info about an already-identified restaurant)

**Level 2 — droppable vs. not droppable, side by side:**
- Droppable (object): "The person (who) I spoke to was very helpful."
- Not droppable (subject): "The person who spoke to me was very helpful." (who = subject of "spoke")

**Level 3 — meaning changes with commas:**
- "The students who studied hard passed the exam." (defining — implies only SOME students studied hard, and those are the ones who passed)
- "The students, who studied hard, passed the exam." (non-defining — implies ALL the students [already identified as a group] studied hard, and all passed; the studying is just additional info about the whole group)

**Level 4 — whose, where, when in complex sentences:**
- "The company, whose headquarters are in Bogotá, where I did my internship, is expanding into new markets this year." (non-defining "whose" clause + non-defining "where" clause, both adding extra background information about an already-identified company)

---

## 13. Related Expressions / Terms
- **Restrictive / non-restrictive clause** — the more formal linguistic terms for defining/non-defining.
- **Relative pronoun** — who, whom, whose, which, that, where, when.
- **Zero relative pronoun** — the linguistic term for the "dropped" pronoun slot in object relative clauses.

1. Defining clauses provide essential, identifying information (no commas); non-defining clauses provide extra, non-essential information about something already identified (commas required).
