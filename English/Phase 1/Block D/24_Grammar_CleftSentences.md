# Lesson 24 — Cleft Sentences (It was John who..., What I need is...)
### Phase 1: Grammar | Block D (Sentence-Level Grammar) | Lesson 3 of 7

---

## 0. Where this fits
This lesson delivers directly on a promise from Lesson 2 (Section 12, Level 4): Spanish can front any element of a sentence for emphasis (*¡Increíble lo que hizo!*), but English's rigid SVO word order (Lesson 1) can't do that the same way. Cleft sentences are English's dedicated tool for achieving that same emphatic fronting effect — "splitting" a simple sentence into two clauses specifically to spotlight one piece of information.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation

A "cleft" sentence takes a normal, single-clause sentence and splits ("clefts") it into two parts, using "it is/was" or a "what" clause, specifically to put spotlight-emphasis on one particular piece of information. Where Spanish would simply move a word to the front of the sentence for emphasis, English restructures the whole sentence around a dummy frame ("It was... who...", "What I need is...") to achieve the same spotlighting effect without breaking SVO order within each clause.

Plain: "John broke the vase." (neutral, no special emphasis)
Cleft: "**It was John** who broke the vase." (emphasis: specifically John, not someone else)
Cleft: "**What John broke** was the vase." (emphasis: specifically the vase, not something else)

---

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2a. It-Clefts — "It was/is [emphasized element] who/that..."
**Form:** It + be + [emphasized element] + who/that/which + [rest of the sentence]
Used to emphasize a specific noun/element by placing it right after "it was/is":
- "**It was Maria** who called you." (emphasis on Maria — not someone else)
- "**It was yesterday** that the package arrived." (emphasis on the timing — yesterday specifically)
- "**It wasn't the price** that bothered me — it was the quality." (emphasis + explicit contrast)

**Any sentence element can be clefted this way** — subject, object, time expression, place expression — which is precisely the flexibility Spanish achieves through free word order, and which English achieves instead through this dedicated structure.

### 2b. Wh-Clefts (Pseudo-Clefts) — "What/Where/When/Why... is/was..."
**Form:** What/Where/etc. + [rest of clause] + be + [emphasized element]
Used to emphasize by first setting up "what I need/want/mean is..." and then delivering the emphasized element at the end — building anticipation before the key information:
- "**What I really need** is a vacation." (emphasis on "a vacation," built up through the "what I need is..." frame)
- "**What surprised me most** was how calm she stayed." (emphasis on her calmness)
- "**Where I grew up** was very different from here." (emphasis on the place, using "where" as the clefting word)

**Reversed order variant** (emphasized element first, "what"-clause after — used for strong emphasis, especially in speech):
- "A vacation is what I really need." (same meaning, emphasized element fronted even further)

### 2c. All-Cleft ("All I want is...")
A related, very common structure using "all" instead of "what," typically implying "only this, nothing more":
- "All I want is a little peace and quiet." (implies: nothing more than this, just this one thing)

---

## 3. Why Native Speakers Use Cleft Sentences
Because plain SVO word order in English can't be freely rearranged for emphasis the way Spanish can (Lessons 1 and 2's core insight), cleft sentences are the grammatical tool that fills this gap — they let a speaker spotlight exactly which piece of information matters most, control the listener's attention and expectations (especially with wh-clefts, which build anticipation), and create explicit contrast ("it wasn't X, it was Y").

---

## 4. When This Matters Most
- **It-clefts** — useful for correcting misunderstandings, clarifying who/what/when specifically is meant, and creating contrast ("It wasn't him, it was her").
- **Wh-clefts** — useful for building emphasis through anticipation, common in explanations, persuasive writing, and presentations ("What this data shows is...").
- **When it matters less:** in very short, simple everyday exchanges, plain sentences with vocal stress often achieve similar emphasis without needing the full cleft structure — cleft sentences shine especially in writing and more deliberate/planned speech.

---

## 5. Frequency & Register
Cleft sentences are used across all registers, but they're especially valuable and common in more formal, explanatory, argumentative, and presentation-style speech/writing, where controlling emphasis and building an argument matters (academic writing, business presentations, persuasive essays). They're also very natural in everyday spoken English for clarification and contrast ("It's not that I don't like it, it's that I don't have time").

---

## 6. American vs. British Note
No major structural or frequency difference between American and British English — cleft sentences are used identically in both varieties.

---

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

| Mistake | Why | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "John was who broke the vase." (missing "it") | Attempting a direct word-for-word structure without the required "it" dummy subject | "It was John who broke the vase." |
| Simply fronting a word for emphasis, Spanish-style, producing an ungrammatical English sentence: "The vase John broke." (attempting emphasis by reordering, without a cleft structure) | Applying Spanish's flexible word-order-for-emphasis strategy directly to English, which doesn't allow this | Use a proper cleft structure: "It was the vase that John broke." / "What John broke was the vase." |
| Confusing "it" (referring to nothing specific, dummy subject) with "it" as a real pronoun referring to something | Not recognizing the it-cleft's "it" as grammatically empty/functional, not a real referent | Recognize "it" in "It was John who..." as a structural placeholder, not a pronoun standing in for a previously mentioned noun |
| Overusing cleft sentences for every emphasis need, sounding unnaturally formal/heavy in casual conversation | Learning the structure and overapplying it, similar to the inversion-overuse trap from Lesson 23 | Reserve clefts for genuine emphasis/contrast needs; plain sentences with vocal stress remain the default for everyday casual emphasis |

---

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ "Cleft sentences are just a fancy, optional way of saying something, with no real function." — They serve a genuine, otherwise-unavailable grammatical function: emphasizing/spotlighting a specific sentence element without breaking English's rigid word order.
- ❌ "It-clefts and wh-clefts are interchangeable in every context." — They create different rhetorical effects: it-clefts emphasize by "correcting/specifying" (often implying a contrast with what might have been assumed), while wh-clefts build anticipation before delivering the key information — subtly different communicative functions.
- ❌ "You can cleft any part of a sentence with 'what,' including the subject easily." — Wh-clefts with "what" work most naturally for objects/things wanted/needed, not smoothly for the subject itself; that's typically where it-clefts (Section 2a) are used instead.

---

## 9. Pronunciation Note
The emphasized element in an it-cleft ("It was **JOHN** who...") or the final element in a wh-cleft ("...is **a VACATION**") carries the strongest sentence stress — the entire structure exists specifically to direct stress/attention to that one word or phrase, so flattening the intonation there undermines the whole point of using a cleft sentence.

---

## 10. Memory Technique
**The "spotlight" image:** picture a stage with a spotlight. Cleft sentences are how English moves the spotlight onto a specific piece of information without rearranging the whole stage (word order) the way Spanish can. "It was John who..." = spotlight on John. "What I need is..." = spotlight building toward the final answer.

---

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern:** It-cleft = It + be + [emphasized element] + who/that + [rest of clause]. Wh-cleft = What/Where/etc. + [rest of clause] + be + [emphasized element]. Both achieve the same underlying goal (spotlighting one element) through fundamentally different structural placements — it-clefts spotlight early, wh-clefts spotlight at the end, building anticipation.

---

## 12. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1:**
- Plain: "She solved the problem." → Cleft: "It was **she** who solved the problem." (emphasis: her, specifically, not someone else)

**Level 2 — wh-cleft, building anticipation:**
- "What really impressed me was her confidence." (builds up, then delivers the key point at the end)

**Level 3 — it-cleft for explicit contrast/correction:**
- "It wasn't the deadline that stressed me out — it was the lack of communication." (correcting a possible assumption, contrasting two possibilities)

**Level 4 — combining cleft with other grammar from this course:**
- "What I should have done was ask for help sooner." (wh-cleft + Lesson 17's "should have" past-modal regret structure, combined into one sophisticated sentence)

---

## 13. Related Expressions / Terms
- **It-cleft** and **wh-cleft (pseudo-cleft)** — this lesson's two core structures.
- **All-cleft** — the related "all I want is..." structure (Section 2c).
- **Fronting** — the general linguistic term for moving an element to the front of a sentence for emphasis (relevant to both this lesson and Lesson 23's inversion).
