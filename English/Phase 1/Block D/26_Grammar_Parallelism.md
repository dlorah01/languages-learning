# Lesson 26 — Parallelism & Sentence Balance
### Phase 1: Grammar | Block D (Sentence-Level Grammar) | Lesson 5 of 7

---

## 0. Where this fits
This lesson covers a subtler, less-taught grammatical principle: when English lists, compares, or connects items, each item must share the **same grammatical form**. Violating parallelism doesn't always produce a "wrong" sentence in a strict rule-breaking sense — it produces a sentence that sounds unbalanced, amateurish, or subtly "off," which is exactly the kind of near-invisible error that separates advanced from native-level English.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation

Imagine a sentence as a set of matching items on a shelf — if you're listing things, each item on the shelf should be the same "shape" (grammatically). "She likes **running, swimming, and to cycle**" breaks the pattern (two gerunds, then suddenly an infinitive) — like putting two matching vases and one random shoe on the same shelf. Parallelism means keeping every item in a list, comparison, or paired structure grammatically consistent.

---

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2a. Parallelism in Lists
Every item joined by "and," "or," commas in a series must share the same grammatical form:
- ❌ "She likes running, swimming, and to cycle." (gerund, gerund, infinitive — inconsistent)
- ✅ "She likes running, swimming, and cycling." (all gerunds — consistent)
- ✅ "She likes to run, to swim, and to cycle." (all infinitives — also consistent, though repeating "to" each time is less common; "She likes to run, swim, and cycle" — sharing one "to" — is more natural)

### 2b. Parallelism with Correlative Conjunctions
Paired connectors (both...and, either...or, neither...nor, not only...but also) require the same grammatical structure on both sides:
- ❌ "She is not only intelligent but also works hard." (adjective vs. verb phrase — mismatched)
- ✅ "She is not only intelligent but also hardworking." (adjective, adjective — matched)
- ❌ "He either wants to leave or staying here." (infinitive vs. gerund — mismatched)
- ✅ "He either wants to leave or wants to stay here." / "He wants either to leave or to stay here." (matched)

### 2c. Parallelism in Comparisons
When comparing two things, both sides of the comparison need matching grammatical structure:
- ❌ "I'd rather read a book than watching TV." (infinitive-flavored vs. gerund — mismatched)
- ✅ "I'd rather read a book than watch TV." (both base verb forms — matched, since "rather...than" conventionally takes base verb forms on both sides)

### 2d. Parallelism Across Longer Structures (Rhetorical Parallelism)
In more sophisticated writing/speech, parallelism is deliberately used for rhetorical effect — repeating a grammatical pattern across clauses for rhythm and persuasive power:
- "We shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight on the landing grounds, we shall fight in the fields..." (Churchill — repeated "we shall fight" structure, deliberate parallelism for rhetorical power)
- This connects to Phase 9 (Writing), where rhetorical parallelism will be covered as a persuasive/stylistic writing tool.

---

## 3. Why Native Speakers Value Parallelism
Parallelism isn't just an aesthetic nicety — broken parallelism genuinely disrupts a listener/reader's processing, because the brain expects the established grammatical pattern to continue, and a mismatched item creates a small, noticeable stumble, even if the listener can't immediately articulate what went wrong. This makes parallelism one of those "invisible until broken" features — you don't consciously notice correct parallelism, but incorrect parallelism registers as awkward or unpolished.

---

## 4. When This Matters Most
- **Formal writing (essays, reports, professional communication)** — parallelism errors are highly noticeable and considered a mark of unpolished writing at this level.
- **Lists/enumerations of any kind** — resumes, bullet points, spoken lists of reasons/examples.
- **When it matters less:** in very casual, spontaneous speech, minor parallelism slips are common even among native speakers and rarely disrupt communication — this is much more of a "polish" issue than a comprehension issue in casual contexts.

---

## 5. Frequency & Register
Parallelism matters across all registers when constructing lists/comparisons, but the *consequences* of breaking it scale with formality — a parallelism slip in casual conversation is barely noticed; the same slip in a job application cover letter or academic essay stands out as a genuine writing weakness.

---

## 6. American vs. British Note
No significant systematic difference — parallelism principles apply identically in both varieties.

---

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

| Mistake | Why | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "I enjoy reading, writing, and to travel." | Mixing gerunds and infinitives in a list (Spanish doesn't enforce this same grammatical-form matching as strictly) | "I enjoy reading, writing, and traveling." |
| "The job requires strong communication skills and to work well under pressure." | Mixing a noun phrase and an infinitive phrase | "The job requires strong communication skills and the ability to work well under pressure." (both noun phrases) OR "The job requires communicating well and working well under pressure." (both gerund phrases) |
| "She is both talented and she works hard." | Mismatched structure after "both...and" (adjective vs. clause) | "She is both talented and hardworking." (adjective, adjective) |
| Producing unbalanced lists in resumes/CVs (a genuinely high-stakes context): "Managed a team, improving efficiency, and was responsible for budgets" (verb, gerund, passive — three different forms) | Not applying parallel structure across bullet points | "Managed a team, improved efficiency, and oversaw budgets." (all past-tense action verbs — the standard parallel form for resume bullet points) |

---

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ "Parallelism is just a stylistic writing preference, not real grammar." — While it's more of a "polish/clarity" issue than a strict grammaticality issue in casual speech, in formal writing specifically it's treated as a genuine correctness standard, not merely optional style.
- ❌ "As long as each individual item is grammatically correct on its own, the sentence is fine." — Each item can be perfectly correct in isolation while still creating an unbalanced, awkward sentence when combined inconsistently with the others — parallelism is about the *relationship* between items, not each item alone.

---

## 9. Pronunciation Note
Rhetorical parallelism (Section 2d) is often reinforced with matching stress/rhythm patterns across the repeated structures in speech — "We shall fight... we shall fight... we shall fight..." each repetition typically gets similar stress and pacing, reinforcing the parallel grammatical structure with parallel *sound* as well, which is part of why it's so rhetorically powerful when spoken aloud.

---

## 10. Memory Technique
**The "matching shelf items" image** from Section 1 — before finalizing a list, comparison, or correlative-conjunction sentence, mentally line up each item and check: "do they all have the same grammatical shape (all gerunds, all infinitives, all nouns, all adjectives)?" If one item looks different, fix it to match the others.

---

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern:** Whatever grammatical form the FIRST item in a list/comparison/correlative structure takes, every subsequent item must match that same form — gerund-gerund-gerund, infinitive-infinitive-infinitive, noun phrase-noun phrase-noun phrase, never mixed.

---

## 12. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1:**
- ❌ "I like pizza, tacos, and to eat sushi." → ✅ "I like pizza, tacos, and sushi." (all simple nouns)

**Level 2 — correlative conjunction parallelism:**
- ❌ "You can either call me or you can text me." (redundant subject repetition breaks the clean parallel) → ✅ "You can either call me or text me." (parallel verb phrases after the shared subject)

**Level 3 — resume/professional bullet point parallelism:**
- ❌ "Responsibilities included managing budgets, team leadership, and to coordinate with vendors." → ✅ "Responsibilities included managing budgets, leading the team, and coordinating with vendors." (all gerund phrases)

**Level 4 — rhetorical parallelism in persuasive speech/writing:**
- "The plan is ambitious, the timeline is tight, and the stakes are high." (three parallel [subject + linking verb + adjective] clauses, building rhetorical rhythm)

---

## 13. Related Expressions / Terms
- **Correlative conjunctions** — both...and, either...or, neither...nor, not only...but also (this lesson's Section 2b focus).
- **Rhetorical parallelism / anaphora** (repeated sentence-opening structure) — the persuasive-writing application, expanded in Phase 9.
- **Faulty parallelism** — the formal term for parallelism errors, common in writing-feedback contexts (worth recognizing if you receive this feedback on formal writing).
