# Lesson 22 — Reported Speech: Backshifting and When You *Don't* Backshift
### Phase 1: Grammar | Block D (Sentence-Level Grammar) | Lesson 1 of 7

---

## 0. Where this fits
This opens Block D, which moves from noun/reference grammar (Block C) into how English restructures whole sentences for different communicative purposes. Reported speech directly reuses the entire tense system from Lessons 6–10 — "backshifting" is really just "move one step back in the tense grid you already know," which makes this lesson largely an application exercise rather than brand-new material.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation

When you report what someone said, you're not quoting them directly (with quotation marks) — you're describing their statement from your current vantage point, later in time. Because time has passed and your perspective has shifted, English typically "backshifts" the tense one step into the past, to reflect that the original statement is now itself a past event.

Direct: She said, "I **am** tired."
Reported: She said (that) she **was** tired.

The verb moved from present ("am") to past ("was") because we're now describing, from a later point, what her present tense was *at the time she said it*.

---

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2a. The Standard Backshift Pattern
| Direct speech | Reported speech |
|---|---|
| Present simple: "I work here." | Past simple: She said she worked there. |
| Present continuous: "I am working." | Past continuous: She said she was working. |
| Present perfect: "I have finished." | Past perfect: She said she had finished. |
| Past simple: "I worked there." | Past perfect: She said she had worked there. |
| Past continuous: "I was working." | Past perfect continuous: She said she had been working. |
| Will: "I will help." | Would: She said she would help. |
| Can: "I can swim." | Could: She said she could swim. |
| Must (obligation): "I must go." | Had to: She said she had to go. |

Notice this is literally Lesson 6's grid, shifted one time-period backward — present becomes past, past becomes past perfect (going "further back" than simple past, exactly matching past perfect's core meaning from Lesson 8: an earlier point relative to another past reference point — here, the reference point is the moment of reporting).

### 2b. Other Necessary Shifts Alongside Tense
Reported speech also typically shifts pronouns, time expressions, and place expressions, to reflect the new speaker/time/place perspective:

| Direct | Reported |
|---|---|
| "I" | he/she (matching the speaker being reported) |
| "here" | there |
| "now" | then / at that moment |
| "today" | that day |
| "yesterday" | the day before / the previous day |
| "tomorrow" | the next day / the following day |
| "this" | that |
| "next week" | the following week |

- Direct: He said, "I'll call you tomorrow."
- Reported: He said he would call me the next day.

### 2c. When You DON'T Backshift — The Genuine Exceptions
This is the part most textbooks underexplain, and exactly where this lesson's title points:

**Exception 1 — the statement is still true / a general fact / unchanged at the time of reporting:**
- Direct: "The Earth is round." → Reported: She said (that) the Earth **is** round. (general truth — no backshift needed, though backshifting is also grammatically acceptable here, just not required)
- Direct: "I work at Google." (still true now, at the moment of reporting) → Reported: She said she **works** at Google. (native speakers often keep present tense here specifically to signal the fact is still current — backshifting to "worked" would subtly suggest she may no longer work there)

**Exception 2 — the reporting verb itself is in present/future tense (very common in immediate, same-day reporting, texting, or ongoing conversations):**
- "What did she say?" "She says she's on her way." (present reporting verb "says," so no backshift needed — this is extremely common in casual real-time conversation)

**Exception 3 — modal verbs that have no past form stay the same:**
- "would," "could," "might," "should," "ought to," "must" (deduction sense) generally don't backshift further, since they're already at their "furthest back" grammatical form, or because backshifting them further isn't grammatically available: Direct: "I might come." → Reported: She said she might come. (no change — "might" doesn't backshift to anything else)

---

## 3. Why Native Speakers Use This System (and Bend It)
Backshifting exists because reported speech is fundamentally about representing someone else's *earlier* statement from your *current* later vantage point — the same "reference point" logic from Lesson 10 applies directly here. But native speakers are pragmatically flexible about it: if the underlying fact is still true, backshifting can obscure that truth, so speakers often keep the original tense specifically to signal current relevance — a genuinely meaningful, deliberate choice, not a random exception.

---

## 4. When This Matters Most
- **Standard backshift** — matters in formal writing, narrative, and reporting past conversations generally (news reporting, storytelling, workplace communication about what was discussed).
- **Exception 1 (still-true facts)** — matters for precision and accuracy; incorrectly backshifting a still-true fact can create a false implication that it's no longer true.
- **Exception 2 (present reporting verb)** — matters constantly in real-time, casual conversation (relaying messages, texting, live discussion).

---

## 5. Frequency & Register
Reported speech is used across all registers — formal news reporting, casual gossip, professional meeting summaries, everyday "she said/he said" conversation. The backshift-exceptions are especially common and natural in casual spoken English, where speakers frequently keep present tense for still-relevant facts even without consciously "deciding" to break the rule — it just sounds right.

---

## 6. American vs. British Note
No major systematic structural difference. British English speakers may be slightly more consistent about backshifting even for still-true facts in more formal contexts, while American casual speech leans slightly more toward keeping the original tense when the fact remains current — a soft tendency, not a hard rule, and both patterns are used and understood in both varieties.

---

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

| Mistake | Why | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "She said that she is tired" (when the tiredness was specifically at the time of speaking, not still true now) | Not backshifting when it's actually needed (over-applying Exception 1 to situations where it doesn't apply) | "She said that she was tired." (backshift needed — a temporary past state, not an ongoing fact) |
| "He told me he will call me tomorrow." (in a past-tense reporting context) | Missing the will→would shift | "He told me he would call me the next day." |
| Not shifting time/place expressions ("today," "here," "tomorrow") when reporting from a different day/place | Spanish reported speech handles some of these shifts, but the systematic correspondence table isn't identical, and learners often forget several at once | Systematically check: pronoun, tense, and time/place expressions — all three need review, not just the verb tense |
| Backshifting modal verbs that don't need it: "She said she woulded come" (attempting to backshift "would" further) | Overapplying the backshift pattern to a modal that has no further past form | "She said she would come." (would stays would — it's already as far back as it goes) |

---

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ "Backshifting is always mandatory, no exceptions." — As Section 2c shows, there are genuine, systematic, meaningful exceptions, not just casual sloppiness.
- ❌ "Reported speech only involves changing tense." — Pronouns and time/place expressions need systematic adjustment too (Section 2b) — a common source of technically-tense-correct but still-wrong reported sentences.
- ❌ "That is required after 'said'/'told' in reported speech." — "That" is optional in reported speech, just like in many other clause types: "She said (that) she was tired" — both are correct, "that" is more common in formal/written contexts, frequently dropped in casual speech.

---

## 9. Pronunciation Note
"Said that" often compresses in fast speech, with "that" reduced almost to nothing when present, or dropped entirely — worth training your ear for both the presence and frequent absence of this connector in natural spoken reported speech.

---

## 10. Memory Technique
**The "one step back" image:** picture the tense grid from Lesson 6 as a staircase. Reported speech moves the original tense one step down that staircase (present → past, past → past perfect), UNLESS the fact is still standing at the top of the staircase right now (still true), in which case you can leave it where it is.

---

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern:** Reported speech = [original tense, shifted one step back on Lesson 6's grid] + [pronoun/time/place adjustments for the new speaker/time/place perspective] + [exception: no backshift needed if the statement remains true/current, or if the reporting verb itself is present-tense].

## 12. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1:**
- Direct: "I'm happy." → Reported: She said she was happy.

**Level 2 — full pronoun + time shift:**
- Direct (Maria, speaking to you, on Monday): "I'll finish this tomorrow." → Reported (you, telling someone else, on Wednesday): Maria said she would finish it the next day (i.e., Tuesday).

**Level 3 — still-true fact, no backshift:**
- Direct: "My sister lives in Paris." (still true) → Reported: He told me his sister **lives** in Paris. (present tense kept, signaling this is still accurate — backshifting to "lived" would oddly suggest she's since moved)

**Level 4 — combining exceptions and standard backshift in one passage:**
- "She called this morning and said she **was** running late because the trains **are** always unreliable on weekends." (backshift for the specific past situation "was running late," but present tense kept for the general, still-true fact about weekend trains)

## 13. Related Expressions / Terms
- **Backshift** — this lesson's central mechanism, moving tense one step back.
- **Reporting verb** — said, told, mentioned, explained, claimed, admitted, etc. (each carries slightly different connotations — worth exploring further in Phase 5, vocabulary/collocations).
- **Indirect/reported speech** vs. **direct speech** (quoted, with quotation marks).
