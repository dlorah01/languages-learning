# Lesson 14 — Modal Verbs I: Ability, Permission, Possibility (can/could/may/might)
### Phase 1: Grammar | Block B (Conditionals & Modality) | Lesson 2 of 5

---

## 0. Where this fits
This opens a four-lesson arc on modal verbs (14–17), one of the highest-value grammar areas in English because modals carry an enormous amount of nuance — certainty, politeness, obligation, deduction — packed into small, high-frequency words. Lesson 10 already previewed modal+aspect combinations; this lesson and the next three build the full system properly, starting with the most everyday modals: can, could, may, might.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation

Modal verbs are English's toolkit for expressing the speaker's **attitude** toward an action — not just "what happens," but "how possible/permitted/certain/necessary is it." Unlike ordinary verbs, modals don't change form (no -s, no -ing, no -ed) and they're always followed by the bare base verb: "she can go," never "she cans goes" or "she can to go."

This lesson covers three distinct jobs that can/could/may/might do — **ability, permission, and possibility** — and the fact that the same modal (like "could" or "might") can shift jobs depending on context is exactly why modals feel slippery to learners. We'll separate the jobs cleanly here.

---

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2a. Ability
| Modal | Time | Example |
|---|---|---|
| can | present/general ability | "I can swim." |
| could | past ability (general) | "I could swim when I was five." |
| will be able to / be able to | future/other tenses (can has no future/perfect forms) | "I'll be able to help tomorrow." / "I've been able to fix it." |

**Key limitation:** "can" and "could" only exist in present and past forms — for any other tense/aspect (future, perfect, etc.), you must switch to "be able to." This is a direct consequence of modals being a closed, grammatically limited class (more on this in Section 3).

**Important nuance — "could" for general vs. specific past ability:**
- General ability: "I could swim when I was young." ✅ (a general, ongoing past skill)
- Specific single achievement: ❌ "I could pass the exam yesterday" → ✅ "I was able to pass the exam yesterday." / "I managed to pass the exam yesterday." (for one-time, specific past accomplishments, "could" sounds wrong — use "was able to" or "managed to" instead. This is a genuinely important, frequently-missed rule.)

### 2b. Permission
| Modal | Register | Example |
|---|---|---|
| can | neutral/informal | "Can I use your phone?" |
| could | more polite | "Could I use your phone?" |
| may | formal/polite | "May I use your phone?" |

- Giving permission: "You can leave early today." / "You may leave early today." (more formal)
- Denying permission: "You can't park here." / "You may not park here." (more formal/official — often seen on signs)

### 2c. Possibility (Epistemic — about likelihood, not permission)
This is the meaning most likely to confuse Spanish speakers, because "may" and "might" here have nothing to do with permission — they express how likely something is:
| Modal | Degree of certainty |
|---|---|
| may | possible, moderate likelihood |
| might | possible, often slightly less certain/more tentative than "may" (the difference is subtle and often negligible in practice) |
| could | possible (also used for possibility, not just ability — a genuinely separate meaning from Section 2a) |

- "She may be at home." (possible she's home — a guess)
- "It might rain later." (possible, somewhat tentative)
- "He could be lying." (possible he's lying — note: totally different from "could" = ability here)

**Crucial distinction: "may not" vs. "might not" for possibility, vs. "cannot/can't" for near-impossibility:**
- "She may not come." (possible she won't come)
- "She can't be at home — I just saw her at the office." (near-certainty she's NOT home — "can't" for strong negative deduction is a distinct, important use, previewed here and covered fully in Lesson 16, Modals of Deduction)

## 3. Why Modals Behave Grammatically Differently From Normal Verbs
Modals are a genuinely special grammatical category in English (called "auxiliary modals" or simply "modals"): they never take -s, -ing, or -ed; they're always followed directly by a bare base verb (no "to"); they form questions/negatives without "do" (unlike ordinary verbs — "Can you...?" not "Do you can...?"). This is a small, closed set of words (can, could, may, might, will, would, shall, should, must) that behave uniformly this way — once you learn this behavior for one modal, it applies to all of them, which is genuinely efficient once internalized.

---

## 4. When Each Meaning/Modal Matters Most
- **Ability (can/could/be able to)** — everyday, all registers, describing skills and capabilities.
- **Permission (can/could/may)** — everyday requests; "may" specifically signals more formal/polite register (customer service, formal writing, addressing someone with respect).
- **Possibility (may/might/could)** — everyday speculation and hedging — extremely common in natural conversation ("it might rain," "she could be right") and directly connects to Phase 6's hedging-language module.

---

## 5. Frequency & Register
"Can" is by far the most frequent and neutral of these modals across all uses (ability, permission, informal possibility contexts sometimes). "Could" is very frequent for polite requests and past ability. "May" carries a distinctly more formal flavor for permission ("May I...?") and appears frequently in both formal and neutral speech for possibility. "Might" is slightly more tentative-sounding than "may" for possibility and is common in all registers.

---

## 6. American vs. British Note
No major structural differences for these specific modals between American and British English. One minor note: "may" for permission ("May I leave early?") is considered slightly more old-fashioned/formal in casual American speech compared to "can," while it remains a bit more commonly used for polite requests in British English — but this is a soft tendency, not a hard rule, and both are correct and understood everywhere.

---

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

| Mistake | Why | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "I can to swim." | Adding "to" after a modal (Spanish *puedo nadar* doesn't have this issue, but learners sometimes over-apply English infinitive patterns from elsewhere) | "I can swim." (modal + bare base verb, never "to") |
| "I could pass the exam yesterday." | Using "could" for a specific, single past achievement instead of general ability | "I was able to pass the exam yesterday." / "I managed to pass the exam yesterday." |
| "I will can help you tomorrow." | Stacking two modals (English doesn't allow two modals together) | "I will be able to help you tomorrow." |
| Confusing "may" (possibility) with "may" (permission) in context, producing ambiguous or wrong-register sentences | Both meanings share the same word, and Spanish doesn't split *poder* this exact way | Learn to distinguish by context: "May I help you?" (permission, asking) vs. "It may rain" (possibility, prediction) — same word, genuinely different job. |
| "Do you can swim?" | Applying "do"-support (needed for ordinary verbs) to a modal | "Can you swim?" (modals invert directly, no "do" needed) |

---

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ "Can, could, may, and might are basically interchangeable." — They differ in register (can vs. may for permission), certainty (may vs. might vs. could for possibility), and grammatical restriction (can/could have no future/perfect forms) — not freely interchangeable.
- ❌ "Could is just the past tense of can, full stop." — "Could" also independently expresses present possibility ("that could be true") and is used for polite requests in the present ("Could you help me?") — it's not purely a past-tense form.
- ❌ "Modals can combine with each other for extra meaning (e.g., 'might could')." — Standard English doesn't allow stacking two modals (though some regional dialects, e.g. certain Southern American English varieties, do use "might could" colloquially — this is a real dialectal feature, not standard/neutral English, and outside the scope of what you should produce).

---

## 9. Pronunciation Note
"Can" and "can't" are a critical listening distinction, especially in American English where the vowel difference is sometimes subtle in fast speech — "can" is typically unstressed and reduced (/kən/) while "can't" retains a clearer, fuller vowel and is usually stressed, plus the final /t/ (though it's sometimes dropped in casual speech, leaving the vowel length/quality as the main cue). Listening for stress and vowel length, not just the "t" sound, is the more reliable strategy.

---

## 10. Memory Technique
**Three-column mental sorting:** whenever you meet can/could/may/might in a sentence, sort it into one of three buckets — **Ability** (skill/capability), **Permission** (asking/giving/denying), or **Possibility** (guessing/predicting likelihood). This three-way sort will resolve most of the ambiguity these modals seem to create.

---

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern:** Modal + bare base verb, always, no exceptions, no "to," no -s/-ing/-ed on the modal itself. This is a single reusable rule covering all nine core English modals (can, could, may, might, will, would, shall, should, must) — Lessons 15–17 will keep reusing this exact structural pattern.

> **Pattern:** For a specific, single, completed past achievement (not general ability), "was/were able to" or "managed to" replaces "could" — this same logic will resurface with negatives too: "couldn't" IS fine for specific past failures ("I couldn't open the door" — negative doesn't have this restriction, only positive "could" for single achievements does).

---

## 12. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1:**
- Ability: "She can speak three languages."
- Permission: "Can I sit here?"

**Level 2 — could: general ability vs. specific achievement:**
- ✅ "I could run fast when I was a teenager." (general ability)
- ❌ "I could win the race last Saturday." → ✅ "I was able to win the race last Saturday." / "I managed to win the race last Saturday."

**Level 3 — may/might for possibility vs. permission, same word:**
- "You may leave whenever you're ready." (permission)
- "The meeting may be postponed due to weather." (possibility, not permission — nobody's "allowing" the meeting to be postponed)

**Level 4 — layering possibility with aspect (connecting back to Lesson 10):**
- "She might be working late tonight." (modal + continuous — a guess about an ongoing action)
- "He may have already left." (modal + perfect — a guess about a completed past action, a direct callback to Lesson 10's Section 3b preview)

---

## 13. Related Expressions / Terms
- **Epistemic modality** — the linguistic term for "possibility/certainty" meanings of modals (as opposed to **deontic modality**, covering permission/obligation — we'll use this term again in Lesson 15).
- **Be able to / manage to** — the necessary substitutes for "can/could" in tenses/contexts modals can't reach.
- **Might could** — a real dialectal (non-standard) double-modal feature, mentioned for cultural literacy, not for production.
