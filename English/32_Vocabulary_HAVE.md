# Lesson 32 — HAVE (including "have got" and causative "have")
### Phase 2: High-Frequency Verb System | Lesson 4 of 8

---

## 0. Where this fits
This lesson resolves two threads previewed earlier: "have got" for possession (Lesson 29) and the causative structure family (previewed with "get" in Lesson 29 and "make" in Lesson 31). "Have" is also central to Lesson 2's *tener*-expression pattern (tener hambre → be hungry, NOT have hunger) — this lesson clarifies exactly where "have" DOES and DOESN'T correspond to Spanish *tener*.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation

"Have" is fundamentally about **possession, relationship, or experience** — owning something, having a characteristic, or undergoing an experience. Beyond its central possession meaning, "have" also plays a crucial grammatical role as an **auxiliary verb** (perfect aspect, Lessons 7-10) and can function causatively (arranging for someone else to do something for you) — a genuinely versatile verb across both lexical and grammatical functions.

---

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2a. Possession (Core Meaning)
- "I have a car." / "She has two brothers." / "They have a big house."
- **"Have got"** — the informal British-leaning-but-universal variant for possession (Lesson 29 callback): "I've got a car." (= "I have a car")

### 2b. Have + Noun — Fixed Experience/Activity Collocations
A huge, high-value category, parallel to Lesson 30's "take" collocations:
- have breakfast/lunch/dinner (NOT "take" or "make" breakfast)
- have a shower/bath (also correct: "take a shower/bath" — both are used, "have" is more British-leaning, "take" more universal/American-leaning)
- have a good time
- have a look (also correct: "take a look" — both used interchangeably)
- have a break (also correct: "take a break")
- have a chat
- have an argument/a fight
- have a baby (= give birth)
- have a headache/a cold/a cough (physical ailments — NOT "tener" translated as some other verb; "have" is correct here)

### 2c. Have (Got) To — Obligation (Lesson 15 Callback)
- "I have to go now." / "I've got to go now." (informal variant)

### 2d. Causative HAVE — Arranging for Someone Else to Do Something
**Form:** have + object + past participle (structurally identical to Lesson 29's causative "get")
- "I had my car repaired." (= I arranged for someone to repair it — nearly identical in meaning to "I got my car repaired," though "have" can feel slightly more neutral/formal, "get" slightly more casual)
- "She had her hair cut." (arranged for a haircut)

**Have + object + base verb** (a distinct causative structure — instructing/asking someone to do something, without "to"):
- "I'll have my assistant call you." (I'll instruct my assistant to call you — note: no "to," unlike the "get someone TO do something" pattern from Lesson 29)

### 2e. Auxiliary HAVE — Perfect Aspect (Grammar Callback)
- "I have finished." / "She had already left." — this is the grammatical "have" from Lessons 7-10 and 16-17, distinct from lexical "have" (possession/experience), though they share the same word.

---

## 3. Resolving Lesson 2's *Tener*-Expression Trap
Lesson 2 established that Spanish *tener* + noun (tener hambre, tener miedo, tener frío) maps to English **"be" + adjective** (be hungry, be afraid, be cold), NOT "have" + noun. This lesson adds the necessary nuance: "have" + noun IS correct for genuine possession/experience nouns (have breakfast, have a headache, have a car) — the trap is specifically about the *tener*-for-physical/emotional-states pattern, not about "have" in general. The key diagnostic: if the Spanish sentence describes an internal state/feeling (hambre, miedo, frío, sueño, razón), use "be" + adjective; if it describes an actual possession or a concrete experience/event (un carro, un dolor de cabeza, un buen tiempo), "have" + noun is correct.

---

## 4. Why Native Speakers Use Causative HAVE/GET Almost Interchangeably (With Subtle Differences)
Both causative "have" and causative "get" describe arranging for someone else to perform an action, but "have" tends to feel slightly more neutral/formal/administrative (having a service performed, as if it's a routine arrangement), while "get" tends to feel slightly more casual or can imply more effort/persuasion involved in arranging it — a subtle register/connotation difference rather than a strict grammatical rule.

---

## 5. When Each Meaning Matters Most
- **Possession + "have got"** — everyday, all registers, essential basic vocabulary.
- **Have + noun collocations (Section 2b)** — extremely common in daily routine descriptions; a genuine parallel category to Lesson 30's take-collocations, equally worth memorizing as fixed chunks.
- **Causative have** — valuable for describing services/arrangements, slightly more formal-leaning than causative "get."

---

## 6. Frequency & Register
"Have got" for possession is very common in British English across registers and used in American English too, though American casual speech sometimes favors plain "have" alone slightly more. "Have" + noun collocations (breakfast, a look, a break) are used across all registers. Causative "have" appears in both everyday and professional/formal contexts, often slightly more formal-sounding than causative "get" in service-related contexts ("I'll have my secretary send you the documents" sounds slightly more formal/professional than "I'll get my secretary to send you the documents").

---

## 7. American vs. British Note
- "Have got" (possession) — used in both, with British English leaning on it more heavily in everyday speech; American English often uses plain "have" more, especially in more neutral/written contexts.
- "Have a shower/bath" vs. "take a shower/bath" — "have" is distinctly more British; "take" is the dominant American form, though both are understood everywhere.
- The past participle "have got" itself (not "gotten") is consistent for possession in BOTH varieties — this doesn't split along the get/gotten line from Lesson 29, since "have got" for possession isn't using "got" as a true past participle of an action, but as a fixed idiomatic possession marker.

---

## 8. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

| Mistake | Why | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "I have hunger." / "I have fear." | Direct translation of *tener hambre/miedo* — the classic Lesson 2 trap, resurfacing here in a new lesson for reinforcement | "I'm hungry." / "I'm afraid." (be + adjective, NOT have + noun, for internal states) |
| "I have 25 years." | Same *tener*-trap pattern, for age specifically | "I'm 25 (years old)." |
| Avoiding "have" + noun collocations entirely, missing natural chunks like "have breakfast," "have a good time" | Not recognizing this as a distinct, legitimate "have" category, separate from the *tener*-trap | Learn "have + noun" experience/activity collocations directly as fixed chunks (Section 2b) |
| "I make my hair cut." / "I do my hair cut." (attempting causative meaning with the wrong verb) | Not knowing the specific causative "have/get + object + past participle" structure | "I had my hair cut." / "I got my hair cut." |

---

## 9. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ "Have is always wrong for Spanish tener-expressions, in every context." — This overcorrects Lesson 2's lesson; "have" IS correct for genuine possession and experience nouns (have a car, have breakfast, have a headache) — the trap is specifically about internal state/feeling nouns (hunger, fear, cold), not about "have" as a whole.
- ❌ "Have got and have are always perfectly interchangeable in every context and register." — "Have got" is specifically for possession/relationship in the present; it doesn't work the same way for habitual actions or in past/future tenses the way plain "have" does ("I had a car" — NOT typically "I had got a car" for simple past possession in most contexts).
- ❌ "Causative have and causative get always mean exactly the same thing with zero difference." — There's a subtle register/connotation difference (have = more neutral/formal-administrative; get = more casual/effort-implying), even though the core meaning is very similar.

---

## 10. Pronunciation Note
"Have got" contracts to "'ve got" in speech ("I've got," "she's got") and is rarely pronounced with a full, separate "have" in natural fast speech — worth training your ear for the contracted form as the default expectation, not the exception.

---

## 11. Memory Technique
**Return to Lesson 2's *tener*-diagnostic, refined:** ask "Is this describing an internal state/feeling (hunger, fear, cold, age, sleepiness) or a genuine possession/experience (a car, breakfast, a headache, a good time)?" Internal state → "be" + adjective. Possession/experience → "have" + noun. This refined two-branch diagnostic resolves the apparent tension between Lesson 2's original rule and this lesson's legitimate "have + noun" category.

---

## 12. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern:** Causative structures across GET, MAKE (causative reaction, not arrangement), and HAVE all share the underlying idea of one person causing an effect/action via another person or force — but each has its own specific grammatical shape (get + obj + to-infinitive/past participle/verb-ing; have + obj + past participle/base verb; make + obj + adjective/base verb for reactions) that must be learned individually, even though the conceptual category (causative verbs) is shared.

---

## 13. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1:**
- "I have a dog." (possession)
- "I've got a dog." (informal possession variant)

**Level 2 — resolving the *tener* trap directly, side by side:**
- "I have a headache." (have + noun — legitimate experience/ailment collocation)
- "I'm hungry." (NOT "I have hunger" — internal state uses be + adjective)

**Level 3 — causative have vs. causative get, subtle register difference:**
- "I had the report reviewed by legal before sending it." (slightly more formal/administrative-feeling)
- "I got the report reviewed by legal before sending it." (slightly more casual, same core meaning)

**Level 4 — combining lexical, auxiliary, and causative HAVE in one sentence:**
- "I've already had my car serviced twice this year, and I have to say, I've never had such reliable service before." (auxiliary "have" for perfect aspect, causative "had," lexical "have to" for obligation, auxiliary "have" again for perfect aspect — four distinct HAVE functions in one natural sentence)
