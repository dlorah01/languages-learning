# Lesson 72 — Professional Email Writing
### Phase 9: Writing | Module 1 of 4

---

## 0. Where this fits
Phase 8 gave you spoken fluency and the "translated vs. natural" diagnostic. Phase 9 moves it all to the page. Writing gives you something speaking never does: **time**. You can plan, choose, and edit. So the standard is higher, and readers notice when a text sounds foreign.

Email is the most common professional writing task, and it is where Spanish-shaped phrasing shows up most: formulaic openings (*Por medio de la presente…*), heavy closings (*Sin otro particular, me despido atentamente*), and literal requests (*I'm waiting for your news*). In English these read as stiff, long, or odd.

Connections:
- **Lesson 71:** the five levels of "translated" (word, collocation, grammar, pragmatics, rhythm) apply directly to emails.
- **Lessons 59, 61:** hedging and polite disagreement are the engine of email tone.
- **Phase 4:** phrasal verbs (*follow up, get back to, look into, sort out*) are the natural verbs of workplace email.
- **Phase 5:** business vocabulary and register (Lessons 56–57).
- **Lesson 73 (next):** academic writing, the formal end of the spectrum.
- **Lesson 75:** tone, style, and clarity editing, which you will use to polish emails.

> **Core idea:** A good professional email is **short, specific, and easy to act on**. The reader should know within five seconds **who you are, what you want, and by when.** Warmth comes from a few well-chosen phrases, not from length.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
Think of an email as a **busy colleague at a doorway**. They have 30 seconds. If you say, "Hi! Quick question: could you send me the Q3 numbers by Thursday? I need them for Friday's meeting," they can answer in one line. If you start with three sentences of background, they get lost and answer later or not at all.

English professional culture usually values:
- **Clarity over ceremony.** Get to the point, politely.
- **Action.** Make it obvious what you are asking and when.
- **Courtesy in small doses.** *Thanks*, *Could you*, *I appreciate it* do the work that formal phrases do in Spanish.
- **Short paragraphs.** White space is a kindness.

A second image: **a postcard, not a letter.** Say the main thing, add a warm touch, sign off.

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 Anatomy of an email
```
Subject:   Specific, action-oriented (what + when)
Greeting:  Hi Ana, / Dear Ms. Lopez,
Opening:   1 short line (thanks / context / purpose)
Body:      The key information, short paragraphs or bullets
The ask:   What you need, from whom, by when
Closing:   1 line (thanks / next step / offer to help)
Sign-off:  Best regards, / Thanks, / Kind regards,
Name:      Your full name
Signature: Title, company, phone (optional)
```

### 2.2 Subject lines: the most important line
A good subject line lets the reader **decide what to do before opening the email.**

| ❌ Weak | ✅ Strong | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Hello | Question about invoice #4521 | specific |
| Meeting | Meeting request: project kickoff, Tue 12 Nov | topic + date |
| Urgent!!! | Action needed by Friday: budget approval | clear action and deadline |
| Information | Could you send the Q3 sales figures? | states the ask |
| Re: Re: Re: Project | Update on the Bogotá project: delay of one week | new subject for a new topic |
| Follow up | Following up: proposal sent on 3 Nov | context |
| Application | Application: Marketing Analyst (Ref. 2231) | role + reference |

**Useful patterns:** *Question about…, Request: …, Action required: …, Follow-up: …, Update: …, Invitation: …, Thank you for…, FYI: …, Reminder: … due [date].*

**Rules:** keep it under about 50 characters when possible (mobile screens), put the key word first, avoid ALL CAPS and multiple exclamation marks.

### 2.3 Greetings
| Situation | Greeting | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Colleague, first name basis | **Hi Ana,** / **Hello Ana,** | most common; "Hey" only with close teammates |
| Group | **Hi all,** / **Hi team,** / **Hello everyone,** | |
| Someone senior or new contact | **Dear Ms. Lopez,** / **Dear Dr. Smith,** | use *Ms.* (not *Miss/Mrs.*) unless told otherwise |
| Unknown name | **Dear Hiring Manager,** / **Dear [Company] Team,** | avoid *Dear Sir/Madam* (dated) |
| Formal letter-like email | **Dear Mr. Brown,** | |
| Reply in a thread | **Hi Ana,** or just **Thanks, Ana,** | threads get less formal as they continue |
| Very generic | *To whom it may concern,* | impersonal; use only if you truly can't find a name |

**Match the other person.** If they sign "Mike," write "Hi Mike," even if you started with "Dear Mr. Brown."

### 2.4 Opening lines
You don't need a long introduction. Pick one short line.

| Purpose | Opening |
|---|---|
| Neutral greeting | *I hope you're doing well.* / *I hope you had a good weekend.* |
| Replying | *Thanks for your email.* / *Thanks for getting back to me.* / *Thank you for your quick reply.* |
| First contact | *My name is Ana Ruiz, and I work in the marketing team at ABC.* |
| Stating purpose | *I'm writing to ask about…* / *I'm writing regarding…* / *I'm reaching out to…* |
| Following up | *I'm following up on my email from last week.* / *Just checking in on…* |
| Referral | *[Name] suggested I get in touch.* |
| Quick note | *Just a quick note to say…* |
| Apology | *Apologies for the delay in getting back to you.* |

**Avoid** the cliché *I hope this email finds you well* (it is common but reads as automatic). *I hope you're doing well* is lighter.

### 2.5 Making a request
Use the polite frames from Lesson 71, and **state the deadline**.

| Level | Phrase | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Neutral | **Could you send me…?** | most workplace requests |
| Softer | **Would you be able to…?** | slightly more polite |
| Very polite | **I was wondering if you could…** | to someone senior or when imposing |
| Direct with courtesy | **Please send me… by Friday.** | clear instructions to a peer or junior |
| Formal | **I would appreciate it if you could…** | formal contexts |
| Soft deadline | **When you get a chance, could you…?** | non-urgent |
| Firm deadline | **I need this by Thursday at 5 pm.** / **Could you get this to me by Thursday?** | urgent |
| Asking for information | **Could you let me know…?** / **Do you know whether…?** | |
| Asking for confirmation | **Could you confirm that…?** | |

**Structure of a good request:** *Context (one line) + Ask + Deadline + Thanks.*
> *I'm preparing the Q3 report. Could you send me the sales figures by Thursday? Thanks so much.*

### 2.6 Giving information and updates
- **Bottom line up front:** state the key result first, then details.
- **Use bullets** for 3+ items or steps.
- **Numbers and dates:** write them clearly (*Tuesday 12 November, 3:00 pm Colombia time (UTC-5)*).
- **Attachments:** say what is attached and why.

| Purpose | Phrase |
|---|---|
| Attaching | *I've attached the report.* / *Attached is the revised draft.* / *Please find attached…* (formal) |
| Linking | *You can find it here: [link].* |
| Summarizing | *In short, …* / *The main points are…* / *To summarize,…* |
| Reporting progress | *We've completed…, and we're now working on…* |
| Bad news | *Unfortunately,… / I'm afraid…* |
| Clarifying | *Just to clarify,…* / *To be clear,…* |
| Pointing to a section | *See the second paragraph.* / *as discussed in the meeting* |

### 2.7 Follow-ups and reminders
Following up is normal. Make it friendly and easy to reply to.

| Time since first email | Phrase |
|---|---|
| 2–3 days | *Just checking in on my email below.* |
| About 1 week | *I wanted to follow up on…* / *Just following up on…* |
| Longer | *I wanted to circle back on this.* / *Gentle reminder that the deadline is Friday.* |
| Final | *I'll assume…unless I hear otherwise.* / *Could you let me know by Friday so I can plan?* |

Add value: re-state the ask in one sentence so the reader doesn't have to scroll.

### 2.8 Apologizing, declining, and delivering bad news
| Situation | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Apologizing for a delay | **Apologies for + noun / -ing** | *Apologies for the late reply.* / *Sorry for taking so long to respond.* |
| Admitting a mistake | **I'm sorry about + the mix-up. Here's the corrected version.** | |
| Declining politely | **Thank you for + noun. Unfortunately, + reason. + alternative.** | *Thank you for the invitation. Unfortunately, I won't be able to attend. Could we meet next week instead?* |
| Saying no to a request | **I'm afraid I can't + verb. + help with alternative.** | *I'm afraid I can't approve this budget, but I can recommend…* |
| Delaying | **We need a bit more time.** | *We'll need until Friday to complete the review.* |
| Rescheduling | **Would it be possible to move…?** | *Would it be possible to move the meeting to Thursday?* |
| Complaining | **I'm writing to raise a concern about…** | calm, factual, specific |

**Tip:** apologize once, briefly, then move to the solution. Don't over-apologize.

### 2.9 Closing lines
| Purpose | Closing |
|---|---|
| Thanking | *Thanks again for your help.* / *Thank you for your time.* |
| Offering help | *Let me know if you have any questions.* / *Please let me know if you need anything else.* |
| Looking ahead | *Looking forward to hearing from you.* / *I look forward to your reply.* |
| Next step | *I'll send the updated file tomorrow.* / *Talk to you on Thursday.* |
| Warm | *Have a great weekend!* / *Hope the rest of your week goes well.* |
| Formal | *I would be grateful for your confirmation.* |

Choose **one** closing line, not three.

### 2.10 Sign-offs
| Sign-off | Register | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Best regards,** | neutral-formal | safe everywhere |
| **Kind regards,** | neutral-formal | slightly warmer (common in UK) |
| **Regards,** | neutral | can sound curt; fine in short threads |
| **Best,** | neutral-friendly | very common in US |
| **Thanks,** / **Thanks so much,** | friendly | when you've made a request |
| **Many thanks,** | friendly-formal | common in UK |
| **Sincerely,** | formal | US letters/formal emails |
| **Yours sincerely,** | formal | UK, when the name is known (*Dear Mr. Smith*) |
| **Yours faithfully,** | formal | UK, with *Dear Sir/Madam* |
| **Cheers,** | informal | UK/Australia; casual |
| **Warm regards,** | warm | good for relationships |
| **Take care,** | informal-warm | |

**Avoid** translations like *Cordially, Greetings, Without more, I say goodbye, Attentively, Saludos* (unless used playfully with someone who knows Spanish).

### 2.11 Tone: friendly, professional, and clear
**The tone dial**
| Dial | Features |
|---|---|
| More formal | no contractions, *Dear*, *I would appreciate*, *Kindly*, longer sentences |
| Neutral professional | some contractions, *Hi*, *Could you*, *Thanks* |
| More casual | *Hey*, short sentences, *Thanks!*, emojis (if culture allows) |

**Tone tools**
- **Please/thank you:** one or two, not many.
- **Modals:** *could, would, might* soften.
- **Hedges:** *a bit, a little, I think, it seems* (Lesson 59).
- **Positive framing:** *Here's what we can do* vs. *We can't do that.*
- **Exclamation marks:** one per email, maximum, to show warmth. More can seem excitable or unprofessional (norms vary).
- **Emojis:** only if the other person uses them.
- **Capitals/bold:** avoid shouting; use bold sparingly for key dates.
- **Humor:** use carefully across cultures.

### 2.12 Structure and layout
- **Short paragraphs** (1–3 sentences).
- **One topic per email**, one main ask. Multiple unrelated topics → separate emails.
- **Bullets or numbers** for lists, steps, and questions.
- **Bold** the deadline or the key question.
- **Length:** most emails should fit on one screen without scrolling.
- **Front-load:** put the important information in the first two lines.
- **Close the loop:** end with what happens next.

### 2.13 Email mechanics and etiquette
| Item | Guideline |
|---|---|
| **To / Cc / Bcc** | *To*: people who must act. *Cc*: people who should know. *Bcc*: hide recipients (privacy, large mailings). |
| **Reply vs. Reply All** | Reply all only when everyone needs it. |
| **Forwarding** | Add a line saying why and what you need (*Forwarding for your input on…*). |
| **Attachments** | Mention them in the text; check you attached them. |
| **Names** | Spell correctly; use the name the person uses. |
| **Time zones** | State them (*10 am Bogotá time (UTC-5)*). |
| **Response time** | Common expectation: within about one business day; say *I'll get back to you by…* if you need longer. |
| **Out of office** | State dates, who to contact, and when you'll reply. |
| **Subject changes** | Change the subject line when the topic changes. |
| **Privacy** | Don't put sensitive data (passwords, IDs) in plain email. |
| **Proofread** | Check names, dates, numbers, attachments before sending. |

**Common abbreviations (professional use)**
| Abbreviation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| FYI | for your information |
| ASAP | as soon as possible (use carefully; can sound pushy) |
| EOD / COB | end of day / close of business |
| OOO | out of office |
| TBC / TBD | to be confirmed / to be determined |
| NB | nota bene (note) (UK) |
| RSVP | please reply |
| cc / bcc | carbon copy / blind carbon copy |
| WFH | working from home |
| ETA | estimated time of arrival |
| PFA | please find attached (common in some regions; fine, but "attached is…" is more natural in US/UK) |

### 2.14 Cultural notes on directness and warmth
- Many English-language workplaces prefer **brief and direct** emails, with a friendly line or two.
- A very formal, ceremonial email may look distant or old-fashioned.
- A very blunt email (*Send the report.*) can look rude.
- Mid-point: **polite request + specific ask + thanks.**
- In many Latin American business contexts, emails are often more formal and relationship-oriented. You can keep warmth (one personal line) while shortening the rest.
- Hierarchy matters in different degrees: in US workplaces, first names are common even with senior people; in some UK, Indian, or other contexts, titles last longer. When in doubt, mirror the other person.

## 3. Why Native Speakers Do This
1. **Time pressure.** People receive dozens of emails per day, so short and specific messages get answered.
2. **Action orientation.** Workplace English is built around "who does what by when."
3. **Politeness through form.** Modals and thanks signal respect without long ceremony.
4. **Mobile reading.** Many emails are read on phones; short lines and bullets help.
5. **Record-keeping.** Emails are evidence; clarity (dates, numbers, decisions) matters.
6. **Relationship maintenance.** A friendly line (*I hope you're well*) keeps the tone human.
7. **Informality creep.** Workplace norms have moved toward less formal salutations and sign-offs over recent decades, especially in tech and startups; traditional sectors (law, finance, government) stay more formal.

## 4. When to Use / When Not To

**Use email when**
- you need a written record;
- the message is non-urgent and detailed;
- you want the reader to act in their own time;
- you share files or links;
- you contact someone for the first time.

**Don't use email (or reconsider) when**
- the matter is urgent (call or message);
- the topic is emotionally sensitive or complex (call or meet);
- many back-and-forth replies are needed (quick call);
- you are angry (write a draft, wait, then edit or call);
- the information is confidential and the channel is not secure.

**Register choices**
| Reader | Style |
|---|---|
| Close teammate | *Hi Sam, quick question…* |
| Manager | *Hi Laura, could you take a look at…* |
| Client | *Dear Mr. Chen,… Best regards* |
| Senior executive | *Dear Ms. Alvarez,… Kind regards* (short, to the point) |
| Unknown contact | *Dear Hiring Manager,…* |
| Mass email | *Hi all,… (clear subject, short)* |

## 5. Frequency & Priority

**Priorities for you**
1. **Subject line and the ask:** clear and specific.
2. **Polite request frames** (*Could you…, Would you be able to…*).
3. **Short opening and closing** (avoid translated formulas).
4. **Greeting and sign-off** matched to the reader.
5. **Deadlines, dates, and numbers** unambiguous.
6. **Natural verbs:** *follow up, get back to, look into, send over, reach out, loop in, set up.*
7. **Short paragraphs and bullets.**
8. **Proofreading** (names, attachments, tone).

**Frequent workplace phrasal verbs and phrases**
| Phrase | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *get back to (someone)* | reply later |
| *follow up (on)* | continue contact about |
| *reach out (to)* | contact |
| *loop (someone) in* | include in the communication |
| *touch base* | have a brief contact |
| *look into (something)* | investigate |
| *send over* | send |
| *set up (a meeting)* | arrange |
| *push back (a date)* | postpone |
| *bring forward* | make earlier |
| *run (something) by (someone)* | ask for opinion |
| *sign off on* | approve |
| *keep (someone) posted* | give updates |
| *take (something) offline* | discuss separately |

(Use these in moderation; clichés like *circle back* and *synergy* can become jargon.)

## 6. American vs. British Notes
| Feature | US | UK |
|---|---|---|
| Greeting | *Hi Ana,* (very common); *Hello Ana,* | *Hi Ana,* / *Dear Ana,* (a bit more formal use persists) |
| Formal sign-off | *Sincerely,* / *Best regards,* | *Yours sincerely,* / *Yours faithfully,* / *Kind regards,* |
| Casual sign-off | *Best,* / *Thanks,* | *Cheers,* / *Many thanks,* / *Best wishes,* |
| Directness | direct, friendly, efficient | more indirect, understatement: *I wonder if you could possibly…* |
| Thanks | *Thanks!* / *Thanks in advance* (some find it presumptuous) | *Many thanks* / *Thanks very much* |
| Dates | 11/12/2026 = November 12 | 11/12/2026 = 11 December |
| Time | *3 pm EST* | *3pm GMT/BST* |
| Spelling | *organize, color, center* | *organise, colour, centre* |
| Titles | *Ms., Mr., Dr.* (with period) | *Ms, Mr, Dr* (often no period) |
| Words | *Please advise* (formal), *vacation, fall* | *Please let me know, holiday, autumn* |
| Out-of-office | *I'm out of the office until…* | *I am on annual leave until…* |

**Date tip:** write dates as **12 November 2026** or **Nov 12, 2026** to avoid confusion between US and UK formats (and with Colombia's day/month/year).

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

### 7.1 Translated phrases
| ❌ Translated | ✅ Natural | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| I'm writing to you for to inform you that… | I'm writing to let you know that… | *para + infinitive* transfer |
| Please be informed that… | (usually just state it) / Just to let you know,… | legalistic |
| Without more, I say goodbye. | Best regards, | *Sin otro particular* |
| Hoping your answer, I say goodbye. | Looking forward to hearing from you. | |
| I'm waiting for your news. | I look forward to hearing from you. / Let me know when you hear anything. | |
| Cordial greeting, | Best regards, | *Cordial saludo* |
| Attentively, | Sincerely, / Best regards, | *Atentamente* |
| I send you attached the file. | I've attached the file. / Attached is the file. | |
| Attached I send you the report. | I've attached the report. | |
| I remit you the document. | I'm sending you the document. | |
| I answer you. | I'm writing in response to your email. / Thanks for your email. | |
| Confirm me, please. | Could you confirm, please? / Could you confirm that…? | |
| Tell me if you have doubts. | Let me know if you have any questions. | *dudas* → *questions* |
| I have a doubt. | I have a question. | |
| Remember me the meeting. | Remind me about the meeting. | *remember* vs *remind* |
| I want to make you a question. | I'd like to ask you a question. | |
| Please, kindly, if you can… | Could you…? | too many softeners |
| Do the needful. | Please take care of this. / Please handle this. | dated/regional |
| Revert back to me. | Get back to me. / Reply to me. | *revert* regional use |
| In relation with your email… | Regarding your email… / In response to your email… | |
| As per your request… | As requested,… / As you asked,… | stiff |
| I'm available for any doubt. | Please let me know if you have any questions. | |
| I hope your answer soon. | I look forward to your reply. | *esperar* |
| Congratulations by your promotion. | Congratulations on your promotion! | preposition |
| Thank you for your attention. | Thank you for your time. / Thanks for your help. | |

### 7.2 Structure and tone mistakes
| ❌ Habit | Effect | ✅ Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Long formal opening paragraph | reader loses interest | one short line, then the point |
| Multiple requests in one long paragraph | actions get missed | bullets, one clear ask each |
| No deadline | no action | state a date/time |
| Vague subject line | ignored | specific and action-oriented |
| Very formal for a colleague | distant | *Hi* + first name |
| Too casual for a client | unprofessional | *Dear Ms. X* + neutral tone |
| Overusing *please* and *kindly* | sounds nervous or demanding | one polite frame |
| Blunt imperative (*Send me…*) | sounds rude | *Could you send me…?* |
| Overusing exclamation marks | unprofessional | at most one |
| Writing "Dear friend" | odd | use the name |
| Using ALL CAPS | sounds like shouting | use bold sparingly |
| Not mentioning attachments | confusion | say what's attached |
| Not proofreading names/dates | embarrassing | check before sending |
| Putting everything in the attachment | reader must open | summarize in the body |
| Apologizing repeatedly | looks unsure | apologize once, give solution |

### 7.3 Grammar traps in emails
| ❌ | ✅ |
|---|---|
| I would like to know if you can to send me… | I'd like to know if you can send me… |
| Could you to send me…? | Could you send me…? |
| I wanted to ask you if is possible… | I wanted to ask if it's possible… |
| I look forward to hear from you. | I look forward to **hearing** from you. |
| Thank you for inform me. | Thank you for **informing** me. / Thanks for letting me know. |
| Sorry for the inconvenients. | Sorry for the inconvenience. |
| Please find attach… | Please find **attached**… |
| The meeting is in Monday. | The meeting is **on** Monday. |
| I'm agree with the proposal. | I agree with the proposal. |
| Informations | information (uncountable) |
| Feedbacks | feedback (uncountable) |
| Best regard | Best regards |

### 7.4 Attitude traps
- **"A formal email must be long."** In English, formal usually means precise and polite, not long.
- **"If I don't use 'please' everywhere, I'm rude."** Modals (*could, would*) carry the politeness.
- **"I should apologize for my English."** Usually unnecessary; clarity matters more.
- **"I'll write it in Spanish and translate."** This produces translated phrasing; plan in English with chunks.

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ **"Formal English means complicated words."** — Plain words are professional.
- ❌ **"Dear Sir or Madam is always safe."** — It sounds dated; find a name or title.
- ❌ **"Informal means unprofessional."** — Neutral-friendly is standard in most workplaces.
- ❌ **"Contractions are inappropriate in email."** — They are normal in neutral professional email; avoid them in very formal letters.
- ❌ **"Thanks in advance is always polite."** — Some readers find it presumptuous; *Thanks for your help* is safer.
- ❌ **"ASAP is always acceptable."** — It can sound pushy; give a date instead.
- ❌ **"I should write long emails to show effort."** — Short, clear emails are valued.
- ❌ **"Exclamation marks look friendly."** — One is fine; many look unprofessional.
- ❌ **"Reply All is safer."** — It often annoys people.
- ❌ **"Emojis are unprofessional everywhere."** — In some workplaces they are normal; follow the culture.
- ❌ **"English emails need the same ceremony as Spanish ones."** — Norms differ; shorter is common.
- ❌ **"Bcc is the same as Cc."** — Bcc hides recipients.
- ❌ **"Yours faithfully and Yours sincerely are interchangeable."** — Faithfully for *Dear Sir/Madam*, sincerely for a named person (UK convention).

## 9. Pronunciation Note → Training Method (for writing)

### 9.1 The five-step email routine
1. **Purpose in one sentence:** *I want [person] to [action] by [date].*
2. **Draft** with the anatomy template (2.1).
3. **Cut:** remove any sentence that doesn't help the reader act.
4. **Check:** subject, greeting, ask, deadline, attachments, tone, names.
5. **Read aloud** once (rhythm and tone; Lesson 66): if it sounds like a speech, simplify.

### 9.2 The 5-second test
Show the email to someone for five seconds. Can they say who it's from, what you want, and by when? If not, rewrite.

### 9.3 Template bank
Keep templates for recurring emails (Section 12) in a document, with blanks. Adapt rather than translating from scratch.

### 9.4 Rewrite practice
Take emails you've written (or sent in Spanish) and rewrite them using the **ask first** method. Compare lengths.

### 9.5 Read the mailbox
Collect 20 emails from native speakers (colleagues, newsletters, customer service). Highlight openings, requests, closings. Add the best to your chunk notebook.

### 9.6 Tone check
Read the email as if you were the recipient: busy, a bit tired, unsure of your intentions. Does anything sound blunt, cold, or demanding? Adjust one phrase.

### 9.7 The "delay and edit" rule
For difficult emails (complaints, refusals, apologies), write a draft, wait an hour, and reread. Remove emotion; keep facts and next steps.

### 9.8 Proofing checklist
- [ ] Right recipient(s), Cc/Bcc?
- [ ] Subject line specific?
- [ ] Greeting matches the person?
- [ ] Ask and deadline clear?
- [ ] Attachments included and mentioned?
- [ ] Dates/times/time zones correct?
- [ ] Names spelled correctly?
- [ ] Tone appropriate?
- [ ] No translated formulas?
- [ ] Spell-check done?

### 9.9 Feedback loop
Once a month, ask a colleague or teacher to review one real email and mark anything unusual. Add corrections to your chunk notebook.

### 9.10 Trouble spots
- Starting with a long background.
- Forgetting the deadline.
- Translating Spanish formulas.
- Over-formality or over-casualness.
- Sending without proofreading.

## 10. Memory Technique

### 10.1 The "WHO–WHAT–WHEN" test
Every email must answer: **Who am I? What do I want? When do I need it?**

### 10.2 "Hi – Why – Ask – Thanks"
A simple skeleton: **Hi** (greeting), **Why** (one line of context), **Ask** (specific request + date), **Thanks** (closing).

### 10.3 The doorway image
Imagine delivering the message to a busy person at a doorway. Say it in 30 seconds. Write that.

### 10.4 Chunk families
Learn requests, follow-ups, apologies, and closings as families (Sections 2.5–2.10), not word by word.

### 10.5 Mirror the sender
When replying, copy the formality level, greeting style, and length of the other person (without copying mistakes).

### 10.6 Replace Spanish formulas
Make a two-column card: **Por medio de la presente → I'm writing to…**; **Sin otro particular → Best regards**; **Quedo atento → I look forward to hearing from you / Let me know.**

### 10.7 Spaced retrieval cards
- *Three things every email should make clear?* → **who, what, when**
- *Neutral request frame?* → **Could you…?**
- *Natural way to say "Quedo atento"?* → **I look forward to hearing from you. / Let me know.**
- *Attach a file naturally?* → **I've attached… / Attached is…**
- *Formal UK sign-off with a named person?* → **Yours sincerely,**
- *Fix "I have a doubt."* → **I have a question.**
- *Reply All when?* → **only when everyone needs it**
- *Date format safe for both US and UK?* → **12 November 2026**
Review on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30.

### 10.8 Weekly practice

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1: Specific subject → greeting → one-line opening → ask + deadline → closing → sign-off.**

> **Pattern 2: Modals soften requests: *Could you…? Would you be able to…?***

> **Pattern 3: One email, one main topic, one main ask.**

> **Pattern 4: Bottom line first, details after.**

> **Pattern 5: Short paragraphs, bullets for lists, bold for deadlines.**

> **Pattern 6: Mirror the other person's formality.**

> **Pattern 7: Everyday verbs beat formal ones (*send, ask, get, tell*).**

> **Pattern 8: Apologize once; offer the solution.**

> **Pattern 9: End with a next step or thanks, not a ceremony.**

> **Pattern 10: Spanish formulas don't travel; use English chunks.**

> **Pattern 11: Check names, dates, numbers, attachments.**

> **Pattern 12: Warmth = one human line, not length.**

> **Connections:** Lesson 59 (hedging), Lesson 61 (diplomatic pushback), Lesson 71 (natural vs. translated), Phase 4 (phrasal verbs), Phase 5 (business vocabulary), Lesson 73 (formal academic writing), Lesson 75 (tone and clarity editing).

## 12. Real-Life Examples (Templates)

### 12.1 Making a request
> **Subject:** Request: Q3 sales figures by Thursday
> Hi Carlos,
> I hope you're doing well. I'm preparing the Q3 report for Friday's meeting. Could you send me the final sales figures by Thursday at noon?
> Thanks so much for your help.
> Best,
> Ana

### 12.2 Following up
> **Subject:** Following up: proposal sent on 3 November
> Hi Ms. Chen,
> I wanted to follow up on the proposal I sent last week. Have you had a chance to look at it? I'd be happy to answer any questions or set up a short call.
> Thank you for your time.
> Kind regards,
> Ana Ruiz

### 12.3 Scheduling a meeting
> **Subject:** Meeting request: project kickoff
> Hi all,
> I'd like to schedule a kickoff meeting for the new project. Would any of these times work?
> - Tuesday 12 November, 10:00 am (Bogotá time, UTC-5)
> - Wednesday 13 November, 2:00 pm
> Please let me know which works best by Friday.
> Thanks,
> Ana

### 12.4 Apologizing for a delay
> **Subject:** Re: Invoice #4521
> Hi Mr. Lopez,
> Apologies for the delay in getting back to you. I've checked the invoice, and the correct amount is $1,250. I've attached the corrected version.
> Please let me know if you have any questions.
> Best regards,
> Ana

### 12.5 Declining politely
> **Subject:** Re: Invitation to the conference
> Dear Dr. Smith,
> Thank you very much for the invitation. Unfortunately, I won't be able to attend because of a prior commitment. I'd be glad to receive the slides afterward, if possible.
> Thanks again, and best wishes for the event.
> Kind regards,
> Ana Ruiz

### 12.6 Delivering bad news
> **Subject:** Update: project delay
> Hi team,
> I'm afraid we'll need an extra week to finish the testing. The delivery date will move from 15 November to 22 November. We're working to keep further changes to a minimum, and I'll send a progress update on Monday.
> Thanks for your understanding.
> Best,
> Ana

### 12.7 Introducing yourself (first contact)
> **Subject:** Introduction: Ana Ruiz (referred by Laura Pérez)
> Dear Mr. Brown,
> Laura Pérez suggested I get in touch. I work in marketing at ABC and I'm exploring ways to collaborate with your team on regional campaigns. Would you have 15 minutes for a call next week?
> Thank you for considering it.
> Best regards,
> Ana Ruiz
> Marketing Manager, ABC

### 12.8 Thanking
> **Subject:** Thank you
> Hi Sam,
> Thanks so much for your help with the presentation yesterday. Your feedback on the slides made a big difference, and the client loved it.
> I really appreciate it.
> Best,
> Ana

### 12.9 Raising a concern / complaint
> **Subject:** Issue with order #8830
> Dear Customer Service Team,
> I'm writing about order #8830, which I received on 5 November. One item was damaged in transit (photo attached). Could you arrange a replacement or a refund? I'd appreciate a reply by Friday.
> Thank you for your help.
> Kind regards,
> Ana Ruiz

### 12.10 Out-of-office message
> **Subject:** Out of office until 20 November
> Thank you for your email. I'm out of the office until 20 November, with limited access to email. For urgent matters, please contact Carlos Díaz (carlos.diaz@abc.com). I'll reply when I return.
> Best regards,
> Ana Ruiz

### 12.11 Before and after
> ❌ *Dear Sir, By means of the present I inform you that I am interested in the vacancy published in your page. I send you attached my CV. I remain attentive to your news. Without more, I say goodbye. Attentively, Ana Ruiz.*
> ✅ *Dear Hiring Manager, I'm writing to apply for the Marketing Analyst position advertised on your website. I've attached my CV and look forward to hearing from you. Best regards, Ana Ruiz*

## 13. Collocations & Related Expressions

**Email vocabulary**
- *send / write / draft / compose / reply to / forward / delete / archive / flag an email*
- *a subject line, a greeting, a sign-off, a signature, an attachment, a thread, an inbox, a draft, a reply, a follow-up, a reminder, a template*
- *cc someone, bcc someone, loop someone in, copy someone in, reply all, forward to*
- *get back to someone, follow up on something, reach out to someone, touch base with someone, circle back to something*
- *send over, send out, send off, send back*
- *attach a file, open an attachment, download, upload, share a link*
- *proofread, double-check, edit, tidy up, tighten, polish*
- *clear / concise / polite / professional / courteous / blunt / curt / stiff / rambling*
- *a quick reply, a prompt response, a detailed reply, a brief note*

**Collocations**
- *send an email / a reminder / a reply / an invitation*
- *make a request / a suggestion / a complaint / an inquiry*
- *meet a deadline / miss a deadline / set a deadline / extend a deadline*
- *schedule a meeting / call / reschedule / cancel / postpone*
- *confirm a time / an appointment / receipt*
- *express thanks / appreciation / regret / concern*
- *provide information / an update / feedback / clarification*
- *raise an issue / a concern / a question*

**Expressions**
- *Thanks for getting back to me.*
- *Sorry for the slow reply.*
- *Could you give me an update on…?*
- *Just a heads-up that…*
- *Looking forward to your thoughts.*
- *Let me know if that works for you.*
- *Feel free to call if easier.*
- *I'll keep you posted.*
- *Happy to discuss.*

**Contrast groups**
| Items | Difference |
|---|---|
| *reply / respond / answer* | reply and respond are general; answer a question/call |
| *forward / send / share* | pass on an email / transmit / make available |
| *cc / bcc* | visible copy / hidden copy |
| *urgent / important* | needs action now / matters greatly (may not be urgent) |
| *regards / sincerely / cheers* | neutral-formal / formal / informal |
| *inquire / enquire / ask* | formal question / UK spelling variant / general |

## 14. Exercises

**A. Subject lines.** Improve each:
1. "Hello" (asking a colleague for a document) 2. "Urgent" (invoice problem) 3. "Meeting" (proposing a call on Tuesday) 4. "Re: Re: Re: Project" (new topic: budget) 5. "Info" (asking about a job opening)

**B. Greetings and sign-offs.** Choose a suitable greeting and sign-off:
6. To your friendly teammate Sam. 7. To a client, Ms. Alvarez, first contact. 8. To an unknown hiring manager. 9. To a UK recruiter, Mr. Hughes (a named person), a formal letter-style email.

**C. Rewrite politely**
10. "Send me the report today." 11. "I need the information now." 12. "Tell me the time of the meeting." 13. "You didn't answer my email."
14. "I want that you confirm the date."

**D. Fix the translated phrases**
15. "Without more, I say goodbye." 16. "I send you attached the invoice." 17. "I'm waiting for your news." 18. "Please be informed that the office is closed." 19. "I have a doubt about the contract." 20. "Remember me the meeting, please." 21. "Cordial greeting," 22. "I hope your answer."

**E. Grammar in emails.** Correct:
23. "I look forward to hear from you." 24. "Thank you for inform me." 25. "Please find attach the file." 26. "Sorry for the inconvenients." 27. "We have some informations for you." 28. "The meeting is in Monday at three."

**F. Order the email.** Put the parts in a logical order: (a) Best regards, Ana (b) Hi Carlos, (c) Could you send me the figures by Thursday? (d) Subject: Request: Q3 figures (e) Thanks so much for your help. (f) I'm preparing the Q3 report for Friday's meeting.

**G. Shorten.** Rewrite in 3–4 lines:
"Dear Mr. Rojas, I hope this email finds you well. I am writing to you with the purpose of informing you that, unfortunately, due to unforeseen circumstances that have arisen in our department, we will not be able to deliver the order on the date that was agreed. We apologize for any inconvenience that this may cause you. We are working hard to deliver it as soon as possible. Without more, I say goodbye. Attentively, Ana."

**H. Tone adjust.** Rewrite the same message for (a) a close colleague and (b) a senior client: *You need to approve the budget today.*

(a) Ask a colleague for a file by Friday. (b) Follow up on a proposal after one week. (c) Decline an invitation politely. (d) Report a one-week delay. (e) Introduce yourself to a potential partner.

## 17. Speaking Practice
(Emails and spoken updates share the same structure.)

**Drill 1 — Say it in 30 seconds.** Take an email you wrote and say its content aloud in 30 seconds: who, what, when.

**Drill 2 — Read aloud.** Read each template in Section 12 aloud with natural rhythm and weak forms (Phase 7).

**Drill 3 — Request ladder.** Say the same request in five levels of politeness (see 2.5).

**Drill 4 — Phone-to-email.** Role-play a phone call that you then summarize in a short email (and vice versa).

**Drill 5 — Spoken follow-up.** Practice a polite voicemail: *Hi, this is Ana from ABC. I'm calling about the proposal I sent last week…*

**Drill 6 — Role-play.** With a partner, one writes and the other reads the email aloud as the recipient, noting anything unclear.

**Drill 7 — Dictate.** Dictate an email to a partner (or to your phone's voice typing) and see what needs editing.
