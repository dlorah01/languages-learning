# Lesson 57 — Business and Professional Vocabulary
### Phase 5: Vocabulary as a System | Module 7 of 8

---

## 0. Where this fits
Lesson 56 gave you the academic end of formal English. This lesson gives you the **workplace**: the English of meetings, emails, negotiations, presentations, reports, performance reviews, and job interviews. Professional English shares many features with academic English (precision, hedging, nominalization), but its goals are different:

| | Academic English | Professional English |
|---|---|---|
| **Main goal** | build and test knowledge | get things done, build relationships |
| **Reader** | experts, evaluators | busy colleagues, clients, managers |
| **Style** | dense, impersonal, cautious | clear, direct but polite, action-oriented |
| **Length** | long, developed | short, scannable |
| **Verbs** | often nominalized | strong action verbs |
| **Risk** | being wrong | being misunderstood or being rude |

You will draw on nearly every earlier lesson: core verbs (Phase 2), prepositions (Phase 3), phrasal verbs (Phase 4: *follow up, sign off on, roll out, push back*), collocations (Lesson 52), intensifiers and softeners (Lesson 53), synonym precision (Lesson 54), and register (Lesson 55). This lesson organizes them around **situations**, which is how you will actually need them.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
In a workplace, language does four jobs at the same time:

1. **Transfers information** (status, numbers, decisions).
2. **Gets action** (requests, deadlines, delegation).
3. **Builds and protects relationships** (tone, politeness, credit, tact).
4. **Signals competence** (clarity, structure, confident-but-calibrated claims).

A learner can have perfect grammar and still fail jobs 3 and 4: *"I need the report today. You are late."* is grammatical and information-complete, but it damages the relationship and signals poor judgment. Compare: *"Could you send me the report by end of day? I need it for tomorrow's meeting."* Same information, a different outcome.

The practical skill is a **toolkit of frames** for recurring situations. Professionals do not invent sentences from scratch. They reach for: *I wanted to follow up on…, Just to confirm…, Would it be possible to…?, Let me circle back to that, Here's where we stand, The main risk is…, What I'd propose is…* A large part of "sounding professional" is having the right 100 frames ready.

> **Core idea:** professional fluency = **clear structure + calibrated politeness + the right chunks**.

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 Professional English in one page: five principles

| # | Principle | In practice |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Lead with the point** (the "bottom line up front") | *I'm writing to request an extension on the report.* Then give reasons. |
| 2 | **Be specific** | *by Friday 3 p.m.* not *soon*; *a 12% increase* not *a big increase* |
| 3 | **Be polite through grammar, not decoration** | *Could you…? / Would it be possible…?* rather than long flattery |
| 4 | **Use strong verbs and concrete nouns** | *We cut costs by 8%* beats *A reduction of costs was achieved* |
| 5 | **Close the loop** | end with the next step, owner, and date: *I'll send the draft by Tuesday. Could you review it by Thursday?* |

### 2.3 Functional frames for key situations
These are the chunks that "sound professional." Learn them as **frames with slots**.

#### (a) Emails
| Function | Frames |
|---|---|
| **Opening** | *I hope you're well. / Thank you for your email. / Thanks for getting back to me. / I'm writing to… / I'm writing regarding… / I'm following up on… / I wanted to check in about…* |
| **Requesting** | *Could you please send…? / Would you be able to…? / I'd appreciate it if you could… / Would it be possible to…? / Could you let me know…? / I was wondering if…* |
| **Giving information** | *Please find attached… / I've attached… / Here's a quick update: … / For your information, … / Just to let you know, … / As discussed, …* |
| **Confirming** | *Just to confirm, … / I can confirm that… / This is to confirm… / Confirming our call: …* |
| **Clarifying** | *Just to clarify, … / Could you clarify what you mean by…? / If I understand correctly, … / To make sure I've understood, …* |
| **Apologizing** | *Apologies for the delay. / Sorry for the confusion. / I apologize for any inconvenience. / Thanks for your patience.* |
| **Delivering bad news** | *Unfortunately, … / I'm afraid that… / I regret to inform you that… / We won't be able to…* |
| **Following up** | *I'm just following up on my previous email. / Just a gentle reminder that… / I wanted to circle back on… / Have you had a chance to look at…?* |
| **Closing** | *Please let me know if you have any questions. / I look forward to hearing from you. / Thanks again for your help. / Looking forward to your reply. / Talk soon.* |
| **Sign-off** | *Best regards, / Kind regards, / Best, / Thanks, / Sincerely,* |

| Function | Frames |
|---|---|
| **Opening** | *Let's get started. / Shall we begin? / Thanks everyone for joining. / The purpose of today's meeting is to… / On the agenda today we have… / Let's go through the agenda.* |
| **Giving updates** | *I'd like to give a quick update on… / Here's where we stand. / We're on track / ahead of / behind schedule. / We've made good progress on… / We've hit a snag with…* |
| **Asking opinions** | *What do you think? / How do you see this? / Do you have any thoughts on this? / I'd like to get your input on… / Would anyone like to add anything?* |
| **Giving opinions** | *In my view, … / From my perspective, … / I'd suggest that… / I think we should… / My concern is that… / It seems to me that…* |
| **Agreeing** | *I agree. / That makes sense. / I couldn't agree more. / Good point. / I'm on board with that.* |
| **Disagreeing politely** | *I see your point, but… / I'm not sure I agree. / I have a slightly different view. / I'm a bit concerned about… / With respect, … / That could be a risk because…* |
| **Interrupting politely** | *Sorry to interrupt, but… / Can I jump in here? / If I could just add something… / May I make a quick point?* |
| **Redirecting** | *Let's park that for now. / Let's take that offline. / Let's stay on topic. / We're running short on time, so… / Let's circle back to that later.* |
| **Clarifying** | *Could you elaborate on that? / What do you mean by…? / Just to make sure we're on the same page, … / Can you walk us through it?* |

#### (c) Presentations
| Function | Frames |
|---|---|
| **Opening** | *Good morning, everyone. Thank you for coming. / Today I'd like to talk about… / My presentation is divided into three parts. / By the end, you'll have a clear picture of…* |
| **Signposting** | *Let me start with… / Moving on to… / Turning now to… / That brings me to… / Let's look at the next slide. / Before I move on, … / To sum up…* |
| **Describing visuals** | *As you can see on this chart, … / This graph shows… / If you look at the figures, … / The key takeaway here is… / Notice how…* |
| **Emphasizing** | *What's important here is… / I want to highlight… / The crucial point is… / The bottom line is…* |
| **Handling questions** | *Great question. / Thanks for asking. / Let me come back to that. / I don't have the exact figure to hand, but I'll follow up. / Does that answer your question?* |
| **Closing** | *To sum up, … / In conclusion, … / The main takeaways are… / Thank you for your attention. I'd be happy to take any questions.* |

#### (d) Negotiation and persuasion
| Function | Frames |
|---|---|
| **Opening position** | *Our proposal is… / What we'd like to propose is… / We're looking for…* |
| **Probing** | *What are your priorities? / What would it take for you to…? / How flexible are you on…?* |
| **Conditional offers** | *If you can commit to X, we can offer Y. / We'd be prepared to… provided that… / On condition that…* |
| **Counter-offers** | *That's a bit higher than we'd budgeted. / Would you consider…? / How about meeting in the middle?* |
| **Concessions** | *We can agree to that. / We're willing to compromise on… / We could be flexible on…* |
| **Refusing** | *I'm afraid we can't go that far. / That's difficult for us. / That's outside our budget.* |

#### (e) Feedback and performance reviews
| Function | Frames |
|---|---|
| **Praise** | *You did an excellent job on… / I was impressed by… / Your contribution to… was invaluable. / Keep up the good work.* |
| **Constructive criticism** | *One area for improvement is… / It would be helpful if you could… / I'd like to see more… / Have you considered…? / The next step would be to…* |
| **Sandwich model** | *positive → development area → positive/next steps* |
| **Setting goals** | *Let's set some objectives for next quarter. / By the end of Q3, I'd like you to… / What support do you need to achieve that?* |
| **Receiving feedback** | *Thanks for the feedback. / That's helpful to know. / I appreciate you pointing that out. / I'll work on that. / Could you give me an example?* |

#### (f) Job interviews
| Function | Frames |
|---|---|
| **Describing yourself** | *I'm a [role] with X years of experience in… / My background is in… / I specialize in… / I'm particularly strong in…* |
| **Experience** | *In my previous role, I was responsible for… / I led a team of… / I managed… / I was in charge of… / I played a key role in…* |
| **Achievements** | *I increased… by X% / I reduced… from X to Y / I successfully delivered… / I was promoted after… / As a result, …* |
| **Strengths** | *One of my strengths is… / I pride myself on… / I'm known for…* |
| **Weaknesses (honest + developing)** | *I used to find it hard to… but I've been working on it by… / An area I'm developing is…* |
| **Motivation** | *I'm drawn to this role because… / What attracts me to the company is… / I see this as an opportunity to…* |

| **Questions to ask** | *What does success look like in this role? / How is performance measured? / What are the biggest challenges the team faces?* |

| Role | Frames |
|---|---|

### 2.4 Power verbs for CVs, reports, and updates
Strong, specific verbs make results visible. Use the **past tense** for completed work and the **present** for ongoing responsibilities.

| Area | Verbs |
|---|---|
| **Leadership** | *led, managed, supervised, directed, coordinated, mentored, coached, oversaw, delegated* |
| **Creation** | *developed, designed, built, launched, established, introduced, created, initiated, founded* |
| **Improvement** | *improved, increased, boosted, streamlined, optimized, enhanced, upgraded, strengthened, accelerated, reduced, cut, minimized* |
| **Analysis** | *analysed/analyzed, evaluated, assessed, audited, identified, researched, forecast, investigated, reviewed* |
| **Communication** | *presented, negotiated, persuaded, drafted, published, communicated, facilitated, liaised with, reported* |
| **Delivery** | *delivered, completed, implemented, executed, achieved, exceeded, secured, won, closed* |
| **Collaboration** | *collaborated with, partnered with, contributed to, supported, trained, onboarded* |

**Weak → strong**
- *was responsible for sales* → *led a 5-person sales team that grew revenue by 20%*
- *did reports* → *produced weekly financial reports for senior management*
- *helped with the project* → *contributed to a project that cut delivery time by 15%*
- *worked on* → *developed / implemented / managed*

### 2.5 Hedging and directness at work
Professional English balances two needs: **clarity** and **tact**.

| Situation | Too blunt | Balanced | Too vague |
|---|---|---|---|
| Disagreement | *You're wrong.* | *I see it a bit differently. My concern is that…* | *Well, maybe, I guess it could be a possibility that…* |
| Request | *Send me the file.* | *Could you send me the file by 3 p.m.?* | *If you ever have time, maybe sometime you could…* |
| Delay | *It's late.* | *We're running a few days behind. The new date is Friday.* | *There might be some small delays, perhaps.* |
| Bad news | *We failed.* | *Unfortunately, we didn't reach the target. Here's what we learned and what we'll do next.* | *Things didn't go entirely as planned, in some respects.* |
| Criticism | *This is bad.* | *This section needs more detail. Could you add data to support the claim?* | *It's quite interesting, but maybe…* |

**Rule:** be **soft on the person, hard on the problem**. Use "we" and "the plan" instead of "you" and "your mistake": *The report is missing the last quarter's data* (not *You forgot the last quarter*).

### 2.6 Numbers, data, and trends (the language of reports)
**Describing trends**
| Direction | Verbs / nouns |
|---|---|
| **Up** | *rise, increase, grow, climb, rocket, surge, soar, jump, go up; a rise, an increase, growth, a surge, a jump* |
| **Down** | *fall, decrease, decline, drop, dip, plunge, plummet, slump, tumble; a fall, a drop, a decline, a slump* |
| **Stable** | *remain stable / steady / flat; level off; plateau; stay the same; stabilize* |
| **Fluctuating** | *fluctuate, vary, swing, oscillate; fluctuations* |
| **Peak / trough** | *peak at, reach a high of, hit a low of, bottom out at* |

**Degree adverbs/adjectives:** *slight(ly), modest(ly), moderate(ly), steady/steadily, significant(ly), sharp(ly), dramatic(ally), rapid(ly), gradual(ly), marginal(ly)*

**Prepositions with numbers**
- *rise **by** 5%* (amount of change); *rise **to** 15%* (end point); *rise **from** 10% **to** 15%*; *stand **at** 15%*; *an increase **of** 5%*; *an increase **in** sales*; *a rise **of** 20 points*; *peak **at** 50,000*; *between 10% and 15%*; *around / approximately / roughly / just over / just under / nearly / almost / more than / fewer than / less than*.

**Fractions and proportions:** *a third of, two-thirds, the vast majority, a small minority, one in five, 20 percent, a fifth, a quarter, half, double, triple, twice as many, three times higher.*

**Years, quarters, periods:** *in 2025, in Q3, in the third quarter, during the first half, in the fiscal year, year-on-year, month-on-month, over the past 12 months, by the end of the year, since 2020, from January to March.*

**Pronouncing numbers (important for clarity)**
- 13 /θɜːrˈtiːn/ vs. 30 /ˈθɜːrɾi/; 14/40; 15/50; 16/60; 17/70; 18/80; 19/90 (stress on *-teen* vs. first syllable).
- 2,500 = *two thousand five hundred* / *twenty-five hundred*; 1,250,000 = *one million, two hundred and fifty thousand* (UK *and*; US usually omits it); 0.5 = *zero point five* / *point five*; 2025 = *twenty twenty-five*; 2005 = *two thousand five* / *two thousand and five* (UK).
- Currency: *$5.50* = *five dollars and fifty cents* / *five fifty*; *€12m* = *twelve million euros*; *£3k* = *three thousand pounds*.
- Percentages: *25%* = *twenty-five percent* (US) / *per cent* (UK).

## 3. Why Native Speakers Do This
Native professionals rely on **templates** more than outsiders realize. Their emails and meeting language are made of chunks (*Just following up on…, I'll circle back…, Let me know if you have any questions*) because they:

1. save time (reading and writing),
2. lower risk (tested phrases rarely offend),
3. signal belonging (shared jargon marks you as part of the group),
4. guide the reader (predictable structure means faster comprehension).

Politeness norms in English-language workplaces (strong in the UK, strong but more informal in the US) are built into grammar: modals, hedges, softeners, thanking, and apologizing. A message without them can feel cold even if the content is perfect. Conversely, over-softening can hide the message. The best professionals are **warm, brief, and specific**.

Also, "small talk" before business (*How was your weekend?*) is not wasted time; it builds rapport and trust. Skipping it can be read as abrupt in many Anglo contexts.

## 4. When to Use / When Not To
**Use**
- Frames in emails and meetings, because they speed communication and reduce risk.
- Specific numbers, dates, and names instead of vague words.
- Active verbs and "we" to emphasize collaboration.
- Plain, concise sentences (15–20 words average in emails).
- A clear subject line with the action: *Request: approval for Q3 budget by Friday*.

**Don't**
- Don't write long, ornate paragraphs. Busy readers skim.
- Don't use "Dear Sir/Madam" when you can find a name.
- Don't overload with jargon (*Let's circle back after we leverage synergies in a deep dive*).
- Don't use *ASAP* or *urgent* for everything: it loses meaning. Give a date and time.
- Don't use all caps or multiple exclamation marks in professional email.
- Don't copy-paste bold, direct translations of Spanish business formulas (*quedo a su entera disposición*).
- Don't send emotional emails. Wait 24 hours, then rewrite factually.
- Don't make jokes or sarcasm with people you don't know; it travels poorly across cultures.
- Don't criticize in public channels (reply-all, group chats). Take it offline.
- Don't say "no" bluntly; offer alternatives: *That won't be possible by Friday, but we could deliver on Monday.*

## 5. Frequency & Register

**Priority (learn in this order)**
1. **Email frames** (opening, request, confirm, follow-up, close).
2. **Meeting frames** (update, opinion, polite disagreement, summarize).
3. **Core business collocations** (meet a deadline, set a goal, reach a target, make a decision, give a presentation, run a meeting).
4. **Trend and number language** (rise by, drop to, year-on-year).
5. **Interview and performance-review language.**
6. **Industry-specific vocabulary** (finance, tech, law, healthcare) as needed.
7. **Jargon** (recognition first).

**Register in business**
| Context | Level (Lesson 55) |
|---|---|
| Internal chat (Slack/Teams) with peers | 2–3 (informal-neutral) |
| Email to colleagues | 3 (neutral) |
| Email to clients / senior leaders | 3–4 (neutral-formal) |
| Contracts, legal notices | 5 (formal) |
| Presentations | 3–4 |
| Phone calls | 3 |
| LinkedIn | 3 (professional-friendly) |
| Job application | 4 |

**Trend:** modern workplace English is **shorter, friendlier, and less formal** than 20 years ago, particularly in US tech and startup environments, while remaining more formal in law, finance, government, and many international contexts. Always follow the culture of your organization.

**Practical tip:** international workplaces often use a **neutral global English**: clear, simple, minimal idioms and phrasal verbs, literal meaning, no heavy slang. When you write to a mixed audience, aim for that style.

### 7.5 Tone and culture traps
- **Too direct (translating Spanish directness):** *I need the file now.* → *Could you send the file by 3 p.m.? I need it for the meeting.*
- **Too formal (translating Spanish ceremony):** *I would like to request your kind attention for…* → *Could you take a look at this when you have a moment?*
- **Excess self-deprecation or apology:** *Sorry, my English is very bad* in every email → unnecessary and weakens your authority; keep it for real communication problems.
- **Over-promising:** *I'll do it immediately* → *I'll do it by Thursday* (specific, realistic).
- **Under-promising or vagueness:** *I'll try* (sounds weak) → *I'll aim to have it done by Friday.*
- **Misreading British understatement:** *That's quite good* (moderate), *I'm not sure that's the best idea* (this is bad), *with respect* (I disagree), *I'll bear that in mind* (probably won't do it), *interesting* (maybe not impressed). Learn to decode indirectness.
- **Misreading American positivity:** *Awesome!*, *Great job!*, *Let's definitely grab coffee sometime* (may be only politeness). Not every enthusiasm is a commitment.
- **First names vs. titles:** most English-speaking workplaces use first names even with seniors; follow the other person.
- **Small talk omitted:** starting a call with business immediately can feel abrupt.
- **Saying "no" bluntly:** *No, we can't.* → *That would be difficult because… What we could do is…*
- **Time zones, dates, and units:** always specify (*3 p.m. Bogotá time (COT)*, *Friday, 14 March*), and avoid ambiguous dates (03/04).
- **Over-using "please" and "kindly":** one *please* per request is enough.
- **Reply-all and "FYI" overload:** cultural; learn the norms of your team.

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ **"Business English is a separate language with special words."** — It is general English plus a set of high-frequency chunks and a clear register. Fluency in general English is the base.
- ❌ **"The more formal and complicated, the more professional."** — Clear and concise is professional. Plain English is a mark of competence.
- ❌ **"Jargon makes me sound like an insider."** — Overuse sounds clichéd; misuse sounds worse. Use what your team uses.
- ❌ **"Politeness = long sentences."** — Politeness = tone and structure (modals, hedges, thanks). A short email can be very polite.
- ❌ **"Native speakers never make mistakes at work."** — They often write quickly and informally. Their norm is *clear and friendly*, not perfect.
- ❌ **"I should avoid contractions in business writing."** — Contractions are normal in neutral business English (*I'll, we're, can't*). Avoid them in contracts and formal letters.
- ❌ **"Passive voice is always more professional."** — Active voice is clearer: *We will deliver the report on Friday* beats *The report will be delivered on Friday* unless the agent is unimportant.
- ❌ **"Saying 'I think' weakens me."** — Used appropriately, it signals a perspective rather than a fact. Overuse weakens; strategic use is good.
- ❌ **"I should apologize every time I make a request."** — Over-apologizing signals low status. Use *Thanks* more than *Sorry*.
- ❌ **"Business idioms are essential for fluency."** — Understanding them is important; using them is optional. Common clear phrases suffice.
- ❌ **"ASAP means immediately."** — It's vague and often irritating; give deadlines.
- ❌ **"Email and chat have the same rules."** — Chat is shorter and more informal; email is more structured. Adjust to the medium.

### 9.2 Numbers and spelling (avoid costly misunderstandings)
- Contrast: **13** /θɜːrˈtiːn/ vs. **30** /ˈθɜːrɾi/. In -teen numbers both syllables are heard, with stress on *-teen*; in -ty numbers, the stress falls on the first syllable and the ending is short.
- When clarity is crucial, **say digits and use confirmation**: *That's one-five, fifteen, not five-zero, fifty.*
- Spell names/emails using the clear alphabet: *A as in Alpha, B as in Bravo…* (or common words: *B for Bogotá*). *At* for @, *dot* for ., *dash/hyphen* for –, *underscore* for _.
- Dates: UK *the fifth of March*, US *March fifth*. Years: *twenty twenty-six*.

### 9.3 Presentation delivery
1. **Chunk with pauses** at meaning boundaries (every 5–10 words).
2. **Stress key content words** and numbers: *Sales rose by TWENTY percent in the THIRD quarter.*
3. **Use intonation to signpost:** rising pitch to introduce, falling at the end of a point; a pause before the key figure.
4. **Slow down** for numbers and names; speak 10–15% slower than normal conversation.
5. **Pronounce final consonants clearly** (*-ed, -s*): *increased* /ɪnˈkriːst/, *launched* /lɔːntʃt/, *costs* /kɒsts/. Dropped endings change meaning in business English (*cost/costs, manage/managed*).
6. **Avoid fillers** (*eh, mmm, este*): replace with a short pause or *So…*.

### 9.4 Phone and video calls
- Use rising-falling intonation to sound friendly; a smile changes the voice.
- Confirm understanding: *Let me repeat that to make sure I've got it right.*
- Use **clear openings/closings:** *Hello, this is…* / *Thanks for calling.*
- Fix audio issues politely: *Sorry, you're breaking up. Could you say that again?*

### 9.5 Spanish-speaker trouble spots in business English
- Initial *s + consonant* clusters: *strategy, stakeholder, schedule, statistics, specialist, speaker, start*; no "e" before.
- /ɪ/ vs /iː/: *deal–dill*, *sheet–shit* (avoid!), *beach–bitch* (avoid!), *live–leave*, *fill–feel*, *ship–sheep*.
- /θ/ and /ð/: *thank, think, three, month, with, they*.
- /v/ vs /b/: *very–berry*, *vote–boat*, *vendor–bender*.
- /ʃ/ vs /tʃ/: *share–chair*, *shop–chop*, *cheap–sheep*.
- Word-final consonants: *project, budget, report, contract, deadline*; keep the last sound.
- Vowel reduction in unstressed syllables: *company* /ˈkʌmpəni/, *different* /ˈdɪfrənt/, *comfortable* /ˈkʌmftərbəl/, *business* /ˈbɪznəs/ (2 syllables).

## 10. Memory Technique

### 10.1 The "situation kit" method
Create a **kit** for each recurring situation: a one-page card with 8–10 frames. Start with four kits:

1. **Email kit:** open, request, confirm, apologize, follow up, close.
2. **Meeting kit:** open, update, opinion, polite disagreement, interrupt, summarize, action items.
3. **Presentation kit:** open, signpost, describe a chart, emphasize, handle questions, close.
4. **Interview kit:** introduce yourself, STAR story, strengths, weaknesses, motivation, questions to ask.

Memorize one kit per week using shadowing and role-play (Section 17). Keep kits in your phone for pre-meeting review.

### 10.2 STAR for stories (interviews and reviews)
**S**ituation → **T**ask → **A**ction → **R**esult. Prepare 5 STAR stories (leadership, conflict, failure, achievement, teamwork). Rehearse each in 90 seconds with concrete numbers.

> *Situation: Our largest client threatened to leave. Task: I had to resolve the complaint within a week. Action: I held a call, identified delivery delays as the root cause, and set up weekly updates. Result: The client renewed for two years and satisfaction rose from 6 to 9.*

### 10.3 Verb–noun hubs for work (Lesson 52 method)
Make noun hubs: **meeting** (*call, schedule, set up, hold, chair, attend, miss, postpone, cancel, adjourn, wrap up*), **deadline** (*set, meet, miss, extend, push back, hit, tight, looming*), **budget** (*draw up, set, approve, cut, exceed, stay within, balance, tight*), **report** (*write, draft, submit, review, present, circulate*), **decision** (*make, reach, defer, reverse, sign off on*), **target** (*set, hit, meet, miss, exceed, achieve*), **team** (*lead, manage, join, build, motivate*), **risk** (*assess, identify, mitigate, take, manage*).

### 10.4 Template library
Maintain a document with your best emails: request, follow-up, polite decline, apology, thank-you, introduction, meeting recap, delay notice, negotiation. Edit and reuse. Over time, you'll internalize the patterns. Add notes on tone and context.

### 10.5 Number practice
Read numbers aloud daily from a news article or report. Practice trend sentences: *Revenue rose from 2.4 million to 3.1 million, an increase of 29%.* Create 5 sentences a day from real data (a stock, weather, company results).

### 10.6 Observe and collect
Collect real emails, meeting recordings (if allowed), LinkedIn posts, earnings-call transcripts, and TED/business talks. For each, copy 3 chunks into your notebook with the function and register label. Notice **how natives soften** requests and **how they structure** updates.

### 10.7 Shadowing and role-play
- **Shadow** 60 seconds of a business podcast or earnings call, copying rhythm and stress.
- **Role-play** with a partner or recording: you are the manager, then the employee; you are the seller, then the buyer.
- **Record** a 2-minute update every week. Listen for: fillers, missing endings, number clarity.

### 10.8 Spaced retrieval cards
- Front: *Politely disagree in a meeting.* → *I see your point, but I'm a bit concerned about…*
- Front: *Close an email asking for a reply.* → *I look forward to hearing from you.*
- Front: *Revenue ___ from 2m ___ 3m.* → *rose / to* (or *grew / to*)
- Front: *You want to move a topic to later.* → *Let's park that for now / take that offline.*
- Front: *Confirm something in writing.* → *Just to confirm, …*
Review on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30.

### 10.9 The 3-minute pre-meeting routine
Before a meeting or call: (1) write your **one-sentence goal**; (2) choose 3 frames you'll use; (3) prepare 1 number and 1 question. This reduces anxiety and improves fluency.

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1: Request = modal + please + reason + deadline.** *Could you send me the report by Friday? I need it for the board meeting.*

> **Pattern 2: Bad news = buffer + fact + reason + next step.** *Thanks for your patience. Unfortunately, the delivery will be delayed by two days due to a supplier issue. We'll confirm the new date by Monday.*

> **Pattern 3: Disagreement = acknowledge + soften + reason + alternative.** *I see where you're coming from, but I'm a bit concerned about the timeline. Would it help if we split the work in two phases?*

> **Pattern 4: Update = status + progress + issue + next step.** *We're on track overall. We've finished the design phase. The main risk is the vendor delay. Next, we'll finalize the contract by Wednesday.*

> **Pattern 5: Feedback = positive + specific development area + support.** *Your analysis was thorough. One area to work on is the executive summary, which could be shorter. I'm happy to review a draft.*

> **Pattern 6: Numbers = what + how much + when + compared to what.** *Sales increased by 12% in Q2, compared with Q1.*

> **Pattern 7: Action items = who + what + by when.** *Ana will send the draft by Tuesday; Luis will review by Thursday.*

> **Pattern 8: Choose verbs of action, not nouns of action.** *We decided / We implemented / We cut* rather than *We made the decision to / We carried out the implementation of / We achieved a reduction of.* (Contrast with Lesson 56: academic English likes nominalization; business English prefers verbs.)

> **Pattern 9: Hedging calibrated to certainty.** *We expect, we anticipate, we're aiming for, it looks likely, there's a risk that* (honest, not vague).

> **Pattern 10: Reduce ambiguity.** Replace *soon, later, a bit, some, ASAP* with times, numbers, owners.

> **Pattern 11: Sign-off language matches the relationship.** *Thanks* (colleague), *Best regards* (client), *Sincerely* (formal first contact).

> **Pattern 12: Warmth markers.** *Thanks so much, I really appreciate it, Great work on this, Happy to help, No problem at all.*

> **Connection to Lesson 55:** register ladders apply to every email and meeting.

> **Connection to Lesson 56:** hedging, noun phrases, reporting verbs, and signposting reappear, but with shorter sentences and more active verbs.

> **Connection to Phase 4:** professional English is rich in phrasal verbs (*follow up, roll out, sign off on, wrap up, push back, scale up*). Use them naturally in neutral registers.

> **Connection to Lesson 52–53:** collocation (*meet a deadline, heavy workload*) and intensifier control (*extremely busy, fairly confident, slightly behind*).

> **Connection to Lesson 58 (next):** idioms are common in business talk (*ballpark figure, back to square one, the ball is in your court*), so Lesson 58 will help you understand and use the most frequent ones.

## 12. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1 — Basic**
- *The meeting starts at ten.*
- *Could you send me the file, please?*
- *I'll call you tomorrow.*
- *Thanks for your help.*
- *We need to finish this by Friday.*

**Level 2 — Everyday work**
- *I'm writing to confirm our meeting on Tuesday.*
- *Could you please review the document and let me know your thoughts?*
- *We're a bit behind schedule, but we'll catch up next week.*
- *I'll follow up with the client this afternoon.*
- *Let's schedule a call to go over the details.*

**Level 3 — Professional**
- *I wanted to follow up on the proposal I sent last week. Have you had a chance to look at it?*
- *Revenue grew by 8% year-on-year, driven mainly by the new product line.*
- *I'm afraid we won't be able to meet the original deadline, but I can offer a revised timeline.*
- *Could you walk me through your reasoning?*
- *Let's take that offline and circle back after the meeting.*

**Level 4 — Strategic**
- *The key risk is supplier dependency; to mitigate it, we propose diversifying across two vendors.*
- *We've exceeded our target by 12%, but we should be cautious about extrapolating from a single quarter.*
- *I'd like to flag a potential issue before it escalates.*
- *Rather than cutting the budget across the board, I'd suggest prioritizing the three projects with the highest ROI.*
- *Given the constraints, a phased rollout seems the most realistic option.*

**Level 5 — Native-like fluency**
- "I appreciate the thoroughness of the proposal, but I'm not convinced the timeline is realistic given our current bandwidth. Could we revisit the scope?"
- "Let me play devil's advocate for a moment: what if the demand doesn't materialize at the levels we're forecasting?"
- "We're not quite there yet, but I'm cautiously optimistic that we'll close the gap by the end of the quarter."
- "I take your point, though I'd argue that the short-term cost is outweighed by the long-term savings."
- "To be fair, the team did an excellent job under tight constraints; the issue was less execution than planning."

### 12.4 Meeting dialogue
> **Chair:** Thanks, everyone, for joining. Today we'll go over the project status and agree next steps. Camila, could you start with an update?
> **Camila:** Sure. We're on track overall. We've completed the design phase and started development. The main risk is the vendor delay, which could push us back by about a week.
> **Chair:** Thanks. Luis, what's your view?
> **Luis:** I see the risk, but I'm a bit concerned about adding another vendor at this stage. Could we ask the current vendor to prioritize our order?
> **Camila:** That's a good point. I'll check with them today and report back by tomorrow.
> **Chair:** Great. Let's summarize the action items: Camila will contact the vendor by tomorrow; Luis will update the timeline by Friday. Anything else? No? Thanks, everyone.

### 12.5 Negotiation dialogue
> **Supplier:** Our price is $50 per unit for an order of 1,000.
> **Buyer:** Thanks. That's a bit higher than we'd budgeted. If we increased the order to 2,000 units, would you consider $45?
> **Supplier:** We could go to $47, provided you commit to a one-year contract.
> **Buyer:** That's closer. Could we meet in the middle at $46 with a 12-month agreement and payment within 30 days?
> **Supplier:** I think we can make that work. Let me confirm with my manager and get back to you by tomorrow.
> **Buyer:** Perfect. I'll send a summary of what we've discussed in writing.

> **Manager:** I wanted to start by saying how impressed I've been with your work on the Atlas project. You delivered on time and kept the client very satisfied.
> **Employee:** Thank you. I really enjoyed working on it.
> **Manager:** One area to focus on next year is delegation. You took on a lot yourself, which is admirable, but it could limit your growth. How do you feel about that?
> **Employee:** That's fair. I tend to double-check everything. I'd like to get better at trusting the team.
> **Manager:** Great. Let's set a goal: by Q2, lead a project with two junior colleagues. What support do you need?

## 17. Speaking Practice

**Drill 1 — Frame shadowing (five times each, with a recording):**
> "Just to confirm, …" / "I'd like to follow up on…" / "I see your point, but…" / "Let's take that offline." / "Here's where we stand."

**Drill 2 — Trend description (aloud, with numbers).** Using real data from a chart, describe it in 30 seconds: *Revenue rose from X to Y, an increase of Z%, driven by…*

**Drill 3 — Number clarity.** Say pairs aloud: 13/30, 14/40, 15/50, 16/60, 17/70, 18/80, 19/90. Then say sentences: *The meeting is at 3:15 on the 13th. The price is $50, not $15.*

**Drill 4 — Meeting simulation (3 minutes).** Record yourself playing two roles: chair and participant. Open the meeting, give an update, disagree politely, and summarize action items.

**Drill 5 — Elevator pitch (60 seconds).** "I'm [name], a [role] with [X] years of experience in… In my last position, I… (achievement with number). I'm now looking for… because…"

**Drill 6 — Phone call.** Role-play: call to reschedule a meeting. *Hello, this is… I'm calling about… Unfortunately, I won't be able to make it. Would it be possible to move it to…? Thank you for understanding.*

**Drill 7 — Handling questions.** Practice answering tough questions with a buffer: *That's a good question. Let me think… Based on what we know so far, … I'd need to check the data, but my initial view is…*

**Drill 8 — Role-play (negotiation).**
Partner: "We can offer a 5% discount."
You: "Thanks. That's helpful, but it's a bit lower than we'd hoped for. If we commit to a larger order, would you consider 8%?"

**Mini Quiz**
1. Lead with the point; be specific; be polite through grammar; use strong verbs and concrete nouns; close the loop.
2. *I see your point, but…; I'm a bit concerned about…; I have a slightly different view.* Any two.
3. End with the next step, owner, and date so the conversation has a clear outcome.
4. *Customer* buys products; *client* receives professional services.
5. *Rise by 5%* = amount of change; *rise to 15%* = end point; *stand at 15%* = current level.
6. *Doubt* means uncertainty; use *I have a question* or *I'd like to clarify*.
7. Situation, Task, Action, Result: a structure for behavioural answers.
8. It is non-standard and redundant (*revert* already means return); say *get back to me*.
9. Any three with meanings from the jargon table.
10. *Salary* is fixed (usually monthly/annual, professional); *wage* is hourly/weekly.
11. Discuss later or privately, not in the meeting.
12. It is vague and overused; specify a date and time.
13. 13 = /θɜːrˈtiːn/ (stress on *-teen*); 30 = /ˈθɜːrɾi/ (stress on first syllable).
