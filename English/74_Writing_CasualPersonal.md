# Lesson 74 — Casual and Personal Writing
### Phase 9: Writing | Module 3 of 4

---

## 0. Where this fits
Lesson 72 covered professional email and Lesson 73 covered academic prose. This lesson covers the register people use most in daily life and that learners often neglect: **casual and personal writing**. That means text messages, chats, social-media posts, comments, blog entries, personal stories, thank-you notes, congratulations, condolences, invitations, reviews, and personal statements.

This is where "natural" matters most. Nobody grades a text message, but your friends, colleagues, and neighbors form an impression of you from it. A message that is grammatically perfect but stiff (*Good afternoon. I would like to inform you that I will arrive late.*) feels like a letter from a bank. A message that is warm and natural builds relationships.

It is also where **Spanish habits** are strongest, because affection, humor, and emphasis are expressed in language-specific ways: diminutives, long greetings, *besos*, *un abrazo*, *Dios te bendiga*, and so on. Direct translation often sounds odd.

Connections:
- **Lessons 59–63:** hedging, fillers, small talk, and turn-taking carry over to chat.
- **Lessons 66–68:** rhythm and intonation shape how punctuation and spelling convey tone.
- **Lesson 71:** natural vs. translated, applied to casual phrases.
- **Lesson 72:** the formal end; compare the registers.
- **Lesson 75 (next):** tone, style, and clarity editing, which you will use to polish personal writing.
- **Lesson 76 (Phase 10):** cultural pragmatics, which explains the "why" behind many of these conventions.

> **Core idea:** Casual writing is **spoken English on the page**. Write the way a friendly, clear person would speak, then add the small signals (punctuation, emoji, spelling) that replace tone of voice.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
When you speak, your listener hears **tone**: smiling, sarcasm, warmth, irritation. On a screen, tone disappears, so writers replace it with **punctuation, capitalization, length, emoji, and word choice**. These small signals carry huge meaning.

Compare:
- *Okay.* → can read as cold, annoyed, or final.
- *Okay!* → friendly.
- *ok* → casual, neutral.
- *okaaay* → hesitant or playful.
- *Okay 🙂* → warm.

All say the same word. The "tone" comes from the form.

A second image: **a conversation over coffee, typed.** You wouldn't hand your friend a speech. You'd say something short, react to what they said, ask something back. Casual writing is the same: short, responsive, human.

A third image: **a photograph vs. a painting.** Personal stories work best when they show a specific moment (a photograph) rather than general statements (a vague painting). *My grandmother's kitchen smelled like cinnamon and burnt toast* is a photograph. *I had a happy childhood* is a caption.

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 Registers in personal writing
| Context | Register | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Text to a close friend | very casual | lowercase possible, abbreviations, emoji, fragments |
| Text to a coworker | casual-neutral | complete-ish sentences, light emoji, polite |
| Group chat | casual | short, reactive, humor, memes |
| Social post (Instagram/Facebook) | casual-public | personal tone, hashtags, emoji |
| Social post (LinkedIn) | semi-professional | first person, story, clear takeaway |
| Blog / newsletter | conversational | voice, structure, headings |
| Thank-you / congratulations note | warm, personal | specific, sincere, short |
| Condolence message | gentle, formal-warm | simple, sincere, no clichés overload |
| Personal statement | personal-formal | authentic voice, concrete stories, clear structure |
| Review (restaurant, product) | casual-informative | opinion + specifics |

**Rule:** match the register of the other person and the platform. If your friend writes in lowercase with no punctuation, a perfectly punctuated paragraph can feel formal or distant.

**Punctuation as tone**
| Form | Typical effect |
|---|---|
| Period at the end of a short text (*Sure.*) | can sound serious, curt, or annoyed |
| No period (*sure*) | neutral, casual |
| Exclamation (*Sure!*) | friendly, enthusiastic |
| Multiple exclamation (*Sure!!!*) | very enthusiastic (use with friends) |
| Ellipsis (*Sure...*) | hesitation, trailing off, or awkwardness |
| Question mark repeated (*Really???*) | surprise, disbelief |
| ALL CAPS (*I LOVE IT*) | strong emotion; shouting if used a lot |
| *asterisks* or _underscores_ | emphasis in plain text |
| Tilde (*sure~*) | playful (some communities) |
| Parentheses ( :) ) | smile / softener |
| Lowercase only | relaxed, intimate |

**Emoji.** Use them to soften or add emotion, not to replace all words. In English-speaking workplaces, one or two friendly emoji are acceptable in chat if others use them; avoid them in formal emails. Some emoji have meanings that differ by community (the 🙏 can mean "thanks," "please," or "praying"; 😅 often signals awkwardness; 💀 can mean "I'm dying of laughter"). Follow your contacts' usage.

**Reactions and short replies**
- *Haha nice.* / *Love that.* / *That's awesome!* / *Oh no!* / *Ugh, that sucks.* / *Wow, congrats!* / *Sounds good.* / *Sounds like a plan.* / *Can't wait!* / *No worries.* / *All good.* / *Totally.* / *For sure.* / *Same.* / *Mood.* / *Ha, true.*

**Response timing.** A quick one-line reply is normal; a long, formal reply to a casual question can feel heavy. It is polite to acknowledge messages within a reasonable time, but norms vary by relationship.

### 2.3 Voice: sounding like a person
**Voice** is the personality in your writing. In casual English it comes from:
1. **Contractions** (*I'm, don't, can't, it's*).
2. **Fragments** (*Great idea. Really.*).
3. **Starting with And/But/So** (*So, I finally did it.*).
4. **Direct address** (*you*).
5. **Concrete details** (*a cold cup of coffee at 6 a.m.*).
6. **Rhythm:** mix short and long sentences (Lesson 66).
7. **Understatement and humor** (*It wasn't my best day.*).
8. **Discourse words** (*honestly, actually, anyway, basically, you know*).
9. **Informal vocabulary** (*kids, stuff, pretty good, kind of*).
10. **Questions** (*Ever tried that?*).

**Voice-killers**
- Stiff openers (*I would like to inform you*).
- Long Latinate words (*commence, subsequently*).
- Stacked qualifiers (*I would perhaps like to possibly suggest*).
- Over-explaining.
- Textbook phrases (*How are you? I'm fine, thank you, and you?*).

### 2.4 Greetings, openers, and closers in casual writing
| Function | Casual options |
|---|---|
| Greeting | *Hi! / Hey! / Hey there / Hello! / Morning! / Hi Ana,* |
| Checking in | *How's it going? / How are you doing? / What's up? / How's your week? / Long time no see!* |
| Replying | *Thanks for the message! / Hey, sorry for the late reply.* |
| Opening a topic | *So, guess what? / Quick question: … / Random, but… / Just wanted to say…* |
| Closing | *Talk soon! / Catch you later! / See you Saturday! / Take care! / Let me know! / Hugs! / Love you! (close friends/family)* |
| Affection (family/close) | *Miss you! / Thinking of you. / Hugs! / Love you lots!* |
| Sign-offs in notes/cards | *Warmly, / Take care, / With love, / Best wishes, / Love, / Yours,* |
| UK friendly | *Cheers! / Speak soon! / xx (kisses)* |

**Don't translate** *Un abrazo* as "A hug" in every context; *Hugs!* or *Take care!* is more natural. *Besos* as "Kisses" at the end of a message is unusual in English except among very close friends or partners (and in the UK, *x* or *xx* among friends is common). *Saludos a tu familia* → *Say hi to your family.*

### 2.5 Warmth in English vs. Spanish
Spanish often expresses affection with **diminutives** (*un momentito, cafecito*), **repetition**, **endearments**, and **extended greetings**. English tends to express warmth with **tone words and small phrases**:

| Spanish warmth | English equivalent |
|---|---|
| *Un momentito* | *Just a sec / one sec* |
| *Cafecito* | *a quick coffee / a little coffee break* |
| *Querida amiga* | *Hey! / Hi, you! / Hi Ana!* |
| *Qué alegría verte* | *So good to see you!* |
| *Te extraño mucho* | *I miss you so much.* |
| *Un abrazo grande* | *Big hug! / Sending hugs.* |
| *Cuídate mucho* | *Take care of yourself.* |
| *Dios te bendiga* | *Take care. / Wishing you all the best.* (blessings are used in some communities, but less in secular contexts) |
| *Felicitaciones* | *Congrats! / Congratulations!* |
| *Me encantó* | *I loved it! / It was amazing.* |
| *Estoy muy agradecida* | *Thank you so much. I really appreciate it.* |
| *Gracias por todo* | *Thanks for everything.* |
| *Que tengas un lindo día* | *Have a great day! / Have a good one!* |
| *Disculpa la molestia* | *Sorry to bother you.* |
| *Te quiero mucho* | *Love you lots.* (close family/friends); *I really care about you.* |

**Endearments in English:** *sweetie, honey, babe, sweetheart* (partners/family; context dependent), *buddy, pal, dude, man* (male friends, US), *mate* (UK/Australia), *love, darling, pet, duck* (UK regional, often non-romantic from older speakers or service staff). Avoid using endearments with colleagues or strangers unless you know the norm.

### 2.6 Writing a personal story (narrative)
A good personal story has a simple structure:

1. **Hook:** start in the middle of the action or with a striking detail.
2. **Context:** who, where, when (briefly).
3. **Complication:** what went wrong or changed.
4. **Climax:** the key moment.
5. **Resolution:** what happened.
6. **Takeaway:** what it means (a line, not a lecture).

**Show, don't tell.** Replace general statements with specific images and actions.

| Telling | Showing |
|---|---|
| I was nervous. | My hands were shaking so much I dropped my pen twice. |
| She was angry. | She slammed the door so hard the picture frames rattled. |
| It was a beautiful day. | The sun was warm on my neck, and the street smelled of fresh bread. |
| I was very tired. | I read the same line four times and still couldn't tell you what it said. |

**Tense.** Narrative is usually in the **simple past** (*I opened the door…*). Use **past continuous** for background (*It was raining…*) and **past perfect** for earlier events (*I had forgotten my keys.*). Switching to the **present** (historical present) can make an anecdote vivid in speech (*So I'm standing there, and he says…*), but keep tenses consistent in writing unless you do it deliberately.

**Dialogue.** Use quotation marks and short tags: *"I can't believe it," she said.* In English, a new speaker usually starts a new line.

**Humor and understatement.** English storytelling often uses understatement (*It wasn't ideal.*) and self-deprecating humor.

### 2.7 Social media writing
| Platform style | Features |
|---|---|
| **Instagram/Facebook caption** | short, personal, emotive, hashtags, emoji |
| **LinkedIn post** | first person, short lines, a story, a takeaway, minimal emoji; avoid clichés (*I'm thrilled to announce…*) unless natural |
| **X/Threads-style post** | very short, punchy, conversational |
| **Comment** | brief, specific, friendly (*Love this!* / *This is so helpful, thanks for sharing.*) |
| **Review** | rating + specifics + honest tone |
| **Forum/Reddit** | clear question, context, politeness, formatting |
| **Group announcements** | clear information first, details after |

**Tips:** hook in the first line, short paragraphs, plain words, one idea per post, and a call to action only when you need one. Public posts are permanent and searchable; consider future readers (employers, clients).

### 2.8 Common occasions: ready-made patterns
**Thank-you note (specific + sincere)**
> *Hi Sam, thank you so much for the book. I started it last night and I'm already hooked. It was so thoughtful of you to remember I like mysteries.*

Pattern: **thank + specific thing + what it meant + (optional) next step.**

**Congratulations**
> *Congratulations on the new job! You really deserve it. Let's celebrate soon.*

Other: *So happy for you! / That's wonderful news! / Well done!*

**Condolences** (simple and sincere)
> *I'm so sorry for your loss. Thinking of you and your family. If there's anything I can do, please let me know.*

Avoid: lengthy philosophical consolation or phrases like *He's in a better place* (can offend). Use: *I'm so sorry. / My thoughts are with you. / She was a wonderful person.*

**Apology (personal)**
> *I'm really sorry I missed your call. Things got hectic. Can we talk tomorrow?*

**Invitation**
> *We're having a few friends over on Saturday, 14 Nov, around 7. Would love to see you! Let me know if you can make it.*
Formal: *You're invited to… RSVP by…* / *Save the date.* / *Join us for…*

**Declining an invitation**
> *Thanks so much for the invite! I can't make it that weekend, but I'd love to catch up soon.*

**Birthday**
> *Happy birthday, Laura! Hope you have an amazing day. 🎉*

**Get well**
> *Sorry to hear you're not feeling well. Get some rest, and let me know if you need anything.*

**Making plans**
> *Free Saturday? Thinking of grabbing lunch around 1.* / *Sounds good. Where should we meet?*

**Reviews**
> *Great little café. The coffee was excellent and the staff were friendly. A bit noisy at lunchtime, but worth a visit.*
Pattern: **overall opinion + 2–3 specifics + small criticism or tip.**

### 2.9 Personal statements and cover narratives
Personal statements (for universities, scholarships, fellowships) mix **personal story** and **clear structure**.

**Typical structure**
1. **Opening scene or moment** (specific, not "Since I was a child").
2. **Development:** what you learned, did, or changed.
3. **Connection to the field or program:** why this path, why now.
4. **Evidence of ability:** experience, projects, results.
5. **Future direction:** what you will do and why this program fits.

**Principles**
- Be **specific**: one concrete story is better than a list of claims.
- Be **honest and reflective**: show growth, not perfection.
- **Avoid clichés**: *passion for helping others, since I was little, I have always dreamed.*
- **Avoid translated rhetoric**: grand opening sentences about humanity and society (Lesson 73) rarely help.
- **Voice:** professional but personal; contractions are often fine.
- **Length:** follow the limit exactly.
- **Proofread:** names, program details, and grammar.

### 2.10 Politeness and sensitivity online
- **Read the room:** humor and sarcasm can be misunderstood without tone of voice; add a signal (*haha, just kidding 🙂*) when needed.
- **Disagree gently:** *I see it differently, but thanks for sharing.* (Lesson 61)
- **Think before posting** when angry; drafts and delays help.
- **Group chats:** avoid long arguments; move sensitive topics to a call.
- **Privacy:** don't share others' private information.
- **Tagging and forwarding:** ask first when appropriate.
- **Cultural humor:** jokes about identity, religion, or politics can offend; avoid them with people you don't know well.
- **Slang and dialect:** use slang you understand; avoid imitating dialects or in-group language you don't share, as it can sound mocking or inauthentic.

### 2.11 Grammar in casual writing: what's flexible
| Feature | Casual allowance | Still avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Fragments | *Great idea. Really.* | confusing, incomplete meaning |
| Contractions | encouraged | none |
| Starting with *And/But/So* | fine | |
| Dropped subject | *Hope you're well.* / *Can't wait.* / *Sounds good.* | in complete statements where unclear |
| Lowercase *i* | texts to friends | formal contexts |
| Abbreviations | common | overuse that blocks comprehension |
| Run-ons | acceptable in chat | in longer posts and stories |
| Slang | fine with peers | unknown slang |
| Double negatives | avoid in standard writing | |
| Basic grammar (verb forms, articles, prepositions) | still matters | repeated errors can still sound confusing |

The flexibility is **in style, not in basic accuracy**. A casual text can drop a subject (*Can't wait!*), but it should not say *I am agree*.

## 3. Why Native Speakers Do This
1. **Digital communication evolved from speech.** Chat is closer to talking than to letters, so it borrows spoken features.
2. **Tone has to be rebuilt.** Without voice and face, writers use punctuation, emoji, and word choice to signal feelings.
3. **Speed.** Short forms (*idk, rn, omw*) save time on small screens.
4. **Group identity.** Slang and style mark who belongs to a group.
5. **Understatement and irony.** English humor often relies on indirectness, which needs careful signals in writing.
6. **Privacy and informality norms.** Many English-speaking cultures prefer a friendly but not overly effusive style in everyday messages.
7. **Storytelling culture.** Personal narrative is valued in education, workplaces, and social media: people expect concrete detail and a clear point.

## 4. When to Use / When Not To

**Use casual style when**
- messaging friends, family, or close colleagues;
- posting on personal social media;
- commenting or chatting in informal communities;
- writing a blog or personal narrative with a conversational voice.

**Use semi-formal style when**
- writing to someone you don't know well (a new neighbor, a landlord, a client in chat);
- posting on LinkedIn;
- writing a personal statement or cover narrative.

**Avoid casual style when**
- writing to professors, recruiters, or senior contacts (unless they set the tone);
- communicating legal, financial, or health-related information;
- writing in a crisis or conflict where clarity and tone matter;
- you don't know the person's culture or preferences.

**Quick test:** *Would I say this in this way to this person's face?* If yes, it's probably fine.

## 5. Frequency & Priority

**Priorities for you**
1. **Natural openers and closers** (*Hey!, Talk soon!, Take care!*) instead of translated ones.
2. **Contractions and simple words.**
3. **Tone signals:** period vs. exclamation, emoji, ellipsis.
4. **Short, reactive replies** (*Sounds good, Love that, No worries*).
5. **Specific details** in stories (show, don't tell).
6. **Warmth phrases:** *so good to hear from you, thanks for thinking of me, I appreciate it.*
7. **Occasion patterns:** thanks, congrats, condolences, invitations, apologies.
8. **Register matching:** mirror the other person.
9. **Avoiding textbook formulas** (*I'm fine, thank you, and you?*).
10. **Basic accuracy:** verb forms, articles, prepositions.

## 6. American vs. British Notes
| Feature | US | UK |
|---|---|---|
| Friend address | *dude, man, bro, girl, guys, y'all (South)* | *mate, pal, love (regional), you lot* |
| Thanks | *Thanks! / Thank you so much! / I appreciate it.* | *Cheers! / Thanks a lot / Ta (informal) / Lovely, thanks* |
| Approval | *awesome, cool, sweet, great* | *brilliant, lovely, great, smashing (older), class (regional)* |
| Sadness/annoyance | *That sucks. / Bummer.* | *Gutted. / That's rubbish. / Annoying.* |
| Tired | *beat, wiped, exhausted* | *knackered, shattered* |
| Very pleased | *thrilled, stoked* | *chuffed, made up (regional)* |
| Kisses at end | uncommon among friends (more with family/partners) | *x* or *xx* common among friends |
| Agreement | *For sure. / Totally.* | *Absolutely. / Definitely. / Fair enough.* |
| No problem | *No problem. / No worries. / You're welcome.* | *No worries. / Not at all. / No bother.* |
| Understatement | less | more: *not bad* = good; *a bit annoying* = very annoying |
| Spelling | *color, favorite, realize* | *colour, favourite, realise* |
| Dates | *Nov 14* | *14 Nov* |
| Weekend phrase | *on the weekend* | *at the weekend* |

**Caution:** copy slang gradually and only after hearing it used in context. A misplaced "innit" or "bro" can sound like a costume.

### 7.2 Style and tone mistakes
| ❌ Habit | Effect | ✅ Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Very formal text to a friend | cold | *Hey! Running late, be there in 10.* |
| Long paragraph reply to a short text | heavy | match length |
| Using only periods in short texts | can seem serious or annoyed | use ! or no period |
| Overusing exclamation marks | can seem too intense or insincere | one or two per message |
| Emoji overload | may seem childish or unclear | 0–2 per message |
| Translating Spanish emojis/expressions (*jajaja*) | *haha* is standard | *haha* / *lol* |
| Skipping basic grammar (*I no have time*) | reduces clarity | *I don't have time* |
| Writing a "speech" in a post | unnatural | short lines, conversational |
| Stacking clichés in personal statements | forgettable | concrete detail |
| Using slang without understanding | sounds off | use sparingly |
| Not replying to a question in chat | feels ignored | acknowledge, then answer |
| Responding with "K" | may seem dismissive | *Okay!* / *Sounds good.* |
| Writing in all caps | seems like shouting | use sparingly |
| Mixing languages unclearly | may confuse | switch deliberately (with bilingual friends is fine) |
| Starting every message with "Hello, how are you?" | repetitive | vary: *Hey!*, *Quick question*, *Guess what?* |

### 7.3 Storytelling mistakes
| ❌ | ✅ |
|---|---|
| "Since I was a child, I have always been passionate about…" | Open with a specific moment. |
| Listing qualities (*I am hardworking, responsible, and dedicated*) | Show them through a short anecdote. |
| Switching tenses randomly | Keep past tense, or use present deliberately. |
| Explaining the moral at length | One line is enough. |
| Over-describing background | Start close to the action. |
| Using only general words (*nice, good, beautiful*) | Use specific details. |
| Overly long sentences | Mix lengths; break up. |

### 7.4 Attitude traps
- **"Casual writing doesn't matter."** It builds relationships and reputation.
- **"I should write casual messages perfectly."** Natural beats perfect.
- **"If I use slang, I'll sound native."** Slang used wrongly sounds forced.
- **"I must translate my Spanish affection exactly."** English warmth sounds different.
- **"Emoji are childish."** Used well, they add tone.

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ **"Casual English has no rules."** — It has strong conventions; they are just different.
- ❌ **"Fragments are mistakes."** — In casual and creative writing they're normal.
- ❌ **"Contractions are lazy."** — They're standard everywhere except very formal writing.
- ❌ **"A period at the end of a text is always correct."** — It can signal seriousness or irritation.
- ❌ **"More exclamation marks = friendlier."** — Moderation sounds more sincere.
- ❌ **"Emoji and abbreviations are only for teens."** — Adults use them widely, with norms.
- ❌ **"A personal statement should show how wonderful I am."** — It should show thoughtful, specific, honest growth.
- ❌ **"Long sentences sound more mature."** — Clear, varied sentences do.
- ❌ **"Everyone in English-speaking countries uses the same slang."** — It varies by country, region, age, and community.
- ❌ **"Don't start sentences with And or But."** — Fine in casual and most general writing.
- ❌ **"Lowercase texting means the person is careless."** — Often it signals relaxed closeness.
- ❌ **"Reading my message aloud is unnecessary."** — It's a great test for tone.

## 9. Pronunciation Note → Training Method (for writing)

### 9.1 The "text it to a friend" test
Read your message imagining it's going to a friend. Does it sound like you? If it sounds like a letter from a lawyer, loosen it up.

### 9.2 Collect real examples
Save 30 real messages, posts, and comments from native speakers that you like (with privacy in mind). Highlight openers, reactions, and closers. Build a "casual phrase bank."

### 9.3 Mirror and adapt
When replying to someone, match their length, punctuation, and emoji level. You'll quickly feel what's natural for that relationship.

### 9.4 Ten-minute freewrite
Write for 10 minutes about your day, in plain English, no stopping, no editing. Then circle the best three lines. This trains fluency and voice.

### 9.5 Show-don't-tell rewrite
Take five "telling" sentences (*I was happy, it was boring, she was nervous*) and rewrite each as a concrete image.

### 9.6 Cut the throat-clearing
Delete the first sentence of your message or post; if it still makes sense, keep it deleted.

### 9.7 Read aloud
Read your text aloud (Lesson 66). Where you stumble, simplify. Where it sounds flat, add rhythm or a detail.

### 9.8 Spanish-to-English intention table
Create a table: *Spanish phrase → intention → natural English* (see 2.5). Add new rows as you learn them.

### 9.9 Voice notes and writing
Record a voice note describing something, then transcribe and tidy it. This transfers spoken naturalness to writing.

### 9.10 Trouble spots
- Overly formal openers.
- Translated blessings and endearments.
- Unclear tone (periods, sarcasm without signals).
- Generic stories without details.
- Using slang you haven't heard in context.

## 10. Memory Technique

### 10.1 Mirror rule
Match the other person: length, punctuation, emoji.

### 10.2 One-line friend test
If a friend wouldn't say it aloud, don't text it.

### 10.3 Camera test for stories
Could a camera film this sentence? *My hands shook* (yes). *I was nervous* (no). Prefer camera sentences.

### 10.4 Three-part thanks
**Thank + specific + what it meant.**

### 10.5 Tone toolkit
Remember five tone tools: **punctuation, length, emoji, word choice, rhythm.**

### 10.6 Personal phrase bank
Keep 10 openers, 10 reactions, 10 closers you actually like.

### 10.7 Spaced retrieval cards
- *Translate "Saludos" in a casual message.* → **Hi! / Hey!**
- *Natural ending instead of "Besos"?* → **Hugs! / Talk soon! / Take care!**
- *"Felicitaciones por tu trabajo"?* → **Congrats on the new job!**
- *Period at the end of "Okay."?* → **can sound curt**
- *Show, don't tell: "I was nervous."?* → **My hands were shaking.**
- *Polite decline to an invitation?* → **Thanks so much for the invite! I can't make it, but I'd love to catch up soon.**
- *"How are you? I'm fine thank you and you?"?* → **How's it going? / Pretty good, you?**
- *Condolence line?* → **I'm so sorry for your loss. Thinking of you.**
Review on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30.

### 10.8 Weekly practice
Each week, write one post or story (150 words) and one thank-you or congratulations note to a real person. Send them (or keep them as drafts). Review how readers respond.

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1: Casual writing = spoken English on the page.**

> **Pattern 2: Tone lives in punctuation, length, emoji, and word choice.**

> **Pattern 3: Mirror the other person's register.**

> **Pattern 4: Contractions, fragments, and simple words are normal.**

> **Pattern 5: Short, reactive replies keep conversations flowing.**

> **Pattern 6: Thanks = specific + sincere.**

> **Pattern 7: Stories: hook → context → complication → climax → resolution → takeaway.**

> **Pattern 8: Show with concrete details; avoid general labels.**

> **Pattern 9: Affection in English: tone words and small phrases, not literal translations.**

> **Pattern 10: Personal statements: one specific story beats many claims.**

> **Pattern 11: Humor and sarcasm need signals in writing.**

> **Pattern 12: Basic grammar still matters, even when style is relaxed.**

## 12. Real-Life Examples

### 12.1 Same message, three registers
> **Formal:** *Dear Ms. Lopez, I regret to inform you that I will be unable to attend tomorrow's meeting. Please accept my apologies.*
> **Neutral:** *Hi Laura, I'm afraid I can't make tomorrow's meeting. Sorry about that. Can I catch up with you afterward?*
> **Casual:** *hey, can't make it tomorrow, sorry! catch up later?*

### 12.2 Text conversations
**1. Making plans**
> **A:** hey, free saturday?
> **B:** yeah! what's up?
> **A:** thinking of grabbing lunch around 1. you in?
> **B:** sounds good! where?
> **A:** that new place on 5th? heard the tacos are great
> **B:** love it. see you there 🙂

**2. Running late**
> **A:** omw but the bus is stuck, maybe 15 min late
> **B:** no worries, take your time!
> **A:** thx, sorry!!

**3. Sharing news**
> **A:** guess what?? i got the job!!!
> **B:** WHAT?! congrats!!! that's amazing, you totally deserve it
> **A:** thank you!! still can't believe it lol
> **B:** drinks to celebrate this week?

**4. Awkward/serious**
> **A:** hey, can we talk later? something's been on my mind
> **B:** of course. call me whenever you're ready.

**5. Tone check**
> **A:** Can you send me the file?
> **B:** Sure.
> **A:** Is everything okay?
> **B:** Yeah, sorry, just busy! Sending it now 🙂
(A short, period-ending "Sure." can read as cold; B repairs with a softener.)

### 12.3 Social posts
**Instagram caption**
> Sunday morning, no plans, good coffee. This is the life. ☕

**LinkedIn post (short story)**
> Last year I almost turned down a project because my English wasn't "perfect." My manager said, "Do you understand the problem? Then you can solve it." I took it. It was the best decision of my year. Lesson: clear beats perfect.

**Comment**
> This is so helpful, thanks for sharing! Saving it for later.

**Review**
> Cozy little café with excellent coffee. Staff were friendly and the pastries were fresh. It gets loud around noon, so go early. Would definitely come back.

### 12.4 Personal story: before and after
> ❌ *When I was a child, I was very nervous to speak English. One day I had to give a presentation and I felt very bad. But at the end everything was fine and I learned that I should believe in myself.*
> ✅ *On the morning of my first English presentation, my hands shook so hard that the slides rattled in my grip. I opened my mouth, and my mind went completely blank. Then I looked at my friend in the third row, who gave me a thumbs-up. I took a breath and started with the only sentence I remembered: "Good morning, everyone." The rest followed. I still get nervous, but I know now that blank moments pass.*

### 12.5 Notes for occasions
**Thank you**
> Hi Marta, thank you so much for helping me move last weekend. I honestly don't know how I would have done it without you. Dinner is on me next week!

**Congratulations**
> Congratulations on the baby! So happy for all of you. Can't wait to meet the little one.

**Condolences**
> I was so sorry to hear about your father. He was such a kind man. Thinking of you and your family, and here if you need anything.

**Invitation**
> We're having a small get-together on Saturday, 14 November, from 7 pm at our place. Food and drinks provided. Would love to see you! RSVP by Thursday.

**Apology**
> I'm really sorry I forgot your birthday. That's on me. Can I take you to lunch to make up for it?

**Get well**
> Sorry you're under the weather. Rest up, drink lots of tea, and let me know if you need groceries or anything.

### 12.6 Personal statement opening: before and after
> ❌ *I have always been passionate about helping people, and since I was a child I knew that I wanted to study medicine to contribute to society.*
> ✅ *At fourteen, I spent a summer translating for my grandmother at her doctor's appointments. I learned two things: how much a single sentence can frighten or comfort, and how little patients are told. Ten years later, that experience is why I want to study medicine.*

### 12.7 Spanish warmth, English warmth
| Spanish message | Natural English |
|---|---|
| Hola, querida amiga, ¿cómo estás? Espero que todo vaya muy bien. Un abrazo grande. | Hey you! How are you? Hope everything's going well. Big hug! |
| Muchas gracias por tu ayuda, eres un ángel. | Thank you so much for your help. You're a lifesaver! |
| Qué alegría saber de ti. Te extraño mucho. | So good to hear from you! I miss you so much. |
| Feliz cumpleaños, que Dios te bendiga. | Happy birthday! Wishing you an amazing year. |

## 14. Exercises

**A. Register shift.** Rewrite each formal message as a casual text:
1. "I would like to inform you that I will arrive approximately fifteen minutes late." 2. "Thank you very much for the invitation. Unfortunately, I will not be able to attend." 3. "Please let me know if you are available this Saturday for lunch."

**B. Make it more natural**
4. Greetings! How are you? I hope you are very well. 5. Congratulations by your new house! 6. I send you a big kiss and a hug. 7. Happy birthday for you, God bless you. 8. I'm waiting for your answer.

**C. Tone reading.** What tone does each message suggest?
9. "Fine." 10. "Fine!" 11. "fine 🙂" 12. "Sure... I guess." 13. "OMG yes!!!"

**D. Show, don't tell.** Rewrite each with a concrete image:
14. I was exhausted. 15. The party was boring. 16. He was very angry. 17. The city was beautiful.

**E. Notes for occasions.** Write a short message (2–4 sentences) for each:
18. Thank a friend for a gift (name the gift). 19. Congratulate a colleague on a promotion. 20. Offer condolences to a friend who lost a family member. 21. Invite friends to a barbecue on Saturday. 22. Decline an invitation kindly.

**F. Fix the errors**
23. I'm agree with you, is a great idea. 24. I'm very exciting for the trip. 25. Thanks God it's Friday! 26. How are you? I'm fine, thank you, and you? 27. Say to your family regards from me. 28. I'm boring, let's do something.

**G. Story rewrite.** Rewrite this opening to be specific and vivid:
"I have always loved cooking since I was a child, and now it is my passion."

**H. Social posts.** Write (a) an Instagram caption about your weekend, (b) a LinkedIn post (50–80 words) sharing a lesson from work, (c) a comment on a friend's post about a new job.

**I. Review.** Write a 50–70-word review of a restaurant, place, or product you know. Include an overall opinion, two specifics, and one criticism or tip.

**J. Mirror practice.** Take three messages (provided or from your own chats). For each, write a reply that matches the sender's length, tone, and punctuation.

**K. Personal statement.** Write a 100–120-word opening for a personal statement for a program of your choice, beginning with a specific moment.

## 17. Speaking Practice
(Oral storytelling transfers directly to writing.)

**Drill 1 — Two-minute story.** Tell a personal story using hook, complication, and takeaway. Record it; transcribe and edit.

**Drill 2 — Show don't tell, aloud.** Say five "telling" sentences, then rewrite them aloud as images.

**Drill 3 — Reactions.** Practice natural reactions to news: *Oh no! / That's amazing! / Wow, congrats! / That sucks. / No way!* Use different intonation (Lesson 68).

**Drill 4 — Voice notes.** Send a 30-second voice note to a friend describing your day; then write the same thing as a text.

**Drill 5 — Role-play.** With a partner, simulate chat: one sends messages; the other replies in the same register.

**Drill 6 — Reading aloud.** Read your texts and posts aloud; adjust anything that sounds unlike you.

**Drill 7 — Thanks and congrats.** Say five thank-you and five congratulation messages aloud, with specific details.

**Mini Quiz**
1. Punctuation, capitalization, length, emoji, and word choice.
2. A period can sound curt or serious; an exclamation mark sounds friendly.
3. Use concrete images and actions instead of general labels.
4. Hugs! / Talk soon! / Take care!
