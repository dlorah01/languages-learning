# Lesson 52 — Collocations I: Verb + Noun and Adjective + Noun
### Phase 5: Vocabulary as a System | Module 2 of 8

---

## 0. Where this fits
Lesson 51 gave you the **system** (capture, record, review, produce, recycle). This lesson supplies the first big block of **content** for your notebook: the two collocation types that cause the most Spanish-speaker errors and give the biggest gains in naturalness.

- **Verb + noun** (*make a decision, pay attention, reach an agreement*)
- **Adjective + noun** (*heavy traffic, strong evidence, a steep rise*)

Phase 2 (Lessons 29–36) already covered the core verbs *get, take, make, do, have, go, come, put, keep, hold, bring, run, turn, set, pass, break, fall*. Here we widen the lens: you will learn the **collocational logic** behind the choices, plus about 300 high-frequency chunks organized by noun, so they are retrievable by *situation* (the way you need them), not by alphabet.

Lesson 53 will continue with adverb + adjective and intensifiers.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
Think of every noun as a **house with its own set of regular visitors**. *Decision* has regular visitors: *make, reach, take (UK), postpone, reverse, announce, challenge*. *Attention* has others: *pay, draw, attract, catch, grab, call, devote*. A visitor who does not belong (*do a decision, make attention*) is not "wrong" in grammar. It simply never comes to that house.

And adjectives behave the same way: *heavy* visits *rain, traffic, smoker, burden, losses*; *strong* visits *coffee, wind, evidence, feeling, case*; *deep* visits *sleep, breath, trouble, concern, voice*. You do not choose between adjectives by dictionary meaning ("fuerte" = heavy? strong? deep?). You choose by **who lives next door**.

So the practical rule is:

> **For every noun you learn, learn its three or four closest verbs and its three or four closest adjectives.**

That is how natives store it. It is also why the notebook in Lesson 51 is organized by noun.

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 What a collocation is (and isn't)
A collocation is a combination of words that co-occur **more often than chance would predict**, and that native speakers **expect**. It is:

- **Not fully fixed** (unlike an idiom): *make a decision / make the decision / make a difficult decision / make decisions*.
- **Not fully free** (unlike a regular phrase): you cannot freely replace *make* with a synonym (*create a decision, build a decision*).
- **Arbitrary from a learner's point of view:** the reasons are historical and statistical, not logical. That is why Spanish logic fails.

### 2.2 The three-way strength scale
Collocations vary in strength. Learn the strong ones first.

| Strength | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Unique / very strong** | only one partner (or almost) | *foot the bill, shrug your shoulders, run a risk* (*take a risk* too), *commit a crime, perform surgery (operate)* |
| **Strong** | one or two dominant partners | *make a mistake, pay attention, heavy rain, bitterly cold, reach a conclusion* |
| **Medium** | several acceptable partners with preferences | *big/large/huge/major problem* (preferences differ by noun) |
| **Weak** | many partners (free combination) | *a red car, a new book* |

Spanish speakers lose most points on **strong** collocations: easy words in unexpected combinations.

### 2.3 Why verbs are the hard part
Spanish has a few very flexible light verbs (*hacer, tener, dar, tomar, poner*) that cover many English verbs. English splits that territory among dozens of verbs, each with its own noun partners:

| Spanish | English splits into… |
|---|---|
| **hacer** | *make* (a decision, progress, a mistake, an effort, a point, money, a call, a mess, a difference, friends) / *do* (homework, business, research, damage, harm, favors, your best, the dishes) / *have* (a look, a break, a go) / *take* (a break, a photo, a walk, a test) / *give* (a speech) / *throw* (a party) / *carry out* (a study) / *conduct* (an experiment) |
| **tener** | *have* (a problem, a meeting, a cold) / *hold* (a meeting, a view) / *be* + adj (*be 20, be hungry*) / *feel* / *get* (an opportunity) / *run* (a temperature, a fever) |
| **dar** | *give* (a talk, advice, an example) / *hold* (a party) / *deliver* (a speech) / *offer* (help) / *throw* (a party) / *pay* (a visit, a compliment) / *take* (a walk) / *cast* (a shadow) |
| **tomar** | *take* (a bus, a break, a photo, a decision) / *have* (a drink, a bath) / *drink* (water, tea) / *make* (a decision) / *catch* (a bus) / *grab* (a bite) |
| **poner** | *put* (on) / *set* (the table, a goal) / *turn on* / *lay* (the table, UK) / *give* (an example) / *place* (an order) / *play* (music) / *show* (a film) |
| **pasar** | *spend* (time) / *pass* (an exam, the salt) / *happen* / *go by* / *have* (a good time) / *experience* |
| **sacar** | *take out* / *get* (a good grade) / *take* (a photo) / *draw* (a conclusion) / *pull out* |
| **cometer** | *make* (a mistake) / *commit* (a crime) |
| **prestar** | *pay* (attention) / *lend* (money) / *provide* (a service) |
| **realizar** | *carry out / conduct* (a study) / *make* (a call) / *complete* / *achieve* (goals) / *perform* |

*Practical rule:* when you reach for *hacer, tener, dar, tomar, poner,* STOP and ask, "What is the English verb for this noun?" Check a collocation dictionary the first few hundred times; after that your ear will begin to know.

### 2.4 Light verbs, delexical verbs
In English, verbs such as *make, do, take, have, give, get, go, put* often carry little meaning of their own in collocations; the **noun** carries the meaning. These are called **light** (or **delexical**) verbs:

- *have a look* = look; *take a walk* = walk; *give a laugh* = laugh; *make an attempt* = attempt.

Natives often prefer the light-verb version in informal speech because it allows modifiers: *have a quick look, take a long walk, give a short laugh*. This is a feature of English style (Lesson 1). Learn it as a pattern.

### 2.5 Adjective + noun: preferences, not meanings
Adjectives that mean roughly the same pick different nouns:

| | **high** | price, temperature, standards, hopes, risk, quality, rate, pressure |
| | **great** | importance, deal, interest, success, majority, pleasure, difficulty |
| | **serious** | problem, injury, illness, doubts, concern, crime |
| | **severe** | weather, pain, shortage, damage, punishment, criticism |
| Big | **big** | decision, mistake, difference, deal, risk, surprise |

| | **huge** | success, amount, impact, profits, crowd |
| | **major** | problem, change, role, factor, issue, city |
| | **vast** | majority, amount, area, range, experience |
| | **sizeable / considerable / substantial** | sum, amount, income, difference, progress (formal) |
| Small | **slight** | chance, difference, increase, delay, accent |

| Quick | **fast** | food, car, growth |

This table is the key to ending "which adjective?" doubt: look at the noun's regular neighbors.

### 2.6 Order of adjectives in a noun phrase
When you stack adjectives, English prefers an order (approximately): **opinion → size → age → shape → colour → origin → material → purpose + noun.**
- *a lovely small old round brown Italian leather writing desk* (extreme example).
- Natural: *a beautiful old house; a big red car; a small wooden box; a long, difficult journey.*

Use two adjectives at most in speech.

### 2.7 Noun + noun and "of" patterns (bridge to Lesson 53)
Many collocations are **noun + noun** (*traffic jam, labour market, interest rate*) or **a [unit] of [noun]** (*a piece of advice, a bit of luck, a sense of humour, a round of applause, a bar of chocolate*). We treat these inside the networks below.

## 3. Why Native Speakers Do This
Same answer as Lesson 51, plus one more: **collocations are the grammar of meaning.** Just as grammar tells you where words go, collocations tell you which words belong together. Natives do not decide among *make/do* each time. They retrieve *make a mistake* as a unit. Their errors are rare, and when they occur, they are slips (*"make" for "do" under pressure*), not systematic.

Also, collocation carries **register**. *Reach a verdict* is legal; *come to a decision* is neutral; *make up your mind* is informal. Using the right level shows control.

---

## 4. When to Use / When Not To
- **Use** the strongest, most idiomatic collocation for the noun in everyday writing and speech.
- **Upgrade** plain verbs with precise ones in academic and professional writing: *raise concerns, draw attention to, reach a consensus, face challenges, conduct research, voice an opinion*.
- **Don't** chase rare collocations to sound sophisticated. A natural, common collocation always beats an exotic one used wrongly.
- **Don't** create collocations by analogy with Spanish. If you are not sure, check a collocation dictionary or corpus.
- **Don't** over-literalize "light verb + noun" when a single verb is better: *make a visit to* vs. *visit* (formal writing often prefers the single verb: *The committee will review* rather than *will carry out a review of*; speech prefers the light-verb version: *Let's have a look*).
- **Careful with prosody:** *cause* and *suffer* go with bad things; *achieve, enjoy, benefit from* go with good things.

### 7.3 Partitive ("a piece of") errors (uncountables)
- ❌ *an advice* → ✅ **a piece of advice / some advice**
- ❌ *informations* → ✅ **information / a piece of information**
- ❌ *a news* → ✅ **a piece of news / some news**
- ❌ *a furniture* → ✅ **a piece of furniture**
- ❌ *a luggage* → ✅ **a piece of luggage / a bag**
- ❌ *much people* → ✅ **many people**
- ❌ *a progress* → ✅ **progress / some progress / a lot of progress**
(See Lesson 19 for quantifiers.)

### 7.4 Other patterns
- **Preposition errors in collocations:** *interested in* (not *on*), *depend on*, *result in/from*, *good at*, *a solution to / for* a problem, *a reason for*, *an increase in*, *a key to*, *an answer to*, *a cause of*, *an effect on*, *the importance of*.
- **Tense/aspect:** *I'm making a decision* vs. *I've made a decision* (resultative). Don't use *I did a decision.*
- **Article:** *take a break*, *have a look*, *make a mistake* (singular needs *a*): ❌ *make mistake*.
- **Plural vs. singular:** *make progress* (no article, no plural), *make a lot of progress*; *make friends* (plural), *make a friend* (rare); *make money* (no article), *make a living*.
- **False friends that wreck collocations:** *actual* ≠ *actual (current)*; *eventually*; *sensible*; *assist*; *realize*; *pretend*; *preoccupied*; *succeed*; *attend*; *carpet*; *constipated*; *embarrassed*; *library/librería*. Pair each with the correct collocation.

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ **"If two English words both translate the same Spanish word, they are interchangeable."** — Choose by noun: *heavy/strong/deep* may all be "fuerte."
- ❌ **"Do is for activities and make is for creation."** — Helpful as a tendency, but unreliable: *make a mistake* (not creation), *do damage* (not activity?). Memorize noun-by-noun (Lesson 31).
- ❌ **"A 'wrong' collocation is just a little unnatural."** — To natives, a wrong collocation can be as noticeable as a wrong preposition.
- ❌ **"Collocations are idioms."** — Idioms have non-literal meaning; collocations are literal but habitual.
- ❌ **"There are too many to learn."** — Learn them by *noun hub* (a few hundred hubs cover most daily communication).
- ❌ **"More formal collocations always sound better."** — Match the register to the context.
- ❌ **"Corpus data is for linguists."** — Free web tools let learners check a collocation in seconds.
- ❌ **"Collocations never change."** — They evolve (*commit suicide* → *die by suicide* in sensitive contexts; *crack the whip* is dated).

## 9. Pronunciation Note
1. **Collocations as rhythm groups.** Collocations are said in one breath group, with stress on the **noun** (the information-carrying word) more than the verb:
   - *make a DEcision* /meɪk ə dɪˈsɪʒn/ (main stress on *-ci-*)
   - *pay atTENtion*
   - *HEAvy TRAFfic* (adjective + noun: both stressed in compounds of new information, but the noun usually gets the nuclear stress)
   - *reach an aGREEment*
2. **Compound nouns stress the first element:** *a TRAFfic jam, a LAbor market, a BANK account, an INterest rate.* Compare adjective + noun: *a heavy TRAFfic* (not a compound).
3. **Weak forms inside chunks:** *a, the, of, to, for* reduce: *a piece of advice* /ə ˈpiːs əv ədˈvaɪs/ ("a piece-a advice").
4. **Link verb + article:** *make a mistake* → /ˈmeɪ kə mɪˈsteɪk/; *take a look* → /ˈteɪ kə ˈlʊk/.
5. **Troublesome sounds in key collocations**
   - *decision* /dɪˈsɪʒn/ (the /ʒ/ sound, as in *vision*)
   - *attention* /əˈtɛnʃn/ (stress on -ten-)
   - *mistake* /mɪˈsteɪk/ (no vowel before *st*; Spanish speakers often say "emistake")
   - *effort* /ˈɛfərt/ (stress first syllable)
   - *progress* noun /ˈprɑːɡrɛs/ (US) vs. verb /prəˈɡrɛs/
   - *advice* /ədˈvaɪs/ (noun, /s/) vs. *advise* /ədˈvaɪz/ (verb, /z/)
   - *conclusion* /kənˈkluːʒn/
   - *research* noun /ˈriːsɜːrtʃ/ (US often /rɪˈsɜːrtʃ/)
6. **Practice method:** shadow the collocation inside a full sentence, not alone. Record yourself and listen for the stress on the noun.

## 10. Memory Technique

### 10.1 Organize by noun hub (the "house" method)
For each key noun, make a mini-map with **verbs on the left** and **adjectives on the right**:

```
 make / reach / take (UK) / postpone / reverse   →  DECISION  ←  tough / difficult / final / snap / wise
```

Practice by cueing yourself with the noun: *"Decision — go!"* and recite verbs and adjectives aloud.

### 10.3 Mnemonics for the "make vs. do" core
- **make** = you *produce* a result that did not exist (a plan, a mistake, a call, progress, money, a decision, noise).
- **do** = you *perform* a general activity or task (homework, chores, business, research, damage, a favor).
- Exceptions are memorized: *make the bed, do the dishes, make a mistake* (not a task!).

### 10.4 Use "light verb + noun" sentence frames
Memorize frames and swap nouns:
- *Let's **have a** ___ .* (look / chat / break / go / drink / meeting)
- *I need to **make a** ___ .* (decision / call / plan / point / suggestion)
- *Can you **give me a** ___ ?* (hand / lift / ring / hint / call)
- *She **took a** ___ .* (break / photo / risk / seat / walk / look)
- *We **reached a** ___ .* (decision / conclusion / compromise / agreement)

### 10.5 Spaced-retrieval card designs
- **Cloze:** *After weeks of debate, the committee finally ___ a decision.* → **reached / made**
- **Noun cue:** *Verbs for "attention": ___ ___ ___.* → pay, draw, attract
- **Spanish cue (bridge):** *"prestar atención"* → ____ → **pay attention**
- **Error-correction:** *"Please make attention."* → **Please pay attention.**
- **Adjective cue:** *"___ rain, ___ traffic, ___ smoker"* → **heavy**

### 10.6 Personalize
For each noun hub, write one sentence about your real life: *Last week I made a big mistake at work.* *I'm paying attention to my sleep.* *I've reached a decision about my studies.* Personal links make the chunk stick (self-reference effect).

### 10.7 The "check three" habit
Whenever you use a verb + noun or adjective + noun in writing and feel doubt, **check three sources** within thirty seconds: (1) a collocation dictionary, (2) a corpus or concordancer, (3) a quoted web search. If you cannot confirm it, choose a safer chunk.

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1: Noun decides the verb.** Don't ask "which verb means hacer?" Ask "what does this noun usually come with?"

> **Pattern 2: Light verb + noun lets you add adjectives.** *Have a look → have a quick look → have a good look → have a closer look.* That is why natives love it.

> **Pattern 3: Success/failure pairs.** *achieve / reach / accomplish a goal* vs. *miss / fail to meet / fall short of a goal*; *win / lose* a game or argument; *pass / fail* an exam; *keep / break* a promise; *meet / miss* a deadline; *solve / face* a problem.

> **Pattern 4: Start–continue–end sets.** *start / launch / set up a business* → *run / manage / operate a business* → *close down / shut down / sell a business*. Learn collocations as life cycles.

> **Pattern 5: Positive vs. negative verbs for the same noun.** *Enjoy / suffer* (good/bad) *success, health, life*; *face / overcome / deal with / tackle a problem*; *raise / address / ignore a concern*.

> **Pattern 6: Intensity ladders for the same noun.** *light / moderate / heavy rain*; *slight / significant / dramatic increase*; *minor / serious / major problem*; *mild / severe pain*; *modest / considerable / substantial gains*; *small / large / vast majority.*

> **Pattern 7: Uncountable nouns need a partitive.** *a piece of advice / information / news / evidence / furniture / luggage; a bit of luck / fun; a sense of humor; an item of clothing; a round of applause; a stroke of luck; a spell of bad weather; a burst of energy.*

> **Pattern 8: Prosody tells you the verb.** *Cause, provoke, spark, trigger, suffer, face, run (a risk), pose (a threat)* → problems/negative; *achieve, enjoy, earn, gain, seek, boost, enhance* → positive.

> **Pattern 9: Formal upgrade for neutral verbs.** *get an answer → receive/obtain; make a mistake → commit an error (formal); have a talk → hold discussions; say your opinion → express/voice an opinion; do research → conduct research; make an attempt → undertake an attempt (rare)*.

> **Connection to Lessons 29–36 (core verbs):** those lessons introduced the verbs; this lesson organizes the nouns around them. Use them as two views of the same network.

> **Connection to Lesson 43 (dependent prepositions):** nouns often have their own prepositions: *a reason for, an increase in, a solution to, an effect on, a cause of.* Store them as part of the collocation.

> **Connection to Lesson 51 (chunks):** every noun hub is a notebook page; every verb+noun pair is a chunk.

> **Connection to Lessons 19 and 20 (quantifiers):** countable vs. uncountable nouns determine the partitive and the adjective (*a large number of* vs. *a large amount of*).

**Mini Quiz**
1. There's no logical rule; the pairing is a matter of convention and frequency in the language, so Spanish logic can't predict it.
2. Because *rain* habitually takes *heavy*; *strong* is reserved for other nouns (*wind, coffee, evidence*). Different nouns select different adjectives.
3. *make* (a decision), *do* (homework), *take* (a photo/an exam), *have* (a party/a look), *give* (a speech), *throw* (a party), *carry out* (a study). Any five.
4. A verb with little meaning of its own, where the noun carries the meaning: *have a look, take a walk, make an attempt, give a laugh.*
5. *A piece of* (advice, information), *a bit of* (luck), *a sense of* (humor), *an item of* (news), *a round of* (applause). Any three.
6. Because retrieval is cued by the noun in real communication; networks are stronger than lists, and alphabetical order produces interference.
7. *Cause* tends to occur with negative things (problems, damage); *achieve* with positive things (goals, success). Using them against their prosody sounds off.
8. Compound nouns stress the first element (*TRAFfic jam*); adjective + noun stress the noun (*heavy TRAFfic*, nuclear stress).
9. Verify uncertain collocations in a collocation dictionary, a corpus, and a quoted web search before using.
10. *Earn/make money*; *miss the bus.*
11. *Make progress* is the established collocation; *do progress* sounds wrong because *progress* does not take *do*. It's an arbitrary convention.
12. *A solution to/for*; *an increase in*; *an effect on* (also *a reason for, a cause of, a key to, an answer to*).
