# Lesson 54 — Synonym Precision: Fine-Grained Differences
### Phase 5: Vocabulary as a System | Module 4 of 8

---

## 0. Where this fits
Lessons 52 and 53 taught which words **go together**. This lesson asks the next question: when several English words seem to mean the same thing (*happy, glad, pleased, delighted, thrilled*), **which one is exactly right?**

This is where advanced learners most often plateau. A B2 learner knows ten words for "big." A near-native speaker knows that *a large number*, *a huge success*, *a vast majority*, *a major problem*, and *a great deal* are not interchangeable, and feels the difference without thinking.

The skill is not memorizing more words. It is learning to **compare** words along a small set of dimensions, so that each new synonym slots into a map instead of becoming another loose item. You will build that map here, and you will use it again in Lesson 55 (register ladders) and Lessons 56–58 (academic, professional, and idiomatic vocabulary).

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
Imagine a paint shop. "Red" is on the label of twenty tins: *scarlet, crimson, maroon, rust, cherry, burgundy*. A painter does not say "they all mean red, so any will do." Each is a different shade, with a different mood, for a different wall.

Synonyms work the same way. English has an unusually large stock of near-synonyms because it borrowed from three layers:

| Layer | Source | Typical feel | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Germanic** (Old English) | everyday core | short, concrete, informal | *ask, begin, help, think, give up* |
| **French** (after 1066) | law, society, taste | polite, formal-neutral | *inquire, commence, assist, consider, surrender* |
| **Latin / Greek** (learned) | science, scholarship | technical, abstract, formal | *interrogate, initiate, facilitate, contemplate, capitulate* |

So *ask / inquire / interrogate* are not three copies of one idea. They are three **layers**, each with its own feel. That history is the reason natives sense levels of formality without being taught.

> **Core idea:** there are almost no true synonyms. Every pair differs in at least one of **meaning, intensity, register, collocation, connotation, or grammar**. Your job is to find which one.

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 The six dimensions of difference
Use these six questions every time you compare two words.

| # | Dimension | Question | Example contrast |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Denotation (core meaning)** | Do they refer to exactly the same thing? | *steal* (take) vs. *rob* (a person/place) vs. *burgle* (a building) |
| 2 | **Intensity** | Is one stronger than the other? | *annoyed < angry < furious < livid* |
| 3 | **Register** | Formal, neutral, informal, slang? | *purchase / buy / pick up / snag* |
| 4 | **Collocation** | Which partners does each take? | *strong evidence* but *powerful argument*; *heavy rain* not *strong rain* |
| 5 | **Connotation** | Positive, negative, or neutral feeling? | *slim* (+), *thin* (neutral), *skinny* (−), *scrawny* (−−) |
| 6 | **Grammar pattern** | Does it take different structures? | *suggest that I go* / *recommend that I go* / *advise me to go* |

A seventh question helps with verbs: **who or what is the subject/object?** (*lend* vs. *borrow*, *rob* vs. *steal*, *rise* vs. *raise*).

### 2.2 Denotation vs. connotation
- **Denotation** = dictionary meaning.
- **Connotation** = the emotional "color" a word picks up from how it is used.

| Neutral | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| *determined* | *resolute, persistent* | *stubborn, pig-headed, obstinate* |
| *thrifty* | *economical, frugal* | *stingy, tight, miserly* |
| *confident* | *self-assured* | *arrogant, cocky, smug* |
| *curious* | *inquisitive* | *nosy, prying* |
| *old* | *experienced, mature, vintage* | *elderly (polite), ancient, decrepit* |
| *slim* | *slender, lean, fit* | *skinny, bony, scrawny* |
| *cheap* | *inexpensive, affordable, economical* | *tacky, shoddy, cheap and nasty* |
| *child-like* | *innocent, youthful* | *childish, immature* |
| *different* | *unique, distinctive* | *odd, weird, peculiar* |
| *political* | *principled* | *opportunistic* |

Spanish speakers often choose by dictionary meaning alone and accidentally insult people: *I think you are very **fat*** instead of *You've put on a bit of weight* (or better, saying nothing). *Skinny* and *thin* are not the same as *delgado*.

This pattern is called **emotive conjugation** (a joke from the BBC series *Yes, Minister*): *I am firm, you are stubborn, he is pig-headed.* Same trait, three attitudes.

### 2.3 Intensity ladders
Words in a semantic field can be ordered by strength. Memorize the ladder, not the isolated words.

| Field | Weak → Strong |
|---|---|

| **Happy** | *content → pleased → happy → delighted → thrilled → ecstatic / overjoyed* |
| **Sad** | *down → unhappy → sad → miserable → heartbroken / devastated* |

| **Surprised** | *surprised → amazed → astonished → astounded → flabbergasted / stunned* |
| **Funny** | *amusing → funny → hilarious → side-splitting* |
| **Big** | *big → large → huge → enormous → gigantic / colossal / vast* |
| **Small** | *small → little → tiny → minute / minuscule* |
| **Good** | *fine → good → great → excellent → outstanding → superb / exceptional* |
| **Bad** | *poor → bad → awful → terrible → dreadful → appalling* |

### 2.5 Collocational restriction (the "same meaning, different company" problem)
A big source of errors. Examples:

| Meaning | Pairs that work | Pairs that don't |
|---|---|---|
| high amount | *large number, great deal, vast majority* | *big number of, high number of people* (rare) |
| quick | *quick glance, rapid growth, fast food, swift action, speedy recovery, prompt reply* | *rapid food, fast glance* |
| strong | *strong coffee, strong evidence, strong wind, strong accent* | *powerful coffee, strong engine* (use *powerful*) |
| high | *high price, high temperature, high standards* | *tall price, big temperature* |
| tall/high | *tall person, tall building, tall tree* / *high mountain, high ceiling, high shelf* | *high person* |
| deep | *deep sleep, deep voice, deep trouble* | *heavy sleep* (rare), *deep rain* |
| broad / wide | *wide road, wide range* / *broad shoulders, broad smile, broad daylight* | *broad road* (rare) |
| big mistake / major problem / huge success / great importance | each noun picks | |
| damage vs. harm vs. injure vs. hurt | *damage* (objects), *harm* (abstract/people), *injure* (accidents), *hurt* (pain, feelings) | *damage a person's feelings* (use *hurt*) |

### 2.6 Grammar patterns that differ
Even when meaning matches, structure can split:

| Verb | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **suggest** | that + subject + base/should; -ing | *She suggested (that) we leave / suggested leaving.* ✗ *suggested me to leave* |
| **recommend** | that…; -ing; object + to? (no: *recommend* + object + to ✗) | *I recommend that you go / recommend going.* |
| **advise** | object + to; that…; -ing | *She advised me to go.* / *She advised going.* |
| **persuade / convince** | object + to / that | *persuaded me to go; convinced me that* |
| **allow / permit** | object + to; -ing | *allowed me to go; permitted him to go; allows parking* |
| **let** | object + base verb | *let me go* (✗ *let me to go*) |
| **make / force** | object + base / object + to | *made me go* / *forced me to go* |
| **lend / borrow** | give / receive | *Can you lend me?* / *Can I borrow?* |
| **rob / steal** | rob someone of; steal something from someone | *They robbed the bank.* / *They stole my wallet.* |
| **say / tell** | say (that) / tell someone (that) | *He said that…* / *He told me that…* |
| **speak / talk** | speak a language, speak to; talk with/about | *speak English; talk about work* |
| **look / watch / see** | look at; watch movement; see result | *look at the board; watch TV; see a doctor* |
| **hear / listen** | hear (involuntary) / listen to (deliberate) | *I heard a noise; I listen to music.* |
| **raise / rise** | transitive / intransitive | *raise prices; prices rise* (Lesson 12) |
| **lay / lie** | transitive / intransitive | *lay the book down; lie on the bed* |
| **fun / funny** | enjoyable / humorous or odd | *a fun party; a funny joke* |
| **economic / economical** | of the economy / cheap to use | *economic growth; economical car* |
| **historic / historical** | important in history / about history | *a historic win; historical novel* |
| **classic / classical** | typical, great / of antiquity | *classic mistake; classical music* |
| **sensible / sensitive** | reasonable / easily hurt | *sensible choice; sensitive skin* |

## 3. Why Native Speakers Do This
Because meaning in English is **distributed across a crowd of near-synonyms**, native speakers learn the differences from exposure, not from definitions. They know *content* is quieter than *thrilled* the way you know a whisper from a shout. Choosing precisely does three jobs:

1. **Accuracy.** *The results were **significant*** (important) vs. *the results were **substantial*** (large). In a research paper, those are different claims.
2. **Attitude.** *He's **thrifty*** (admiring) vs. *he's **stingy*** (critical).
3. **Social positioning.** *I'm **pleased** to inform you* (formal) vs. *I'm **glad** to tell you* (neutral) vs. *I'm **chuffed** to bits* (British informal).

In practice natives also **avoid repetition** (elegant variation): they rotate *said / added / noted / pointed out / explained*. Learners who repeat *said* eleven times in a paragraph sound plain; but learners who vary without precision (*he exclaimed, retorted, gasped* in every line) sound overwritten. Control matters more than variety.

## 4. When to Use / When Not To
**Use precise synonyms when**
- You write formally (reports, essays, cover letters) and need to avoid repeating basic words.
- You want to signal **attitude** (e.g., *determined* vs. *stubborn*).
- You need a **stronger or weaker** word than the default.
- You're giving feedback and want to **soften** (*challenging* instead of *difficult*).

**Don't**
- Don't use a rare synonym just to impress. Use it only if you've seen it in context with the same meaning and collocation.
- Don't trade a natural, simple word for a formal one in conversation (*purchase* instead of *buy* in a café).
- Don't use a thesaurus blindly. A thesaurus lists *meanings*, not *collocations or register*. Always check a learner's dictionary and example sentences.
- Don't use strong words for mild situations (*I'm devastated that the meeting is at 3* sounds ironic or dramatic).
- Don't change the structure when you swap verbs (*suggest me to…* ✗, *explain me* ✗: Lesson 7/20 pattern).
- Don't assume Spanish cognates carry the same weight (*molestar* vs. *molest*, *constipado*, *embarazada*, *sensible*: see 7.1).

## 5. Frequency & Register
**Frequency priorities (learn these dimensions first)**
1. The ten or twelve fields in Section 2.7 (happy, sad, angry, tired, big, important, good, say/tell, look/see, think, help, start/end).
2. Verbs of communication, because they carry attitude and are used everywhere: *say, tell, ask, answer, explain, mention, suggest, claim, admit, argue*.
3. Adjectives of evaluation, because they appear in every opinion: *good, bad, important, difficult, interesting, strange, useful, serious*.

**Register**
- **Everyday core:** short Germanic words (*start, ask, help, show, get*).
- **Professional:** French-derived (*begin, inquire, assist, demonstrate, obtain*).
- **Academic/learned:** Latinate (*initiate, interrogate, facilitate, illustrate, acquire*).
- **Phrasal verbs** (Phase 4) usually stand at the informal end: *find out / discover / ascertain*; *put off / postpone / defer*; *look into / investigate / examine*.

**Rule of thumb:** to move a sentence *up* in formality, replace phrasal verbs with their Latinate equivalents; to move it *down*, replace Latinate verbs with phrasal verbs or Germanic words. Lesson 55 builds the full ladder.

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

### 7.1 False friends (cognates that diverge)
| Spanish | ❌ Wrong English | ✅ Correct |

| *asistir (a clase)* | assist the class | **attend** (*assist* = help) |
| *constipado* (= cold) | constipated | **have a cold** (*constipated* = can't go to the bathroom) |

### 7.2 Choosing by one dictionary translation
- ❌ *He is very **old** (viejo).* → ✅ *elderly* (polite), *older*, *aged*
- ❌ *You are **fat**.* → ✅ (best avoided) *heavy / overweight* (clinical) / *you've put on weight*
- ❌ *She is very **ugly**.* → ✅ (avoid) *not very attractive / plain*
- ❌ *My boss is very **cheap**.* → ✅ *stingy / tight-fisted* (person) vs. *cheap* (price)
- ❌ *a **cheap** salary* → ✅ *a low salary*
- ❌ *It's a **strong** price* → ✅ *a high price*
- ❌ *I **ask** a question to him* → ✅ *ask him a question*
- ❌ *I **say** to him that…* → ✅ *I **tell** him that…* / *I **say** that…*
- ❌ *I **speak** with you about…* → ✅ *I **talk** to you about…* (*speak* is more formal/one-way)
- ❌ *He **told** "hello"* → ✅ *He **said** hello.*
- ❌ *I **look** TV* → ✅ *I **watch** TV.*
- ❌ *I **see** a movie* → ✅ *I **watch / see** a movie* (both ok; *watch* at home, *see* at the cinema)
- ❌ *Listen the music* → ✅ *Listen to the music.* (*Hear* = without trying.)
- ❌ *I **am agree*** → ✅ *I **agree**.*
- ❌ *Win money* → ✅ *earn / make money.*
- ❌ *Gain a salary* → ✅ *earn a salary.*
- ❌ *Lose the bus* → ✅ *miss the bus.*
- ❌ *I **want** that you come* → ✅ *I want you to come.*

### 7.4 Overusing a single synonym
- Using *very good* for everything (Lesson 53).
- Using *nice* as the universal positive adjective (*nice* is weak in academic writing).
- Using *thing, stuff, a lot of* as default nouns (replace with precise nouns).
- Using *said* in every dialogue tag—fine in fiction; but in academic writing vary *argues, notes, claims, states, suggests, points out* **accurately**.
- Using *big* for everything: *big problem* ✅ but *big importance* ✗ → *great importance*.

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ **"Synonyms are interchangeable."** — Almost never. They differ in at least one of the six dimensions.
- ❌ **"A thesaurus gives me the right word."** — It gives candidates. You must check collocation, register, and connotation.
- ❌ **"More complex words sound more intelligent."** — Precision does; unusual words used wrongly do the opposite.
- ❌ **"Formal English = long words."** — Formal English is about *structure and precision*, not length. *Show* can be better than *demonstrate*.
- ❌ **"If Spanish has one word, English must have one."** — English often splits what Spanish unites (*saber/conocer* = know; *ser/estar* = be; *tomar* = take/drink/catch).
- ❌ **"Cognates are safe."** — Cognates often have shifted meaning (7.1).
- ❌ **"Strong words are always better."** — Overusing strong words ("devastated," "absolutely horrible") exhausts their power.
- ❌ **"Negative words are rude."** — Sometimes accurate negative words are necessary (*flawed, inadequate, misleading*); the skill is to be precise and polite.
- ❌ **"Dictionary labels (formal/informal) are enough."** — Native intuition also uses collocation and context. Check examples.
- ❌ **"A synonym family is a list."** — It's a **map** (scale + register + collocation).

## 9. Pronunciation Note
1. **Stress shifts with suffixes.** Many synonyms differ in suffix and thus stress:
   - *ECOnomy → ecoNOMic → ecoNOMical* (/ˌɛkəˈnɑmɪk/, /ˌɛkəˈnɑmɪkl/)
   - *HIStory → hisTORic → hisTORical* (/hɪˈstɔːrɪk/)
   - *deMONstrate → demonSTRAtion*
   - *SIGnificant → sigNIFicance*
2. **Minimal pairs among confusable synonyms**
   - *quite* /kwaɪt/ – *quiet* /ˈkwaɪət/ – *quit* /kwɪt/
   - *later* /ˈleɪtər/ – *latter* /ˈlætər/
   - *affect* /əˈfɛkt/ – *effect* /ɪˈfɛkt/
   - *accept* /əkˈsɛpt/ – *except* /ɪkˈsɛpt/
   - *advice* /ədˈvaɪs/ – *advise* /ədˈvaɪz/
   - *loose* /luːs/ – *lose* /luːz/
   - *principal* /ˈprɪnsəpəl/ – *principle* /ˈprɪnsəpəl/ (same sound!)
   - *sensible* /ˈsɛnsəbəl/ – *sensitive* /ˈsɛnsətɪv/
   - *beach* /biːtʃ/ – *bitch* /bɪtʃ/ (avoid this error!)
   - *sheet* /ʃiːt/ – *shit* /ʃɪt/ (same warning)
3. **Strength = stress and pitch.** Upper-ladder words are said with more emphasis and wider pitch: *"I was FURIOUS."* vs. *"I was a bit annoyed."* The prosody must match the word's intensity.
4. **Understatement intonation.** *"Not bad."* (low, slow) = good. *"That's interesting."* (flat) = doubtful. Synonym choice and intonation work together; *fascinating* with a flat tone sounds sarcastic.
5. **Syllable counts of formal synonyms.** Latinate words are longer, so rhythm slows: *begin* /bɪˈɡɪn/ → *commence* /kəˈmɛns/; *help* /hɛlp/ → *facilitate* /fəˈsɪlɪteɪt/. Check word stress in a dictionary and say them aloud.
6. **Spanish-speaker trouble spots:** *th* in *thrilled, thorough, thought*; /ʒ/ in *pleasure, measure*; /dʒ/ vs /j/ in *major, joy*; consonant clusters in *exhausted* /ɪɡˈzɔːstɪd/, *strength* /strɛŋkθ/, *depressed* /dɪˈprɛst/; avoid initial vowel before *st-/sp-* (*"estressed"* ✗).

## 10. Memory Technique

### 10.1 The Contrast Chart (your core tool)
For any group of near-synonyms, create a chart with these columns:

| Word | Strength (1–5) | Register | Typical partners | Connotation | Example (your own) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *pleased* | 2 | neutral-formal | with the results; to meet you | + | *I was pleased with the feedback.* |
| *delighted* | 4 | neutral | absolutely delighted | ++ | *I'm delighted to announce…* |
| *thrilled* | 4 | informal | about the news | ++ | *She was thrilled about the offer.* |

Build **one chart per week** for one family (happy, big, say, look…). The act of comparing is the learning.

### 10.2 The Intensity Thermometer
For gradable fields (anger, happiness, fear, tiredness), draw a vertical thermometer, place words from weakest to strongest, and tag each with its intensifier (Lesson 53): `a bit annoyed → very angry → absolutely furious`. This links Lessons 53 and 54.

### 10.3 Sentence-slot test
To find the right word, use a **slot test**: write the sentence with a gap and try each candidate aloud.
> "We were ___ by the news." (surprised / astonished / shocked / stunned / flabbergasted)
> If you've heard *shocked by* or *taken aback by* many times, those feel right; *flabbergasted by* is possible but marked (humorous).

### 10.4 The "Cousin Test" (register)
Imagine saying the word to your cousin over coffee. If it sounds odd, it is formal. Imagine writing it in a legal contract. If it sounds odd, it is informal. Use that range to place words.

### 10.5 Emotive conjugation exercise
Write three versions of the same fact with different attitudes:
- *She's determined / persistent / stubborn.*
- *He's thrifty / careful with money / stingy.*
- *They are confident / self-assured / arrogant.*
This trains connotation sensitivity.

### 10.6 Retrieval cards with scenarios
Use situation prompts, not definitions:
- Front: *Your friend's good news. You are happy but not wildly so. Which word?* → **glad / pleased**
- Front: *The most extreme word for "angry."* → **furious / livid**
- Front: *You steal from a person on the street with violence.* → **mug**
- Front: *"The wind was ___" (a very strong word for cold).* → **bitter / biting**

### 10.7 Replace-and-compare (editing habit)
Once a day, take a paragraph from your own writing, highlight one or two weak words (*good, bad, nice, thing, big, very*), and replace each with a precise option. Then check the new word in a learner's dictionary for collocation and register. Record the pair in your notebook with a "trap" note.

### 10.8 Spanish-bridge with caution
When you find an English synonym, write the **Spanish nuance** you want to express, not the dictionary word. Example: *pleased: satisfecho con el resultado; delighted: encantado (más fuerte); thrilled: emocionado (energía alta)*. You are mapping **feelings**, not words.

### 10.9 Spaced review
Review charts on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30 (Lesson 51). Test yourself by **choosing**, not recognizing: "Which word fits?"

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1: The six-dimension check.** Meaning, intensity, register, collocation, connotation, grammar. If two words fail on any one dimension, they are not interchangeable.

> **Pattern 2: Germanic = casual, French = polite, Latin = learned.** *Ask / inquire / interrogate; help / assist / facilitate; end / finish / terminate; buy / purchase / acquire.*

> **Pattern 3: Upper ladder = ungradable.** *Furious, exhausted, delighted, enormous, terrible* take *absolutely/utterly*, not *very* (Lesson 53).

> **Pattern 4: Same fact, different attitude** (emotive conjugation): *firm / stubborn; thrifty / stingy; curious / nosy; confident / arrogant.* Choose the connotation you intend.

> **Pattern 5: Negative-prosody verbs.** *Cause, provoke, trigger, suffer, endure, tolerate, admit, confess, claim, allege, blame* often come with bad news. Positive: *achieve, earn, enjoy, praise, welcome, celebrate.*

> **Pattern 6: Reporting verbs encode stance.** *State/announce* (neutral), *claim/allege* (doubt), *admit/concede* (reluctant truth), *insist/maintain* (firmness), *point out/note* (neutral emphasis), *argue/contend/assert* (academic stance).

> **Pattern 7: Verbs of perception split by effort.** *See / hear / notice* (no effort) vs. *look at / listen to / watch* (effort); *stare / glare / glance* (manner).

> **Pattern 8: Physical → abstract extension.** *Sharp* (knife) → *sharp mind*; *heavy* (box) → *heavy responsibility*; *bright* (light) → *bright student*; *warm* (temperature) → *warm welcome*.

> **Pattern 9: Euphemism ladder.** *Dead → passed away → no longer with us*; *fired → let go → made redundant (UK)*; *old → elderly → senior*; *poor → disadvantaged → low-income*; *problem → issue → challenge → opportunity.* Euphemisms are polite synonyms; know when they are appropriate and when they obscure.

> **Pattern 10: Synonym + preposition.** The same meaning may require different prepositions: *angry **about** a decision / **with** a person / **at** a situation*; *different **from/to/than***; *similar **to***; *afraid **of***; *proud **of***; *responsible **for***; *aware **of***; *surprised **at/by***; *pleased **with/about***. See Lesson 43.

> **Connection to Lesson 52:** collocation is the main tiebreaker among synonyms (*heavy rain* but *strong coffee*).

> **Connection to Lesson 53:** intensifiers depend on gradability, which depends on the word's position on the ladder.

> **Connection to Lesson 51:** each contrast chart is a notebook page; each synonym is a chunk with its partners.

> **Connection to Lesson 55 (next):** register ladders are synonym families sorted by formality.

> **Connection to Phase 6:** informal synonyms (*mate, pal, mad, knackered, gonna*) enter natural speech.

## 12. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1 — Basic differences**
- *I'm **glad** you came.* (specific news)
- *I'm **happy** with my job.* (general)
- *He's **tall**; the mountain is **high**.*
- *Please **tell** me the truth; he **said** he was busy.*
- *I **watch** TV; I **look at** the board; I **see** a bird.*

**Level 2 — Intensity**
- *I was **annoyed**, then **angry**, and finally **furious**.*
- *The film was **good**; the acting was **excellent**; the ending was **outstanding**.*
- *She was **tired**, then **exhausted**, then **completely drained**.*
- *The test was **hard**; the exam was **gruelling**.*

**Level 3 — Register**
- *We **commence** at nine.* (formal) / *We **start** at nine.* (neutral) / *We **kick off** at nine.* (informal)
- *Please **inform** me.* / *Please **let me know**.* / *Keep me in the loop.*
- *I **require** your assistance.* / *I **need** your help.* / *I need a hand.*

**Level 4 — Connotation**
- *She's **determined**; he's **stubborn**.*
- *He's **economical**; she's **stingy**.*
- *They are **self-confident**; that guy is **cocky**.*
- *Our **curious** daughter; the **nosy** neighbor.*
- *A **slim** model; a **skinny** teenager; a **scrawny** cat.*

**Level 5 — Native-like precision**
- "The report is **significant**, not just **substantial**: it **reveals** a pattern rather than merely **indicating** a trend."
- "I wouldn't say he's **lying**; he's being **economical with the truth**."
- "She **admitted** the mistake, but he only **acknowledged** it, and the CEO merely **conceded** that 'issues' had arisen."
- "The evidence **suggests**, but does not **prove**, that the policy **reduced** crime; it certainly didn't **eliminate** it."
- "The team felt **disheartened**, not **devastated**: they were **disappointed** by the loss, **frustrated** by the delays, but still **hopeful**."

**Reporting-verb ladder (same speech, different stance)**
- *"We will raise prices," said the CEO.* (neutral)
- *…, **announced** the CEO.* (public statement)
- *…, **admitted** the CEO.* (reluctantly)
- *…, **claimed** the CEO.* (doubt)
- *…, **insisted** the CEO.* (firm)
- *…, **warned** the CEO.* (negative consequence)
- *…, **hinted** the CEO.* (indirect)
- *…, **confessed** the CEO.* (guilt)

**Mini-text: before and after**
> ❌ *"The movie was very good. The actors were very good and the story was very good. The ending was very bad."*
> ✅ *"The film was gripping. The acting was superb, the plot kept me guessing, and the ending was a letdown."*

**Drill 1 — Ladder recitation (rising intonation):**
> "Annoyed… angry… furious… livid!"
> "Pleased… happy… delighted… ecstatic!"
> "Big… large… huge… enormous… gigantic!"

**Drill 2 — Same fact, three attitudes (say with matching tone):**
- "She's determined… persistent… stubborn."
- "He's thrifty… economical… stingy."
- "They're confident… self-assured… arrogant."

**Drill 3 — Register switch.** Say each sentence three times: formal, neutral, informal.
- *We commence at nine. / We start at nine. / We kick off at nine.*
- *I require your assistance. / I need your help. / I need a hand.*

**Drill 4 — Reporting-verb stance.** Say the same sentence with each verb: *"The system is safe," he **said / claimed / admitted / insisted / warned / hinted**.* Change tone to match.

**Drill 5 — Precision challenge (60 seconds).** Describe your last holiday without using *very, nice, good, bad, big, thing.* Record yourself and replace any weak words you used.

**Drill 6 — Role-play.**
Partner: "What did you think of the proposal?"
You: "Honestly, I found it promising but not entirely convincing. The budget is ambitious rather than realistic, and the timeline looks a bit optimistic. That said, the core idea is genuinely strong."

**Mini Quiz**
1. Denotation, intensity, register, collocation, connotation, grammar pattern.
2. Denotation = core dictionary meaning; connotation = emotional coloring. Example: *thrifty* (+) vs. *stingy* (−).
3. *Furious* is an extreme (ungradable) adjective; it takes *absolutely/utterly/completely*: *absolutely furious.* (Or use gradable *very angry*.)
4. Describing the same trait with different attitudes: *I'm firm, you're stubborn, he's pig-headed.*
5. Any three: *actually→currently; assist→attend; constipated→have a cold; embarrassed→pregnant; eventually→possibly; sensible→sensitive; realize→carry out; molest→bother; library→bookstore; carpet→folder.*
6. *Say* + (that) clause, no indirect object; *tell* + person + (that) clause (or *tell the truth/a story*).
7. *See* = perceive without effort; *look at* = direct your eyes deliberately; *watch* = follow movement over time.
8. *Economic* = relating to the economy; *economical* = cheap to use, not wasteful.
9. It lists meanings but not collocation, register, or connotation. You must check examples in a learner's dictionary.
10. Germanic = casual, French = polite/neutral, Latin = learned. E.g., *ask / inquire / interrogate.*
11. Strength, register, typical partners, connotation, and your own example sentence.
12. *Claimed* (doubt), *admitted* (reluctant truth), *insisted* (firmness), *warned* (negative consequence), *hinted* (indirect). Any three.
