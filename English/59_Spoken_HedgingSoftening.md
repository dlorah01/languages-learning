# Lesson 59 — Hedging and Softening Language
### Phase 6: Natural, Spoken English | Module 1 of 5

---

## 0. Where this fits
Phase 5 gave you a vocabulary system. Phase 6 turns it into **natural speech**. We start with the small words that do the most work in everyday English and that Spanish speakers most often leave out: *kind of, sort of, I guess, I think, maybe, a bit, just, I was wondering if, to be honest.*

You have met pieces of this already:
- **Lesson 53:** intensifiers and downtoners (*very, fairly, slightly*).
- **Lesson 55:** register and politeness as an axis.
- **Lesson 56:** academic hedging (*tend to, appear to, it may be that*).
- **Lesson 57:** softening requests and disagreement at work.

This lesson brings them together for **spoken, informal-to-neutral English**, where hedging is not optional decoration. It is part of the grammar of being polite and being believable.

> **Core idea:** in English, a message with no softening can sound rude, arrogant, or childish. A message with too much can sound weak or evasive. Fluency means **dialing** the softness up or down on purpose.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
Imagine every sentence has two dials:

1. **Certainty dial:** how sure am I? (*It's cold* → *It's kind of cold* → *I think it might be cold.*)
2. **Force dial:** how strongly am I pushing the listener? (*Close the door.* → *Could you close the door?* → *I was wondering if you'd mind closing the door.*)

**Hedges** turn the certainty dial down. **Softeners** turn the force dial down. Often one word does both: *I guess we could leave now* is less certain *and* less pushy than *We should leave now.*

Why do it? Because English speakers treat the **listener's freedom** as important. A bare command or a flat contradiction limits it. A softened version leaves room for the listener to say no, disagree, or add information, and that is considered polite and respectful.

Spanish also softens (*¿Podrías…?, quisiera, tal vez, como que, más o menos, o sea, digamos*), but in different places and in different ways. The most common problem is not that Spanish speakers are rude. It is that they **translate the directness of the Spanish situation into English**, where the same sentence lands harder. Compare:

| Spanish (normal) | Literal English | What the listener hears |
|---|---|---|
| *Dame el reporte.* | *Give me the report.* | an order from a boss |
| *No, eso está mal.* | *No, that's wrong.* | a hostile correction |
| *Quiero un café.* | *I want a coffee.* | a child, or a very direct customer |
| *Eso no es verdad.* | *That's not true.* | an accusation |

None of these is "wrong" grammar. They are **wrong force**.

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 The vocabulary of softening: a map

| Category | What it does | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Epistemic hedges** | show uncertainty about truth | *I think, I guess, I suppose, maybe, perhaps, probably, might, could, seems, apparently, as far as I know* |
| **Approximators** | make quantity or category fuzzy | *about, around, roughly, or so, kind of, sort of, more or less, -ish, like, ten or twelve* |
| **Downtoners / understaters** | reduce intensity | *a bit, a little, slightly, somewhat, fairly, rather, a tad, not very* |
| **Shields (attribution)** | move responsibility elsewhere | *According to…, They say…, I heard…, It's said that…, From what I understand…* |
| **Politeness softeners (requests)** | reduce pressure | *could, would, would you mind, I was wondering if, if you don't mind, possibly, just, perhaps* |
| **Face-saving openers** | prepare for disagreement/bad news | *I'm afraid…, Unfortunately…, With respect…, I hate to say this, but…, Don't get me wrong, but…* |
| **Vagueness markers (rapport)** | keep tone casual and shared | *and stuff, or something, or whatever, and so on, that sort of thing, you know* |
| **Tag questions / question forms** | invite agreement | *It's cold, isn't it? / That's a bit expensive, don't you think?* |
| **Frankness markers** | signal honest (often unwelcome) content | *to be honest, honestly, frankly, to tell you the truth, I have to say* |

### 2.2 Epistemic hedges: "how sure am I?"
A scale you can actually use:

| Certainty | Phrases |
|---|---|
| **~100%** | *It is… / Definitely… / Without a doubt…* |
| **~90%** | *Probably… / I'm pretty sure… / I'd say… / It looks like…* |
| **~70%** | *I think… / I believe… / It seems… / I reckon (UK)… / I figure (US)…* |
| **~50%** | *Maybe… / Perhaps… / It might be… / I guess… / I suppose…* |
| **~30%** | *It's possible that… / It could be… / There's a chance that…* |
| **~0–10%** | *I doubt… / I don't think… / I'd be surprised if…* |

**Key grammar points**
1. **Position of *maybe / perhaps / probably*.**
   - *Maybe* usually goes at the **start**: *Maybe he's late.* (Also possible after a comma at the end, informally.)
   - *Perhaps* is similar but slightly more formal.
   - *Probably* goes **before the main verb** (after *be* and the first auxiliary): *He probably forgot. / He is probably at home. / He will probably come.* ❌ *Probably he will come* is not natural in standard speech.
   - *Might (just)* and *may* are modals: *He might be late.* Do not write ❌ *He maybe is late.*
2. **Negative raising: *I don't think…* (not *I think… not*).**
   - ✅ *I don't think she's coming.*
   - ❌ *I think she isn't coming.* (understandable, but unnatural and stronger)
   - Spanish says *Creo que no viene*; English prefers moving the negation up. This is one of the most important softening patterns, since the negative is made less direct.
   - The same goes for *I don't suppose, I don't expect, I don't believe, I can't imagine.*
3. **Short answers:** *I think so. / I don't think so. / I guess so. / I hope so. / I hope not.* (Not ❌ *I think yes / I think no.*)
4. **Seems / appears / looks like:** *It seems (that) he's upset. / He seems upset. / It looks like rain.* *Seems like* is common in US informal speech: *Seems like a good idea.*
5. **I feel like…** (US informal) = *I think…*: *I feel like we should wait.* It frames an opinion as a feeling, which is harder to argue with. In UK English, *I feel that…* is more common in writing; *I feel like* is spreading.

### 2.3 Approximators: *kind of, sort of, like, -ish, or so*

| Item | Function | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **kind of / sort of** | soften an adjective, verb, or statement | *It's kind of cold. I sort of liked it. He's sort of famous.* |
| **a kind/sort of + noun** | category member that doesn't fit perfectly | *It's a sort of café, a kind of bar.* |
| **-ish** | roughly | *We'll meet at eightish. It's reddish. He's tallish.* |
| **or so / or something / or whatever** | approximate amount/type | *about ten minutes or so. Let's grab coffee or something.* |
| **like** | approximator (also a filler; Lesson 60) | *It cost like fifty bucks.* |
| **more or less** | roughly, mostly | *We're more or less finished.* |
| **around / about / roughly / approximately** | numbers | *around 300 people* |
| **a little / a bit** | softener for adjectives | *a bit late, a little tired* |
| **pretty** (US informal) | fairly/quite | *It's pretty good.* (= good, not great) |

**Notes**
- *Kind of / sort of* are spoken as **/ˈkaɪndə/ and /ˈsɔːrdə/** ("kinda, sorta"). In writing, use *kind of / sort of* in informal text, *somewhat / rather* in neutral-formal.
- *Sort of* is slightly more common in UK; *kind of* in US. Both are everywhere.
- ❌ *I kind of like it very much.* (contradiction). Don't combine a downtoner with a strong intensifier.
- *Kind of / sort of* can also be a **response**: *Are you tired? — Kind of.* = a little.

### 2.4 Downtoners and understatement (link to Lesson 53)

| Word | Strength | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **slightly, a little, a bit, a tad** | weakest | *a bit* is the most common spoken form |
| **somewhat, rather, fairly** | medium | *rather* is more British/formal; *fairly* = moderately |
| **quite** | **UK: moderate (≈ fairly); US: strong (≈ very)** | *The film was quite good.* UK = decent; US = very good |
| **pretty** | US informal ≈ fairly | *pretty good* |
| **not very / not that / not too / not all that** | negative softener | *not very good* (= bad) is gentler than *bad* |

**Understatement with negation (very British, but widely used)**
- *It's not bad.* = it's good.
- *It wasn't the best presentation.* = it was poor.
- *I'm not entirely convinced.* = I disagree.
- *That's not quite what I meant.* = You misunderstood.

### 2.5 Request softeners (the "politeness ladder")
From most direct to most indirect:

1. *Close the door.* (imperative; blunt)
2. *Close the door, please.*
3. *Can you close the door?* (ability question, informal)
4. *Could you close the door?* (neutral polite)
5. *Would you mind closing the door?* (very polite; note **-ing**; reply: *Not at all / Sure*)
6. *I was wondering if you could close the door.* (indirect)
7. *I don't suppose you could close the door, could you?* (very indirect, very British)
8. *I hate to bother you, but would it be possible to…?* (formal/deferential)

**What makes each step softer? Four mechanisms:**
- **Modal verbs** (*can → could → would*): distance from reality equals less pressure.
- **Past tense for present meaning:** *I was wondering…, I wanted to ask…, I was hoping…, I thought you might…* The past tense creates psychological distance.
- **Progressive aspect:** *I'm wondering if… / I was thinking that…* makes it tentative.
- **Embedding in a longer frame:** *I was wondering if…, Do you think you could…, Is there any chance that…* Longer = more polite.

**The word *just***
- *I just wanted to check…, Could you just send me the file?, I just have a quick question.*
- *Just* minimizes the request ("this is small"). It is extremely common in friendly speech and emails.
- **Caution:** overused in professional writing, it can sound apologetic and weaken authority (*I just wanted to follow up… I was just wondering…*). Use it once, not in every sentence.

**Please, thanks, and tags**
- *Could you send me the file, please?* One *please* is enough.
- *You couldn't send me the file, could you?* (negative question + tag; polite, expects a possible "no").
- *Thanks in advance.* (once)

### 2.6 Softening disagreement and bad news

**The four-part disagreement**
1. **Acknowledge:** *I see what you mean. / That's a fair point.*
2. **Soften:** *I'm not sure… / I'm a bit concerned… / I'm afraid…*
3. **Give your view:** *…that it'll work in that timeframe.*
4. **Offer a bridge:** *Could we try… instead? / What if we…?*

> *I see your point, but I'm not sure it'll work in that timeframe. What if we split it into two phases?*

| Blunt | Softer |
|---|---|
| *You're wrong.* | *I don't think that's quite right. / I see it a bit differently.* |
| *That's a bad idea.* | *I'm not sure that's the best way to go about it.* |
| *No.* | *I'm afraid that won't be possible. / I'd rather not, if that's OK.* |
| *I don't like it.* | *It's not really my thing. / I'm not a big fan.* |
| *You made a mistake.* | *There seems to be a small error here.* |
| *You're late.* | *We were expecting you a bit earlier.* |
| *I disagree.* | *I'm not sure I agree. / I'm not entirely convinced.* |
| *I don't understand you.* | *Sorry, I'm not sure I follow. / Could you say that again?* |

**Face-saving openers (warn the listener something unwelcome is coming)**
- *I'm afraid…, Unfortunately…, I hate to say it, but…, With all due respect…, No offense, but…, Don't take this the wrong way, but…, I don't want to be rude, but…, To be honest…, Frankly…*
- **Caution:** *No offense, but…* and *With all due respect…* often **signal** an offense is coming. They don't actually protect the listener; they warn them. Use a genuinely softer frame (*I might be missing something, but…*) when you can.

### 2.7 Attribution and shields
Speakers distance themselves from claims they can't verify or don't want to own:
- *Apparently the meeting is canceled.* (I heard it)
- *From what I understand, the budget was approved.*
- *They say it'll rain tomorrow.*
- *As far as I know / As far as I can tell, he's still in Madrid.*
- *If I remember correctly, it starts at nine.*
- *Correct me if I'm wrong, but…*
- *I could be wrong, but…*
- *I might be missing something, but…*

These protect **your credibility** if you turn out to be mistaken.

### 2.8 Vague language for rapport
Vague language is not sloppy; it is a social signal. *Let's grab a coffee or something* is friendlier than *Let us meet in a café at 4:00 p.m.* Typical markers:
- *and stuff, and things like that, or whatever, and so on, and all that, or something like that, that kind of thing, you know, sort of thing*
- Vague nouns: *thing, stuff, whatever, a lot of, a bunch of, a couple of* (*a couple of* = about two, often more loosely).
- Vague quantifiers: *loads of (UK), tons of, heaps of (UK/AUS), a ton of (US)*.

**Don't use vague language** when precision matters (contracts, instructions, data, safety, exams).

### 2.9 Frankness markers: *to be honest, honestly, frankly*
Counterintuitively, *to be honest* is **not** a softener in function. It signals that "what comes next is my real opinion", and is typically followed by something less than flattering. But it functions socially as a **cushion**: it frames the opinion as a personal confession.
- *To be honest, I didn't love the ending.* (opinion, softened)
- *Honestly, it's not great.* (stronger)
- *Frankly, I think we should stop.* (formal or slightly stern)
- Overuse implies that your other statements are *not* honest; don't put it in every sentence.

### 2.10 Over-hedging: when softness backfires
Sociolinguistic research on "powerless language" shows that stacking hedges lowers perceived competence and confidence:
- ❌ *Um, I just kind of wanted to maybe ask if you could possibly look at this, if you have time, sorry.*
- ✅ *Could you look at this when you have a moment? I'd like your feedback by Friday.*

**Rules of thumb**
1. **One hedge per idea** is usually enough.
2. **Hedge opinions and predictions; never hedge facts or commitments.** ✅ *I think it'll take two weeks.* ❌ *I'll maybe send it on Friday, I guess.* → ✅ *I'll send it on Friday.*
3. **Be soft on the person, hard on the problem.** (Lesson 57.)
4. **Soften the delivery, not the content.** If you must say "no", make it clear.
5. **Match the audience.** Senior audiences want a clear bottom line; friends tolerate vagueness.

## 3. Why Native Speakers Do This
1. **Politeness theory (face).** Every speaker wants to be respected (*positive face*) and left free to act (*negative face*). Direct orders and contradictions threaten both. Hedges lower the threat.
2. **Accuracy.** English speakers value not overstating. *I think* or *it seems* reflect honest uncertainty. In a culture where being wrong in public is embarrassing, hedging protects credibility.
3. **Rapport and equality.** Vague, casual language says "we are equals; I'm not lecturing you."
4. **Conflict avoidance.** Many English-speaking cultures prefer indirect disagreement and understatement, particularly in the UK, but also in the US South, Canada, and professional settings.
5. **Thinking time.** *Sort of, I mean, you know, kind of* give planning time without silence.
6. **Habit and group identity.** Heavy use of *like, kinda, I guess* signals youth and informality; light use signals formality.

It is also **not universal**: Spanish-speaking cultures often show warmth through directness, closeness, and emphasis. Colombian Spanish, in particular, is rich in diminutives (*un momentico, un favorcito, ahorita*) and conditional politeness (*me regalas…*, *me colaboras con…*, *sería tan amable de…*). You already have the instinct for softening; you just need **English tools**.

## 4. When to Use / When Not To

**Use hedges and softeners to…**
- make requests, offers, invitations, and refusals;
- disagree, correct, or give feedback;
- report uncertain information;
- give opinions on matters that are open to debate;
- keep casual conversation relaxed;
- be honest about approximation (*about 200, or so*);
- deliver bad news.

**Do NOT hedge when…**
- you need to be **clear and urgent** (safety, emergencies, instructions): *Stop. Move back.* not *Maybe we could step back a little?*
- stating **facts you know**: *The meeting is at 3.* not *I think the meeting is kind of at 3.*
- making **commitments**: *I'll deliver on Friday.*
- negotiating from strength (too many hedges signal weakness);
- **writing precise technical or legal text**;
- you're answering a **direct yes/no question** and your answer is yes/no: *Is the file ready? — Yes.* (Not: *Well, I think it's kind of ready, more or less.*)
- the hedge **contradicts** the sentence (❌ *I'm very kind of tired*).
- you are already in a hesitant state; adding more makes you sound lost.

**Also remember:** hedging is not lying. Don't use *I think* to hide that you actually know, and don't use vague language to avoid responsibility.

## 5. Frequency & Register

**Priority order**
1. *I think / I don't think / I guess / maybe / probably* (certainty).
2. *Could you…? / Would you mind…? / I was wondering if…* (requests).
3. *a bit / a little / kind of / sort of / quite / pretty / fairly* (downtoners).
4. *I'm afraid / unfortunately / to be honest / I'm not sure* (face-saving).
5. *just / or something / and stuff / whatever* (casual vagueness).
6. *apparently / as far as I know / if I remember correctly* (shields).
7. *seems / appears / tends to* (more formal; Lesson 56).

**Register**
| Level | Typical items |
|---|---|
| **Very informal / spoken** | *kinda, sorta, I dunno, or whatever, and stuff, like, y'know, I guess* |
| **Informal–neutral** | *I think, maybe, a bit, kind of, I was wondering if, probably, to be honest* |
| **Neutral–formal** | *perhaps, somewhat, rather, I'm afraid, it seems, if I may, would you mind* |
| **Formal/academic** | *it appears that, tends to, may well, to a certain extent, arguably, presumably* |

**Frequency note:** *I think, maybe,* and *just* are among the most common items in English speech. *Kind of* and *sort of* rank very high in informal conversation. *Perhaps* is more frequent in writing and in British speech; *maybe* dominates US speech.

## 6. American vs. British Notes

| Feature | US | UK |
|---|---|---|
| **General style** | positive framing, enthusiasm, "softening by warmth" (*That's a great idea! What if we also…*) | understatement, indirectness, "softening by distance" (*That's quite an interesting idea. I wonder if we might…*) |
| **"I think" variants** | *I feel like… / I figure… / I guess…* | *I reckon… / I suppose… / I dare say…* |
| **quite** | strong (≈ very): *quite good* = very good | moderate (≈ fairly): *quite good* = decent; *quite brilliant* = very |
| **rather** | formal, rare | common: *rather good, rather tired* |
| **pretty (adv.)** | common: *pretty good* | less common; *fairly* more typical |
| **fairly** | rare in speech | common |
| **Tag questions** | *Right? You know?* (*It's cold, right?*) | *isn't it? don't you think? wasn't it?* |
| **Requests** | *Can I get a coffee? Could you…?* | *Could I possibly…? I don't suppose you could…? Would you mind…?* |
| **Apology reflex** | *Sorry* for real mistakes, *excuse me* for attention | *Sorry* as a reflex (bumping, asking, interrupting): *Sorry, could you tell me the time?* |
| **Disagreement** | *I see it differently. I'm not sure about that.* | *I'm not sure that's quite right. With respect…* |
| **"Interesting"** | usually positive | often polite for "I disagree" |
| **"That's brave"** | rare | "that's a risky/bad idea" (Yes Minister style) |
| **"Not bad" / "not too bad"** | OK to good | good; "not bad at all" = very good |
| **"Mind you"** | rare | *Mind you, it was cheap.* (on the other hand) |
| **"I'll bear that in mind"** | rare | polite for "probably not" |
| **"With all due respect"** | direct pushback | more frequently sarcastic/distancing |
| **"Cheers"** | rare as thanks | thanks, goodbye, or toast |

**Decoding British indirectness (a famous table, adapted):**
| British says | Often means |
|---|---|
| *I hear what you say.* | I disagree and don't want to discuss it. |
| *With the greatest respect…* | You are an idiot (or nearly). |
| *That's not bad.* | That's good. |
| *Quite good.* | Fairly good, not very. |
| *Perhaps you could give that some more thought.* | Redo it. |
| *I'll bear it in mind.* | I probably won't do it. |
| *Oh, by the way…* | The main point of the conversation. |
| *I'm sure it's my fault.* | It's your fault. |
| *Could we consider some other options?* | I don't like this one. |
| *That's an interesting idea.* | I don't like it. |

**US decoding:**
| American says | Often means |
|---|---|
| *Let's circle back.* | Not now. |
| *We should grab lunch sometime!* | Polite goodbye (not an invitation) unless a date is set. |
| *That's a great start.* | It needs a lot of work. |
| *I hear you, but…* | I don't agree. |
| *No worries.* | It's fine. |
| *Interesting.* | Often neutral or doubtful. |

**Practical tip:** with unknown listeners, soften in the *Neutral* zone (*I think, could you, a bit*), and listen for how they speak; mirror their level of directness.

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

### 7.1 Too direct (translation of Spanish directness)
| ❌ Error | ✅ More natural | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| *I want a coffee.* | *Could I have a coffee, please? / I'd like a coffee.* | *I want* sounds childish or abrupt |
| *Give me the menu.* | *Could I see the menu, please?* | |
| *Tell me your name.* | *Could I get your name?* / *What's your name?* | |
| *Close the window.* | *Could you close the window, please?* | |
| *Move!* (to a person) | *Excuse me, could I get past?* | |
| *You are wrong.* | *I don't think that's quite right.* | |
| *No, that's not true.* | *Hmm, I'm not sure that's the case.* | |
| *I don't like your idea.* | *I'm not sure that'll work.* | |
| *Don't do that.* | *It might be better not to do that. / I'd rather you didn't.* | |
| *You must come.* | *You should come! / You have to come, it'll be fun.* | *must* = obligation; *should/have to* friendlier |

| *Do you speak English? No? Then bring someone.* | *Excuse me, is there someone who speaks English?* | |

### 7.3 Over-hedging and "too apologetic"
- *Sorry, sorry, my English is not good, I'm sorry, but can you maybe help me a little, please?* → *Excuse me, could you help me with something?*
- Constant self-correction (*I think, maybe, I'm not sure*) in presentations or interviews → decide first, state the claim, then add **one** limitation: *The data suggests costs will fall by 10%, although this depends on supplier prices.*
- Using *just* in every sentence in emails.
- Over-thanking.

| *regálame / colabórame* | *gift me / collaborate me* | *could you…, would you mind…* |

### 7.5 Cultural misreads
- **Hearing hedges as "no commitment."** *We should definitely get lunch sometime* might not be an invitation. *I'll try to be there* may mean "no". *I'll think about it* may mean "no".
- **Hearing softened criticism as praise.** *That's interesting. Maybe you could think about adding more data.* may mean "this needs serious work".
- **Taking "How are you?" literally.** It's a greeting, not a request for medical details (Lesson 60).
- **Missing the ladder.** If someone moves from *Could you…?* to *I was wondering if…* to *I don't suppose…*, they're signaling serious reluctance to impose; respond generously.
- **Treating "I'm afraid" as fear.** It's a polite frame (*I'm afraid I can't*), not an emotion.

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ **"Hedging is weakness."** — Calibrated hedging is competence. Researchers hedge every claim in publications because precision matters. In conversation, hedges show tact.
- ❌ **"Natives use *kind of / sort of / like* because they are lazy."** — They do real work: precision, politeness, rapport.
- ❌ **"The more polite words, the better."** — Excess is clumsy and untrustworthy. Choose one or two well-placed softeners.
- ❌ **"*I think* always means doubt."** — It often marks an opinion rather than doubt (*I think we should go*), and can be a polite way to state a firm view.
- ❌ **"*To be honest* is a politeness phrase."** — It marks frankness and can precede a harsh opinion.
- ❌ **"*Please* makes any request polite."** — *Give me the report, please* is still a command. Grammar and modals matter more than *please*.
- ❌ **"Americans are always direct and British always indirect."** — Both vary hugely; US business culture can be very positive and indirect ("great start!"), and British people can be blunt with friends.
- ❌ **"Softening is dishonest."** — It's honest if the message remains clear. Dishonest softening hides the message.
- ❌ **"*Can you…?* is rude."** — It's fine among peers and in informal situations; *Could you…?* is a safer default.
- ❌ **"Fillers like *you know* and *I mean* are errors."** — They are a normal part of spoken fluency (Lesson 60 covers them).
- ❌ **"I should avoid *just*."** — Used sparingly, it's friendly. Overused in professional writing, it weakens.

### 9.2 Intonation is part of the softening
- **Rising tone on requests** sounds friendlier: *Could you ↗ help me?* A flat or falling tone sounds like a demand.
- **Falling-rising tone** (a fall then rise) expresses reservation: *I think so ↘↗* (= probably, but I have doubts), *It's fine ↘↗* (= it isn't).
- **A slight rise at the end of a hedge** invites response: *It's kind of expensive, isn't it? ↗*
- **A smile** changes pitch and softens the voice. It's audible, even on the phone.
- **Tone beats words.** *Could you help me?* with sharp falling tone and no smile can still sound rude.

### 9.3 Pause and chunking
Place hedges **at the edges of chunks**: *I think | we should wait.* *To be honest | I'm not sure.* Don't break them: ❌ *I | think we | should wait.*

### 9.4 Trouble spots for Spanish speakers
- **/ɪ/ in *think, guess, bit, did, it*:** short, not "eet."
- **/θ/ in *think, thing, something*:** tongue between teeth, not /s/ or /t/.
- **/h/ in *honest, hour* is silent; /h/ in *hope, however* is pronounced.**
- **Reduced *of*:** "kinda", "sorta", "lotta" (but only in speech).
- **Word stress:** *per-HAPS, a-PPA-rent-ly, PRO-ba-bly, HON-est-ly.*
- **Don't add a vowel before s-clusters:** *sort of, some, such, suppose* (not "e-sort", "e-suppose").
- **Linking:** *I was wondering if → "I was wondering-if"* (linked), *kind of → "kinda"*.

## 10. Memory Technique

### 10.1 The two-dial method
Before you speak, ask:
1. **Certainty dial:** 100%? 70%? 40%? → choose *is / I think / might*.
2. **Force dial:** order? request? suggestion? → choose *Do it / Could you / I was wondering if*.

Practice labeling any sentence by dial settings. It becomes automatic.

### 10.2 The "pad–point–offer" formula
For delicate messages: **Pad** (soft opener) + **Point** (clear content) + **Offer** (way forward).
> *I'm afraid* (pad) *the report isn't ready yet* (point). *I can send it by Friday if that works* (offer).
> *I might be missing something* (pad), *but I don't think the numbers add up* (point). *Could we go through them together?* (offer).

### 10.3 Soften the edge, not the message
Imagine a knife with a clear message: you only cover the **blade** (tone), not the message (content). If the listener can't tell what you want, you've over-softened.

### 10.4 Ladder cards
Make flashcards with one function on the front and the ladder on the back:
- Front: *Ask someone to repeat.* → Back: *What? → Sorry? → Sorry, could you say that again? → I'm sorry, I didn't quite catch that.*
- Front: *Say you disagree.* → *No. → I don't agree. → I'm not sure I agree. → I see it a bit differently.*
- Front: *Decline an invitation.* → *No. → I can't. → I'm afraid I can't make it. → I'd love to, but I've already got plans.*

### 10.5 Translate the *function*, not the words
Instead of translating *Quiero un café*, ask: "what do I want to do?" → "order politely" → *Could I have a coffee, please?* Use function-based phrase banks (Lesson 57).

### 10.6 Spot-the-softener listening
Watch one 5-minute scene from a series or interview. Tally every hedge/softener you hear (*I think, kind of, a bit, I was wondering, just*). Note who uses more (boss vs. employee, friends vs. strangers). Patterns will appear quickly.

### 10.7 Shadowing with intonation
Shadow short exchanges, copying **tone**, not just words. Record yourself saying the same sentence in three versions: abrupt, neutral, warm. Compare.

### 10.8 Spaced retrieval
Review on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30:
- *Front:* "I think she isn't coming" → *I don't think she's coming.*
- *Front:* "Probably he will come" → *He'll probably come.*
- *Front:* "Make this request polite: Send me the file." → *Could you send me the file, please?*
- *Front:* "Quite good" in the UK → *fairly good*; in the US → *very good*.

### 10.9 The "one hedge" rule
In writing and speeches, check each sentence: if there are two or more hedges, remove one.

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1: Request = modal + please/just + reason.** *Could you just send me the file? I need it for the meeting.*

> **Pattern 2: Indirect request = past/progressive + embedded clause.** *I was wondering if you could… / I was hoping you might… / I wanted to ask whether…*

> **Pattern 3: Negative raising.** *I don't think/suppose/believe/expect + clause.* ✅ *I don't think it's ready.*

> **Pattern 4: Opinion = hedge + claim.** *I think / I feel like / It seems to me / In my view + claim.*

> **Pattern 5: Disagreement = acknowledge + soften + view + bridge.** *I see your point, but I'm not sure… Could we…?*

> **Pattern 6: Downtoner + adjective.** *a bit/slightly/somewhat/fairly/kind of + adjective.* ✅ *a bit late*, ❌ *very kind of late*.

> **Pattern 7: Approximator + number.** *about/around/roughly/or so/-ish + number.* *Around ten people. Ten or so. Tenish.*

> **Pattern 8: Attribution shield = source + claim.** *Apparently / According to X / From what I hear + claim.*

> **Pattern 9: Bad news = buffer + fact + reason + next step.** *I'm afraid we're running behind. The supplier was late. We'll confirm the new date tomorrow.*

> **Pattern 10: Tag question to invite agreement.** *positive statement + negative tag; negative statement + positive tag.* *It's a bit cold, isn't it? / You don't mind, do you?*

> **Pattern 11: Vague closer = list + vague tag.** *We bought pasta, rice, and stuff. We could watch a movie or something.*

> **Pattern 12: Echo + soften.** *Not sure about that.* *Hmm, I'm not sure about that.* → softened by a hesitation marker.

> **Connections:** Lesson 53 (downtoners and intensifiers), Lesson 55 (politeness axis), Lesson 56 (academic hedging, same tools, more formal), Lesson 57 (workplace frames), Lesson 58 (discourse idioms), **Lesson 60 (next):** fillers and discourse markers (*you know, I mean, actually, like*), which are close cousins of hedges.

## 12. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1 — Basic**
- *I think so.*
- *Maybe tomorrow.*
- *It's a bit cold.*
- *Could you help me, please?*
- *I'm not sure.*

**Level 2 — Everyday**
- *I don't think he's coming.*
- *It's kind of expensive, but it's good.*
- *Could I have the bill, please?*
- *We'll probably be there around eight.*
- *I'm afraid I can't make it tonight.*

**Level 3 — Intermediate**
- *I was wondering if you could give me a hand with this.*
- *To be honest, I wasn't that impressed.*
- *It's a bit of a long story, but basically we lost the keys.*
- *I'm not sure that's quite what he meant.*
- *Apparently the flight is delayed by an hour or so.*

**Level 4 — Advanced**
- *I hate to be a pain, but would you mind moving your bag so I can sit down?*
- *I might be missing something here, but isn't that the opposite of what we agreed?*
- *I see where you're coming from; I just wonder if it's realistic given the timeline.*
- *It's not that I disagree, it's more that I'm not sure we have enough data yet.*
- *Don't get me wrong, I like the idea; I just think we need to test it first.*

**Level 5 — Native-like**
- "I don't suppose you'd happen to know whether the shop's still open, would you?"
- "Honestly, I'm kind of torn. On the one hand it's a great opportunity, but on the other, I'm not sure I'm ready."
- "I mean, it's not terrible, but it's not exactly what I had in mind either."
- "That's sort of what I was getting at, yeah, though maybe not in so many words."
- "Look, I could be wrong, but I get the feeling they're not as interested as they seem."

### 12.1 Mini-dialogues

**1. Ordering (café)**
> **A:** Hi! What can I get you?
> **B:** Hi. Could I have a medium latte, please?
> **A:** Sure. For here or to go?
> **B:** To go, please. And I was wondering, do you have anything gluten-free?

**2. Declining an invitation**
> **A:** Do you want to come to the party on Saturday?
> **B:** Oh, thanks for asking! I'd love to, but I'm afraid I've already got plans. Maybe next time?
> **A:** Sure, no worries!

**3. Polite disagreement at work**
> **A:** I think we should launch next week.
> **B:** I see your point. I'm just a bit concerned about the testing. We haven't finished the final checks. What if we launch the following Monday?
> **A:** Hmm, that could work.

**4. Giving feedback**
> **A:** What do you think of my draft?
> **B:** It's got some really good ideas. To be honest, I felt the introduction was a little long. Maybe you could cut it down a bit?

**5. Asking for repetition**
> **A:** …and the meeting is at 10:30 in room 4B.
> **B:** Sorry, I didn't quite catch that. Could you say it again?

**6. Uncertain information**
> **A:** Is the museum open on Mondays?
> **B:** I think it's closed, but I'm not 100% sure. Let me check.

**7. Bluntness in the wrong place (and fix)**
> ❌ **Customer:** I want to speak with the manager. This is wrong.
> ✅ **Customer:** Excuse me, I think there might be a mistake on the bill. Could I speak to the manager, please?

### 12.2 Email examples
> **Request (soft):** *Hi Sam, I hope you're well. I was wondering if you could take a quick look at the attached report when you have a moment. No rush, but it would be great to have your thoughts by Thursday. Thanks so much!*

> **Disagreement (professional):** *Thanks for the proposal. I see the logic, but I'm a bit worried about the cost. Would it be possible to explore a lower-budget option?*

> **Decline:** *Thank you for the invitation. Unfortunately, I won't be able to attend, but I hope it goes really well.*

### 12.3 Before and after
> ❌ *"I want that you send me the information today. You are late. I don't understand why it is not ready."*
> ✅ *"Could you send me the information today, please? I was expecting it earlier, so I wanted to check if there's a problem."*

> ❌ *"I think that no. That is not possible, it is wrong."*
> ✅ *"I don't think so. I'm afraid that's not quite right."*

> ❌ *"Maybe I'll possibly kind of try to do it, I guess, if I have time, sorry."* (over-hedged)
> ✅ *"I'll try to do it by Friday."*

## 13. Collocations & Related Expressions

**Hedging verbs and phrases**
- *seem / appear / look / sound + adjective:* *It seems fine. It looks complicated. It sounds good.*
- *tend to, be likely to, be inclined to, be bound to* (strong), *happen to* (*if you happen to see her…*)
- *as far as I know / as far as I can tell / as far as I'm concerned / as far as I remember*
- *from what I can see, from what I've heard, from where I'm standing*
- *if I'm not mistaken, if I remember correctly, if I understand correctly, correct me if I'm wrong*
- *to a certain extent / to some degree / in a way / in some ways / up to a point*
- *more often than not, by and large, on the whole, for the most part, generally speaking*
- *it's a bit of a…* (*it's a bit of a mess / a long story / a stretch*)
- *a bit on the + adjective side* (*a bit on the expensive side*)
- *not exactly / not quite / not really / not entirely / not necessarily / not that much*

**Softeners in requests**
- *Do you think you could…? Is there any chance you could…? Would it be OK if…? Would you mind if I…? Is it all right if…? Would it be possible to…? I don't suppose you could…?*
- *I hate to bother you, but… / Sorry to trouble you, but… / Excuse me for asking, but…*

**Softeners in disagreement**
- *I'm not sure about that. / I'm not convinced. / I'm not entirely sure I agree. / I see it a bit differently. / That's one way of looking at it. / I take your point, but… / Fair enough, but… / I wouldn't put it that way. / Isn't it more a question of…?*

**Softeners in bad news**
- *I'm afraid… / Unfortunately… / It looks like… / It seems we… / I'm sorry to say… / I wish I had better news.*

**Vague language**
- *and stuff, and things, or something, or whatever, and so on, that sort of thing, a bunch of, a couple of, loads of, a fair few, a good deal of, quite a lot of, kind of a, sort of a*

**Contrast groups**
| Items | Difference |
|---|---|
| *maybe / perhaps / possibly / probably* | *probably* ≈ 80–90%, *maybe/perhaps* ≈ 50%, *possibly* ≈ 20–40% (also polite: *Could you possibly…?*) |
| *I think / I guess / I suppose / I reckon / I believe / I feel* | *think* neutral; *guess* less sure (US); *suppose* reluctant/ resigned; *reckon* UK informal; *believe* more formal/ firm; *feel* subjective |
| *a bit / a little / a tad / slightly / somewhat* | decreasing informality: *a tad, a bit, a little, slightly, somewhat* |
| *quite / pretty / fairly / rather* | UK moderate/ US strong; *pretty* US informal; *rather* UK formal |
| *to be honest / honestly / frankly / to tell you the truth* | informal / informal-strong / formal-direct / informal |
| *about / around / roughly / approximately* | spoken → formal |
| *I'm afraid / unfortunately / sadly* | polite refusal / neutral negative / emotional |
| *sort of / kind of / like / -ish* | vague approximators; *like* also filler |
