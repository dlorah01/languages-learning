# Lesson 35 — BRING / RUN / TURN
### Phase 2: High-Frequency Verb System | Lesson 7 of 8

---

## 0. Where this fits
This lesson fully resolves "bring" (previewed in Lesson 33's go/come system) and introduces two more verbs — RUN and TURN — whose literal, physical meanings are simple, but whose figurative extensions are extensive and genuinely high-value for sounding native.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation

- **BRING** = carry/cause to come toward the reference point (fully resolved from Lesson 33's directional system).
- **RUN** = beyond literal running, extends to "operate/function," "manage/be in charge of," and "extend/continue" — the connecting thread is *ongoing operation or movement through a process*.
- **TURN** = beyond literal rotation, extends to "become" (a change of state, similar to Lesson 29's "get + adjective"), and many fixed phrasal-verb-adjacent expressions about redirecting attention or changing course.

---

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2a. BRING — Full Resolution from Lesson 33
- Literal: "Bring your laptop to the meeting." (toward the reference point)
- **Fixed collocations:** bring something to someone's attention, bring back memories, bring about change (cause to happen), bring up a topic/child (raise), bring something into question

### 2b. RUN — Operate, Manage, Extend, Continue
- **Operate/function:** "The engine runs smoothly." / "This app runs on any device."
- **Manage/be in charge of:** "She runs a successful business." / "He runs the marketing department."
- **Extend/continue (of time, roads, processes):** "The road runs along the coast." / "The contract runs until December." / "The meeting ran late." (extended longer than planned)
- **Run + adjective (become, similar to "get + adjective"):** "The well ran dry." / "Supplies are running low." (a specific, somewhat fixed pattern for gradual depletion/change, narrower in scope than "get + adjective")
- **Fixed collocations:** run a risk (similar to "take a risk," slightly more formal-leaning), run late/on time, run errands, run a test, run in the family (a hereditary trait)

### 2c. TURN — Become, Redirect, Change Course
- **Literal rotation:** "Turn the key." / "Turn left at the corner."
- **Become (change of state, especially color, weather, age milestones, or gradual transformation):** "The leaves turned yellow." / "It's turning cold." / "He turned 30 last week." (note: "turn" for age is a fixed, specific usage — "he turned 30," not "he became 30" or "he got 30")
- **Fixed collocations:** turn a blind eye (deliberately ignore), turn the tables (reverse a situation), turn over a new leaf (start fresh/change behavior), turn something down (refuse — previewed here, fully covered as a phrasal verb in Phase 4), turn out (to be) — (the eventual result turns out to be something, often unexpected — previewed here, fully covered in Phase 4)

---

## 3. The Get/Turn/Run Overlap for "Become" — A Genuine Nuance Worth Flagging
Lesson 29 established "get + adjective" as the dominant, general-purpose "become" pattern. "Turn" and "run" both have narrower, more specific "become" uses:
- **"Turn"** is used specifically for color changes, weather changes, and specific fixed expressions (turn pale, turn red, turn cold, turn 30): "She turned pale when she heard the news."
- **"Run"** is used specifically for a narrow set of gradual depletion/change expressions (run dry, run low, run late): "We're running low on time."
- **"Get"** remains the general, flexible default for most other "become" situations (get tired, get angry, get better) that don't fall into "turn" or "run"'s narrower specific categories.

This is a genuine three-way overlap where the general rule (get + adjective) has specific, fixed exceptions (turn/run + certain adjectives) that must be learned individually — directly extending this entire phase's running theme about collocations being partially rule-governed and partially fixed by convention.

---

## 4. Why Native Speakers Maintain These Specific "Become" Niches
English's vocabulary evolved with certain "become" contexts crystallizing around specific verbs (turn for color/weather/age, run for depletion) likely due to historical/idiomatic entrenchment rather than a fully derivable logical system — this is precisely the kind of arbitrary-but-fixed pattern that requires direct memorization (per this entire phase's method) rather than derivation from first principles.

---

## 5. When Each Matters Most
- **Bring collocations** — everyday communication about causing things to happen, raising topics, and evoking memories.
- **Run (manage/operate)** — extremely common in professional/business contexts (running a company, running a department, a system running smoothly).
- **Turn (become, specific niches)** — common in everyday descriptive language (weather, color, age) — genuinely high-frequency despite being a narrower category than "get."

---

## 6. Frequency & Register
All three verbs and their extended meanings are used across every register. "Run a business/department" is especially common in professional contexts. "Turn" for color/weather/age is universal, everyday, casual-to-neutral vocabulary.

---

## 7. American vs. British Note
No major systematic differences in these core meanings and collocations between American and British English.

---

## 8. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

| Mistake | Why | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "She becomes 30 next week." (instead of the fixed "turn" pattern for age) | Not knowing the specific, fixed "turn + age" collocation | "She turns 30 next week." |
| "The leaves get yellow in autumn." (using the general "get" pattern where "turn" is the fixed, specific choice) | Overgeneralizing "get + adjective" into a niche that "turn" specifically occupies | "The leaves turn yellow in autumn." |
| "We are getting low on supplies." (acceptable, but "running low" is the more specific, more natural fixed collocation for depletion) | Not knowing the narrower "run + adjective" depletion pattern | "We are running low on supplies." (both are technically understandable, but "running low" is the more idiomatically precise, natural choice) |
| "He administers a company." (using a more literal, less natural translation of *administra* instead of the natural English collocation) | Direct translation from Spanish *administrar/dirigir* | "He runs a company." (the natural, high-frequency English collocation for this meaning) |

---

## 9. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ "Get, turn, and run are just three interchangeable ways to say 'become,' with no real pattern." — Each occupies specific, largely fixed niches (get = general default; turn = color/weather/age; run = gradual depletion) — not freely interchangeable, though there's some overlap flexibility in casual speech.
- ❌ "Turn always means literal rotation." — As Section 2c shows, "turn" extends significantly into "become" and several fixed idiomatic expressions (turn a blind eye, turn the tables) with no rotational meaning at all.
- ❌ "Run only refers to the physical act of running." — "Run" extends extensively into operation, management, continuation/duration, and gradual depletion — a genuinely versatile verb well beyond its literal meaning.

---

## 10. Pronunciation Note
Fixed idiomatic expressions with "turn" and "run" (turn a blind eye, run in the family, turn the tables) are best learned and produced as complete rhythmic chunks (per Lesson 3's method), with natural stress typically falling on the content noun (BLIND eye, FAMily, TAbles) rather than on the verb itself.

---

## 11. Memory Technique
**Build a three-column "Become" reference chart:** GET (general default: tired, angry, better, worse), TURN (color/weather/age: red, pale, cold, 30), RUN (depletion: low, dry, late, out of time). This directly extends the matched-pair/contrast-list method from Lessons 30-31, now applied to a three-way split instead of a two-way one.

---

## 12. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern:** "Become" in English isn't one single pattern — it's a general default (get + adjective) with specific, fixed niche exceptions (turn for color/weather/age, run for gradual depletion) that must be individually memorized as fixed collocations, layered on top of the general "get" rule you can otherwise rely on.

---

## 13. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1:**
- "Bring your ID with you." (literal, toward the reference point)
- "The car runs well." (operate/function)

**Level 2 — turn for age, a genuinely fixed, non-obvious pattern:**
- "I turn 25 next month." (NOT "become 25" or "get 25" — this specific collocation is fixed)

**Level 3 — run for gradual depletion, contrasted with general "get":**
- "We're running out of time." (fixed depletion collocation) vs. "I'm getting worried about the deadline." (general "get + adjective," no depletion-specific collocation needed here)

**Level 4 — combining bring, run, and turn correctly in one natural, sophisticated passage:**
- "The news brought back memories of when she used to run the family business, back before things turned difficult and they started running low on funding." (bring back = evoke, run = manage, turned difficult = became, running low = depleting)

---

## 14. Related Expressions / Terms
- **Bring/Run/Turn as phrasal verb bases** — many collocations here (bring up, turn down, run out) will resurface as full phrasal verbs with particles in Phase 4.
- **Fixed "become" niches** — this lesson's central practical takeaway, extending Lesson 29's general "get + adjective" pattern with specific exceptions.
