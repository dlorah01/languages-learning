# Lesson 69 — Decoding Fast Native Speech
### Phase 8: Listening & Speaking Fluency | Module 1 of 3

---

## 0. Where this fits
Phase 7 taught you how English speech is **produced**: sounds, stress, rhythm, glue, melody. Phase 8 puts all of it to work **in real time**. This lesson turns your pronunciation knowledge into a **listening system**.

The complaint behind this lesson is one of the most common among advanced learners:

> *"I know the words. I can read this easily. But when a native speaker says it at normal speed, I only catch a few words and then I'm lost."*

That gap is not a vocabulary problem and it is not (mostly) a speed problem. It is a **decoding** problem, and it is trainable.

Connections:
- **Lesson 65:** stressed words are your anchors.
- **Lesson 66:** the beat tells you where the information is.
- **Lesson 67:** the reductions you now know are exactly what hides words.
- **Lesson 68:** the tune tells you question vs. statement, finished vs. continuing, serious vs. joking.
- **Lessons 59–63:** hedges, fillers, backchannels, and turn-taking phrases are the "invisible" words in real conversation.
- **Lesson 70 (next):** real-time speaking strategies (what to do when *you* must answer).

> **Core idea:** Native listeners do not hear every word. They **predict** the sentence, **catch the stressed anchors**, and **confirm** with context. Your job is to build the same system.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
Imagine a **blurry photo** of a street. You can't read the shop signs, but you recognize it's a street, there are cars, there's a bakery on the corner, and so you can guess what the blurry sign says. You use **what you see (bottom-up)** and **what you expect (top-down)** together.

Listening works the same way. Fast, reduced speech is a blurry photo. Native listeners do not "zoom in" on every word; they combine:
- **Bottom-up:** the sounds that arrive (stressed syllables, rhythm, tune).
- **Top-down:** what makes sense here (topic, situation, grammar, common phrases).

Most advanced Spanish-speaking learners over-rely on bottom-up: they try to catch **every word in order** and translate it. When one word is missed, they stop to think, and by then the next ten words have passed. The system collapses.

A second image: **a river and a net**. You can't stop the river (the speaker). You need a net that catches the **big fish** (stressed content words) and lets the plankton (reduced function words) go through, trusting that you can reconstruct what the plankton was.

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 Why fast speech is hard: five separate problems
When you "don't understand," the cause is usually one (or more) of these. Learning to **diagnose** which one saves months.

| # | Problem | What it feels like | Typical cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Segmentation** | "I can't tell where words begin and end." | linking, reductions, assimilation (Lesson 67) | dictation, respelling, chunks |
| 2 | **Recognition** | "I hear it, but I don't know the word." | unfamiliar vocabulary, or a known word in an unexpected sound form | vocabulary + hearing the word in speech |
| 3 | **Parsing** | "I know every word, but the sentence makes no sense." | long or complex grammar, fronted clauses, interruptions | grammar chunks, listening to whole sentences |
| 4 | **Memory** | "By the end of the sentence I've forgotten the beginning." | translating in your head, overloaded working memory | stop translating, chunk, take notes |
| 5 | **Context** | "I understand the sentences, but not the point." | missing cultural, topic, or genre knowledge | pre-listening preparation, background reading |

Most learners treat all five as "my listening is bad." Diagnose first.

### 2.2 Bottom-up and top-down processing
- **Bottom-up:** sound → syllables → words → phrases → meaning.
- **Top-down:** meaning/expectation → predicted words → check against sound.

Research in psycholinguistics repeatedly shows that listeners use prediction heavily: they anticipate likely next words from context, and this is what lets native listeners handle noise, speed, and accents. Predictions can be wrong, and good listeners revise quickly.

**Example:** "I'm sorry I'm late, my train was ______."
Before the last word arrives, a native listener expects *delayed*, *cancelled*, *late*, *stuck*. Even if the final word is partly masked by noise, the guess is usually right.

### 2.3 What hides words in fast speech (a map from Lessons 64–67)
| Mechanism | Example | What the ear gets |
|---|---|---|
| Weak forms | *a cup **of** tea* | "a-cup-a-tea" |
| Linking | *turn it off* | "tur-ni-toff" |
| Coalescence | *did you* | "didja" |
| Elision | *next day* | "nexday" |
| Flapping (US) | *get up* | "geh-dup" |
| Glottal stops (UK/US) | *button* | "bu'n" |
| Contractions | *I would have* | "I'd've" |
| Fused reductions | *want to / got to* | "wanna / gotta" |
| Squeezed syllables | *probably, interesting* | "prob'ly, int'resting" |
| Dropped /h/ | *tell him* | "tell 'im" |
| Fillers | *you know, I mean, like* | invisible "noise" words |
| Overlaps and false starts | *I think— I mean, I thought...* | broken sentences |

### 2.4 Anchors: the words that carry the message
Content words (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs, negatives, question words, numbers) are **stressed and clear**. Function words (articles, prepositions, auxiliaries, pronouns, conjunctions) are **weak and fast**.

> "I'**d** **LIKE** to **BOOK** a **TABLE** for **TWO** at **SEVEN**."
> Anchors: *LIKE, BOOK, TABLE, TWO, SEVEN.*

If you catch just the anchors, you can reconstruct the message: **like / book / table / two / seven**. This is the "telegram" idea from Lessons 65–66, now used for listening.

**Warning anchors (don't miss them):**
- **Negatives:** *not, never, no, hardly, barely, n't* (a missed *n't* reverses the meaning).
- **Numbers:** *fifteen / fifty* (stress position differs), *thirteen / thirty*.
- **Modals:** *can / can't*, *should / shouldn't*, *must / mustn't*.
- **Question words and tags.**
- **Contrast words:** *but, however, although, actually*.

### 2.5 Speed: what is "fast"?
- Rates vary widely by speaker, genre, and emotion. Careful speech such as news reading or presentations is often around 140–170 words per minute; casual conversation, interviews, and comedy often sit higher, with bursts that are much faster. These are rough ranges, not rules.
- English speakers can seem faster than they are because function words are compressed (Lesson 66) and pauses are short.
- Speed also depends on **predictability**: familiar phrases (*How are you doing?*) are rushed; new information is slower.
- Practical implication: don't aim to "speed yourself up." Aim to **recognize the compressed forms** so speed stops mattering.

### 2.6 Listening is not translation
When you translate each English word into Spanish, you add a delay (and a memory load). Native-like listening goes **directly from sound to meaning**. Symptoms of translating while listening:
- You understand slow speech but collapse at normal speed.
- You catch words but can't summarize.
- You are tired after 5 minutes.

Remedies: shorter chunks, focus on meaning ("What is the speaker doing: asking, complaining, suggesting?"), and **practice with material that is slightly easier** than you think you need, many times.

### 2.7 Working memory and chunking
Working memory holds only a small amount of material at a time. Listeners overcome this by **chunking**: grouping words into meaningful units (*in the middle of*, *as a matter of fact*, *would you mind if*). The more chunks you know as chunks, the less you must hold.

**This is why vocabulary lessons built around collocations and phrasal verbs (Phases 3–5) help listening:** a known chunk takes one slot in memory instead of five.

### 2.8 Prediction tools
| Tool | How it helps | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Situation** | restaurant, interview, airport have predictable language | *Are you ready to order?* |
| **Collocation** | after *heavy*, expect *rain, traffic, smoker* | *We had heavy ___* |
| **Grammar** | after *Would you ___*, expect a base verb | *Would you mind ___ing?* |
| **Discourse markers** | *however* signals contrast, *so* signals a result | *The plan looks good. However,...* |
| **Tune** | rise = question, fall = finished, fall-rise = "but" coming | Lesson 68 |
| **Repetition** | speakers re-state key ideas | *The key issue — the real problem — is...* |

### 2.9 Accents and varieties
You will hear English from many places. Some general points:
- **Vowels** change most between accents; **consonants** and **stress** are more stable.
- **Rhotic vs. non-rhotic:** US/Canadian/Scottish/Irish are mostly rhotic; most of England, Australia, New Zealand, and South Africa are non-rhotic.
- **T-sounds:** US flap (*water*), UK glottal stop (*bottle*).
- **Rhythm:** several varieties (many Indian, Caribbean, and African Englishes, among others) are more syllable-timed, which can feel "clearer" to you but also has different stress patterns.
- **Rate:** speakers vary a lot within each variety.
- **Strategy:** gradual exposure; listen to the same speaker over time; look for **pattern correspondences** (e.g., this speaker says /ɪ/ in *bit* more like /i/).
- **Do not assume** that "difficult" accents are "wrong" or that one is the only target. Your goal is **flexibility**.

### 2.10 Genres and registers: different listening
| Genre | What's hard | Helpful trick |
|---|---|---|
| **News** | density, vocabulary | headlines first, topic prediction |
| **Podcast conversation** | overlap, fillers, humor | listen for topic changes |
| **Lectures** | long sentences, jargon | note keywords, signposts (*first, however, in conclusion*) |
| **Films/series** | slang, background noise, emotion | use subtitles strategically (see 9.4) |
| **Phone calls** | no visual cues, poor audio | ask for repetition, confirm numbers |
| **Meetings** | multiple speakers, interruptions | identify speakers, notes, summary |
| **Interviews** | prepared vs. spontaneous | recognize rehearsed phrases |
| **Customer service** | scripts + variation | expect standard phrases |
| **Comedy** | wordplay, culture | accept partial understanding |

### 2.11 Visual and contextual cues
Face, lips, gestures, slides, background, and tone of voice all provide information. Video listening is generally easier than audio-only. When practicing, include **both** (to build comprehension) and **audio-only** (to match phone calls and podcasts).

### 2.12 Recovery: what to do when you lose the thread
1. **Don't stop.** One missed word costs you one word; stopping costs you the next ten.
2. **Keep the anchors.** Notice what you caught.
3. **Wait for a repeat or rephrase.** Speakers often restate (*In other words,... What I mean is...*).
4. **Use the gap:** *Something about a deadline... maybe next week...* keep a provisional meaning.
5. **Revise** when more comes.
6. **Ask** when it matters (Section 4).

This "keep going" skill is the main difference between fluent and non-fluent listeners.

## 3. Why Native Speakers Do This (How Natives Listen)
1. **Prediction is automatic.** Natives anticipate words because they know common phrases, grammar, and topics.
2. **Tolerance for missing pieces.** They routinely miss words and don't notice, because context fills the gap.
3. **Focus on meaning, not form.** They remember ideas, not exact wording.
4. **Chunk-based storage.** They have thousands of stored phrases that need little processing.
5. **Social use of attention.** They listen to tone and body language for attitude.
6. **Repair is normal.** Asking *Sorry, what was that?* is routine and not embarrassing.
7. **Exposure.** Years of exposure to accents and genres built flexible expectations.

## 4. When to Use / When Not To

**Use full active decoding (anchors, prediction, chunking) when**
- listening to conversation, meetings, lectures, podcasts, series;
- the speaker is fast or the audio imperfect;
- you must follow the gist and respond.

**Slow down and ask for repetition when**
- the information is critical (numbers, names, addresses, instructions, medical, legal, financial);
- you're unsure about a negative or a modal (*can / can't*);
- the call is poor;
- you will act on what you heard.

**Don't**
- pretend to understand important information;
- repeat "Sorry?" five times in a row (vary your repair: see 14);
- stop to analyze one word while the speaker moves on;
- over-rely on subtitles (see 9.4);
- translate word by word.

**Useful repair phrases (listener side)**
| Situation | Phrase |
|---|---|
| Didn't hear | *Sorry, could you say that again?* |
| Partly heard | *Sorry, you said the meeting is at... ?* |
| Too fast | *Could you slow down a little?* |
| Unknown word | *What does ___ mean?* / *Sorry, what do you mean by ___?* |
| Check a number | *Was that fifteen, one-five, or fifty, five-zero?* |
| Confirm meaning | *So what you're saying is...?* |
| Check spelling | *How do you spell that?* |
| Poor line | *Sorry, the line is bad. Could you repeat that?* |
| General | *Sorry, I missed the last part.* |

## 5. Frequency & Priority

**What gives the biggest listening gains**
1. **Hearing the high-frequency function words and reductions** (the 100 most common spoken chunks).
2. **Following stressed anchors** and ignoring the rest at first.
3. **Prediction** from context, collocations, and grammar.
4. **Staying with the speaker** after a miss.
5. **Volume:** lots of input, at the right level, regularly.
6. **Exposure to different accents** (after the base is strong).
7. **Vocabulary** in the topics you need.

**Time and level** (rough guide)
| Goal | Daily plan |
|---|---|
| Maintain | 20–30 min of listening in any form |
| Improve | 30–45 min, including 10 min of intensive work |
| Fast progress | 60+ min, mixing extensive and intensive, with a weekly review |

**Register of materials**
| Level of difficulty | Examples |
|---|---|
| Easier | learner podcasts, graded interviews, news slow reports, vlogs with clear speech |
| Medium | news broadcasts, TED-style talks, interview podcasts, documentaries |
| Hard | casual multi-speaker conversation, sitcoms, comedy, regional accents, phone calls, meetings |

## 6. American vs. British Notes
| Feature | US | UK |
|---|---|---|
| Typical features that hide words | flapping (*butter*), reduced *t* in *-nt-* (*winter*) | glottal stops (*better*), dropped /h/, *innit*, intrusive /r/ |
| *can / can't* | *can't* has a full vowel /kænt/; *can* is weak /kən/ | *can't* /kɑːnt/ with a long vowel; *can* is weak /kən/ |
| Common casual forms | *gonna, wanna, whaddaya, gotcha, y'all* | *gonna, wanna, innit, cuppa, wotcha, d'you* |
| Intonation | narrower; uptalk in some groups | wider; more fall-rise |
| Words (see Lesson 77) | *apartment, truck, fall* | *flat, lorry, autumn* |
| Media you'll meet | many TV series, podcasts, YouTube | BBC, comedy panel shows, regional dramas |

**Practical advice:** pick **one** variety as your main training set at first (it reduces variation), and then add the other gradually. Keep a note of the differences that surprise you.

**Other varieties** (Irish, Scottish, Australian, Indian, South African, Caribbean, Nigerian, etc.): you'll meet them in international work. Expose yourself gradually; they all follow regular patterns, and a few hours with one speaker makes that speaker much easier.

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

### 7.1 Listening habits
| ❌ Habit | Result | ✅ Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Translating each word | falls behind, tires quickly | listen for meaning units; practice shadowing |
| Trying to catch every word | panic after one miss | catch anchors; accept gaps |
| Stopping to figure out a word | loses the next 10 seconds | note it, move on, return later |
| Expecting full forms (*going to*, *want to*) | doesn't recognize *gonna, wanna* | learn reductions as chunks |
| Expecting every syllable (syllable-timed ear) | misses squeezed words | train with beat grids (Lesson 66) |
| Over-trusting cognates | mishears false friends (*actually*, *eventually*, *sensible*) | check context |
| Ignoring unstressed *not/n't* | reverses meaning | listen for the negative in context and tune |
| Mixing up *can / can't* | confusion | listen to vowel, context, following sentence |
| Missing final *-s, -ed, -ing* | tense/number errors in understanding | practice with dictation |
| Confusing numbers (*thirteen/thirty*, *fifteen/fifty*) | wrong information | listen to stress position; confirm |
| Using only subtitles in Spanish | no ear training | switch to English subtitles, then none |
| Using only slow or graded material | surprise at real speech | add real speech gradually |
| Listening only passively in the background | little learning | do some intensive work |
| Guessing wildly and then not checking | false understanding | guess, then verify |
| Fearing to ask for repetition | miscommunication | learn polite repair phrases |

### 7.2 Specific sound traps for Spanish speakers (hearing)
| Pair | Trap | Clue |
|---|---|---|
| *ship / sheep* | /ɪ/ vs /iː/ | length and tension; context |
| *bat / but / bought* | /æ/ vs /ʌ/ vs /ɔ/ | context; vowel quality |
| *thirteen / thirty* | stress: thir-**TEEN** vs **THIR**-ty | stress position |
| *fifteen / fifty* | stress: fif-**TEEN** vs **FIF**-ty | stress position |
| *can / can't* | weak vs full vowel | following words; tune |
| *a / the* | /ə/ vs /ðə/ | the sound of /ð/ |
| *he's / she's* | /h/ vs /ʃ/ | context |
| *want / won't* | /wɑnt/ vs /woʊnt/ | vowel |
| *live (verb) / leave* | /ɪ/ vs /iː/ | context |
| *walk / work* | /ɔ/ vs /ɜr/ | final consonant |

### 7.3 Attitude traps
- **"I need to understand 100%."** Native speakers rarely do. 80–90% with good context is normal.
- **"If I just learn more vocabulary, my listening will improve."** Vocabulary helps, but hearing the words in real speech is a separate skill.
- **"Subtitles help me learn."** They help comprehension; they can also become a crutch that prevents ear training.
- **"Native speakers talk too fast."** Often they talk at a normal speed with heavy reductions.
- **"I'll practice listening later; first I'll finish grammar."** Listening is learned by listening.

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ **"Listening is a passive skill."** — It's an active process of predicting and checking.
- ❌ **"If I understand slow English, I'll understand fast English."** — Fast English includes reductions that slow English hides.
- ❌ **"Native speakers hear every word."** — They fill many gaps from context.
- ❌ **"My problem is vocabulary."** — Often you know the word; you can't recognize it in its spoken form.
- ❌ **"Accents are the main problem."** — Reductions and speed in your target accent matter more at first.
- ❌ **"I should avoid slang and informal speech."** — It is much of what you'll hear.
- ❌ **"Transcripts ruin listening practice."** — They are a tool when used after you try listening without them.
- ❌ **"More hours of background listening is enough."** — Some intensive, focused work is needed.
- ❌ **"It's embarrassing to ask for repetition."** — It's normal and respected.
- ❌ **"A clear speaker is always easier."** — Clear but unfamiliar content or accent can be harder than a casual speaker on a topic you know.
- ❌ **"I'll understand once I live in an English-speaking country."** — Exposure helps greatly, but purposeful practice speeds it.
- ❌ **"Listening ability is a talent."** — It's a trainable set of habits.

## 9. Pronunciation Note: Training Method (for listening)

### 9.1 The four-pass method (15–20 minutes, one 1–2 minute clip)
1. **Pass 1 — Gist.** Listen once without stopping. Write 3 sentences: who, where, what's happening.
2. **Pass 2 — Anchors.** Listen again and write the key words you catch (telegram).
3. **Pass 3 — Dictation.** Listen in short segments (5–10 seconds) and write exactly what you hear. Replay as needed.
4. **Pass 4 — Check and respell.** Compare with the transcript. Mark every gap. For each, write the **sound** (respelling) and the **reason** (linking, weak form, coalescence...).
Then listen once more **without** the transcript and notice how much more you hear.

### 9.2 Partial dictation (gap-fill)
Take a transcript, blank out 10 function words, and listen. This trains you to hear *to, of, the, a, and, can, have* in context.

### 9.3 Shadowing
Repeat after a speaker with a delay of about half a second (Lesson 66). Shadowing trains your ear and your mouth together.

### 9.4 Subtitle ladder
1. Spanish subtitles (comprehension only; short use).
2. English subtitles, while listening.
3. English subtitles, only for checking after listening.
4. No subtitles.
Move up when you understand about 80% at the current step. Always do at least one "no subtitle" session per week.

### 9.5 Speed ladder
Use the playback speed option: 0.75x → 1x → 1.25x on a clip you know. Training at 1.25x makes 1x feel easier. Don't use slowed speech as your only input.

### 9.6 Extensive vs. intensive listening
- **Extensive:** lots of easy-to-medium material for gist and enjoyment (podcasts on your commute).
- **Intensive:** short clips, repeated, analyzed (the four-pass method).
A good week has both: roughly 80% extensive, 20% intensive.

### 9.7 Predict-then-check
Pause a video or audio mid-sentence and guess the next word or phrase. Then press play. This trains the top-down side.

### 9.8 Listening log
| Date | Source | Level | What I understood | What I missed | Why (sound / word / grammar / memory / context) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
Fill in after each intensive session.

### 9.9 Error bank
Keep a list of phrases you misheard with respelling and the correct form, for spaced review (days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30).

### 9.10 Trouble spots
- Stopping the audio too often.
- Practicing only with subtitles.
- Choosing material that is too hard (frustration) or too easy (no growth).
- Not reviewing the clips.
- Giving up after one hard accent.

## 10. Memory Technique

### 10.1 The blurry-photo rule
"Don't zoom; guess the picture." Look for the shape of the sentence before the detail of every word.

### 10.2 Net and river
Catch the big fish (anchors), let the plankton go (function words). Reconstruct later.

### 10.3 The 3-second rule
If you miss a word, give yourself 3 seconds to let it go. Then rejoin the speaker.

### 10.4 The "What is the speaker doing?" question
Ask: Are they *asking, complaining, suggesting, refusing, joking, explaining, warning*? Function first, words second.

### 10.5 Chunk bank
Keep a list of 50 high-frequency spoken chunks (*I was going to..., You know what I mean?, It depends on..., How come...?, Do you mind if...?*) and review them as sounds, not just as text.

### 10.6 Respelling notebook
Write the **sound** of what you missed: *whatcha, jeet, coulda, innit, dunno, kinda*. Your eyes will teach your ears.

### 10.7 Spaced retrieval cards
- *Five causes of listening failure?* → **segmentation, recognition, parsing, memory, context**
- *What do you catch first in fast speech?* → **stressed content words (anchors)**
- *What to do after missing a word?* → **keep going; don't stop**
- *Words you must not miss?* → **negatives, numbers, modals, question words**
- *thirteen vs thirty?* → **thir-TEEN vs THIR-ty (stress)**
- *Best subtitle strategy?* → **move from Spanish → English → check only → none**
- *Extensive vs intensive ratio?* → **about 80/20**
- *Best way to train prediction?* → **pause and guess the next word**
Review on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30.

### 10.8 Weekly review
Every Sunday: re-listen to the clip you did on Monday without the transcript. Notice the improvement. This is motivating and shows what to review.

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1: Anchors first.** Stressed content words carry the message.

> **Pattern 2: Function words are fast and weak; assume they are there.**

> **Pattern 3: Predict, then check.**

> **Pattern 4: One miss ≠ lost; keep going.**

> **Pattern 5: Negatives, numbers, modals, and question words need special attention.**

> **Pattern 6: Known chunks are cheap in memory; unknown chunks are expensive.**

> **Pattern 7: The tune tells you the speech act** (question, statement, "but...").

> **Pattern 8: Reduction is regular (Lesson 67); learn the patterns, not each case.**

> **Pattern 9: Context is a second ear.**

> **Pattern 10: Intensive practice fixes specific gaps; extensive practice builds fluency.**

> **Pattern 11: Match difficulty: slightly above comfortable.**

> **Pattern 12: Repair requests are a skill, not a weakness.**

> **Connections:** Lessons 64–68 (the whole Phase 7 system), Lessons 59–63 (spoken discourse), Phase 3–5 (collocations and chunks), Lesson 70 (speaking in real time), Lesson 71 (sounding natural).

## 12. Real-Life Examples

### 12.1 From what you hear to what was said
| What reaches your ear | What was said | Processes |
|---|---|---|
| "Whaddaya wanna do tonight?" | What do you want to do tonight? | linking, weak forms, reductions |
| "Jeet yet?" | Did you eat yet? | coalescence, elision |
| "I dunno, maybe." | I don't know, maybe. | reduction |
| "Cuppa tea?" | Would you like a cup of tea? | UK reduction |
| "Couldja send me the file?" | Could you send me the file? | coalescence |
| "I'd've called, but I lost my phone." | I would have called, but I lost my phone. | double contraction |
| "S'pose so." | I suppose so. | aphesis (lost first syllable) |
| "Dya know what I mean?" | Do you know what I mean? | fusion |
| "Hafta go." | I have to go. | reduction |
| "Lotta people." | A lot of people. | reduction |
| "Wassup?" | What's up? | fusion |
| "Prob'ly gonna rain." | It's probably going to rain. | squeeze + reduction |

### 12.2 Anchor reconstruction
| Heard | Anchors you caught | Reconstruction |
|---|---|---|
| "I was thinking we could maybe grab lunch around one." | thinking / grab / lunch / one | a lunch plan at one o'clock |
| "I'm afraid the manager's not available till Thursday." | afraid / manager / not / Thursday | the manager is not available until Thursday |
| "Don't forget to submit the form by Friday." | don't forget / submit / form / Friday | a reminder with a deadline |
| "It wasn't that bad, actually." | wasn't / bad / actually | it was better than expected |

### 12.3 Prediction practice
Predict the likely next word:
- "Could you tell me how to get to the ______?" (station / airport / museum)
- "It's not as expensive as I ______." (thought / expected)
- "I'm sorry to ______ you, but..." (bother / interrupt / trouble)
- "To be honest, I'm not ______ sure." (really / entirely / totally)
- "We need to talk about the ______." (budget / schedule / problem)

### 12.4 Mini-dialogue: what you might catch
> **A:** "Hey, so, I was gonna ask ya, are ya free Saturday? We're thinkin' of havin' a few people over."
> **Anchors:** *gonna ask / free Saturday / thinking / people over.*
> **Meaning:** an invitation for Saturday.
> **B:** "Oh, um, I think so? Lemme check. What time were you thinkin'?"
> **Anchors:** *think so / check / what time.*
> **Meaning:** probably yes; asks for the time.

### 12.5 Number and negative traps
> "It costs **thirteen** dollars." vs. "It costs **thirty** dollars."
> "I **can** come." vs. "I **can't** come." (check the tune and context.)
> "It's **not** ready." — the *not* is easy to miss in fast speech; the following words help.
> "It's **fifteen** dollars." vs. "**fifty** dollars." (stress on -TEEN vs FIF-)

### 12.6 Recovery in action
> **Speaker:** "...so the deadline's been pushed to the, uh, the twenty-something of next month because of the, you know, the audit."
> **You:** (miss "twenty-something") — you keep going: *deadline moved to next month because of an audit.*
> **You (later):** "Sorry, which date did you say for the deadline?" ✅

## 13. Collocations & Related Expressions

**Vocabulary**
- *catch (a word), pick up (a word / on a detail), make out (what someone said), follow (a conversation / a lecture), keep up with (the speaker), lose track of (the conversation), get the gist, get the main idea, miss the point*
- *decode, tune in, tune out, zone out, listen in, eavesdrop, overhear*
- *fast / slow / clear / mumbled / muffled / garbled / slurred speech*

## 17. Speaking Practice
(Speaking practice supports listening: what you can say, you can hear.)

**Drill 1 — Say it the way it sounds.** Read the respelled phrases in 12.1 aloud until they feel natural.

**Drill 2 — Shadow a clip.** Shadow 60 seconds of a podcast. Repeat once with the transcript, once without.

**Drill 3 — Anchor retell.** Listen to a 1-minute clip and retell it in 4–5 sentences using only the anchors you remember.

**Drill 4 — Repair role-play.** With a partner, practice asking for repetition in five different ways (variety matters).

**Drill 5 — Number dictation.** Have a partner say 10 numbers (including *13/30*, *15/50*, phone numbers). Write them and check.

**Drill 6 — Predict game.** A partner reads half-sentences; you complete them.

**Drill 7 — Summarize.** After listening to a 2-minute segment, summarize in 30 seconds.

**Drill 8 — Fast and slow.** Say a sentence twice: slowly and carefully, then fast with reductions. Listen to your recording of both.

**Mini Quiz**
