# Lesson 46 — Phrasal Verbs with PUT
### Phase 4: Phrasal Verbs | Module 3 of 7

---

## 0. Where this fits
Lesson 34 taught PUT as a core verb (put it here, put pressure on, put an end to). Lesson 44 gave you the transparency spectrum, and Lesson 45 gave you separability. This lesson adds one new skill: **three-part phrasal verbs** (*put up with*, *put down to*), where a verb carries *two* particles. These are everywhere in spoken English, and they are among the hardest for Spanish speakers because Spanish has no equivalent structure.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
PUT is the **placing** verb: you take something and set it somewhere. The particle tells you *where it ends up or what happens to it*.

- **put + on / in / down / up** → set it at a location (clothes on a body, a sign up on a wall, a baby down in a crib).
- **put + off / back / aside / away** → move it in **time or space** (postpone, return, save, store).
- **put + out / down** → **stop** something (a fire, a rebellion, a pet's suffering).
- **put + across / forward / together** → **present or assemble** (an idea, a plan, a team).
- **put + up with / down to / up to** → **tolerate, attribute, instigate** (the three-part group).

Think of it as a hand placing a piece on a board. The board can be a body, a calendar, a conversation, or a mental slot.

---

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 Six families

| Family | Core idea | Phrasal verbs |
|---|---|---|
| **Wearing / applying** | place on the body or surface | put **on**, put **on** (weight/accent/show) |
| **Time and storage** | move in time or space | put **off**, put **back**, put **aside**, put **away**, put **by** (UK) |
| **Stopping** | end something | put **out**, put **down**, put **an end to** |
| **Presenting / assembling** | make visible or build | put **forward**, put **across**, put **together**, put **up** (a sign, a tent) |
| **Lodging / paying** | give or receive support | put **up** (a guest), put **up** (money), put **in** |
| **Three-part verbs** | tolerate, attribute, provoke | put **up with**, put **down to**, put **up to** |

### 2.2 Meanings with examples

**Wearing / applying**
- **put on** — dress yourself or apply: *Put on a jacket; it's cold.* / *She put on lipstick.* / *Put on some music.*
- **put on** (weight) — gain: *I put on three kilos over the holidays.*
- **put on** (pretend): *Her accent is put on.* / *He's just putting it on.* (UK/US)
- **put on** (stage): *The school is putting on a play.*

**Time and storage**
- **put off** — postpone: *Don't put off the call any longer.* Also: discourage, repel: *The smell put me off my dinner.*
- **put back** — return to its place, or delay: *Put the book back.* / *The meeting was put back to Thursday.*
- **put aside** — save, set apart: *I put aside $200 a month.*
- **put away** — store: *Put away your toys.* (Also slang: eat a lot; or imprison.)

**Stopping**
- **put out** — extinguish: *Firefighters put out the fire.* / *He put out his cigarette.* Also: inconvenience: *I don't want to put you out.* Also: publish: *The band put out a new album.*
- **put down** — place on a surface; also suppress; also insult; also euthanize: *She put down her bag.* / *The army put down the revolt.* / *Stop putting yourself down.* / *They had to put the dog down.*

**Presenting / assembling**
- **put forward** — propose: *She put forward an idea.*
- **put across / put over** — communicate effectively: *He puts his ideas across clearly.*
- **put together** — assemble, create: *We put together a presentation.*
- **put up** — erect or display: *They put up a tent.* / *We put up posters.* Also: raise prices: *They put up the rent.* (UK; US "raised")

**Lodging / paying**
- **put up** (a person) — give a bed: *Can you put me up for the night?*
- **put in** — invest time or effort or install: *She put in a lot of hours.* / *They put in a new kitchen.* / *I'll put in a good word for you.*

**Three-part verbs**
- **put up with** — tolerate: *I can't put up with this noise.*
- **put down to** — attribute to a cause: *I put his rudeness down to stress.*
- **put up to** — instigate someone to do something (usually bad): *Who put you up to this?*

### 2.3 Separability — extended to three-part verbs

| Phrasal verb | Type | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| put on (clothes) | Separable | *put it on / put on a hat / put a hat on* |
| put off | Separable | *put it off / put off the meeting* |
| put back | Separable | *put it back* |
| put away | Separable | *put them away* |
| put out | Separable | *put it out* |
| put down | Separable | *put it down* |
| put forward | Separable | *put it forward* |
| put across | Separable | *put your point across* |
| put together | Separable | *put it together* |
| put up (erect) | Separable | *put it up* |
| put in | Separable | *put it in* |
| put up with | **Inseparable** | *put up with it* (never: *put it up with*) |
| put down to | Special | *put X down to Y* (X goes in the middle) |
| put up to | Special | *put someone up to it* |

**The three-part rule:** the object comes **after the whole unit** for *put up with*. For *put down to* and *put up to*, the structure is **put + [object] + particle + [second object]**, so the first object sits in the middle:
- ✅ *I put it down to bad luck.*
- ❌ *I put down to it bad luck.*
- ✅ *Who put you up to this?*
- ❌ *Who put up you to this?*

## 3. Why Native Speakers Use Them
Phrasal PUT verbs are the default because Latinate substitutes sound heavy:

| Phrasal (natural) | Formal alternative |
|---|---|
| put up with | tolerate, endure |
| put off | postpone, defer |
| put forward | propose, submit |
| put out (fire) | extinguish |
| put together | assemble, compile |
| put down to | attribute to |
| put across | convey, communicate |
| put on (a play) | stage, produce |

Natives choose the phrasal verb in speech, interviews, and most business emails. In legal or academic writing, the Latinate verb signals precision. Note that *tolerate* sounds oddly distant in spoken English: "I can't tolerate my neighbor" feels stiff, while "I can't put up with my neighbor" feels human.

---

## 4. When to Use / When Not To
- **Use** *put up with* when complaining or describing patience; it carries emotion.
- **Use** *put off* for postponing, but notice its second meaning (discourage), which depends on context.
- **Use** *put forward* in meetings and academic discussion for proposals. It is more formal than *suggest*.
- **Don't** use *put up with* with a positive thing. You don't "put up with" a gift. It implies something unpleasant.
- **Don't** use *put down* of a person unless you mean insult or euthanize a pet. For physical placing, it is neutral.
- **Be careful** with *put out*: it has a rude-sounding "inconvenience" use that is polite, and a different meaning (publish) that is neutral.

## 6. American vs. British Notes
- **UK:** *put up the rent / the price* = raise. **US:** usually *raise the rent*.
- **UK:** *put by* (save) is older and slightly dated: *put some money by*. **US:** prefer *set aside* or *save*.
- **UK:** *put someone's back up* = annoy someone. Rare in the US.
- **UK:** *put the kettle on* (start making tea) is iconic. **US:** *put on a pot of coffee*.
- **US:** *put in a good word* works in both; *put in for* (apply for a job or transfer) is common in US military and workplace talk.
- **Both:** *put on weight* is standard; *put on some weight* softens it.
- **Pronunciation of "put":** /pʊt/ in both, never /pʌt/ (a common error; see Section 9).

---

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

1. **Pronoun placement (same trap as Lesson 45)**
   - ❌ *Put on it.* / *Put off it until Monday.*
   - ✅ *Put it on.* / *Put it off until Monday.*

2. **Splitting *put up with***
   - ❌ *I can't put up it with.* / *I can't put it up with.*
   - ✅ *I can't put up with it.*
   - *Why:* it looks like a long separable verb, but the three parts form one inseparable unit.

3. **Using *put up with* + infinitive**
   - ❌ *I can't put up with to wait.*
   - ✅ *I can't put up with waiting.* / *I can't put up with the wait.*
   - *Why:* the final *with* is a preposition, so it takes a noun or gerund.

4. **Confusing *put off* (postpone) with *take off***
   - ❌ *The meeting was taken off to Friday.*
   - ✅ *The meeting was put off until Friday.*

5. **Spanish *poner* over-extension**
   - ❌ *Put the TV* (for "turn on the TV") → ✅ *Turn on the TV* / *Put the TV on* (UK/US both acceptable, but "turn on" is safer).
   - ❌ *She puts herself nervous.* → ✅ *She gets nervous.* (*ponerse nervioso* ≠ *put*; revisit the become-chart in Lessons 35–36)
   - ❌ *Put me a coffee, please.* → ✅ *Could I have a coffee, please?* / *Could you get me a coffee?*
   - ❌ *I'm going to put an example.* → ✅ *I'm going to give an example.*

6. **Wrong preposition in *put down to***
   - ❌ *I put it down for stress.* / *I put it down by stress.*
   - ✅ *I put it down to stress.*

7. **Mispronouncing *put* as *but***
   - ❌ /pʌt/ ("putt," as in golf)
   - ✅ /pʊt/ (short, rounded vowel like *good*, *book*).

---

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ "*Put off* means remove." — That's *take off*. *Put off* is about delaying or discouraging.
- ❌ "*Put up with* is rude." — It's neutral to informal; the rudeness depends on tone, not the verb.
- ❌ "All three-part phrasal verbs are inseparable." — *Put down to* and *put up to* take a middle object.
- ❌ "*Put* always means place." — It has dozens of non-spatial meanings: postpone, tolerate, attribute, stage, publish.
- ❌ "Phrasal verbs are the same in every English." — Many have US/UK split (*put up the price* vs. *raise the price*).

---

## 9. Pronunciation Note
1. **PUT /pʊt/** — short vowel, as in *foot*. Do not say /pʌt/ ("putt"). Minimal pair: *put* /pʊt/ vs. *putt* /pʌt/ (golf).
2. **Particle stress:** *put ON*, *put OFF*, *put UP with*. In *put up with*, stress falls on **UP**: /pʊt ˈʌp wɪð/. The *with* is weak.
3. **Linking:** *put it on* → /pʊ ˈtɪ ˈtɒn/ ("puh-ti-ton"). The /t/ of *put* links to *it*, and the /t/ of *it* links to *on*. Native speakers use a flap /ɾ/ in US English: /pʊɾɪˈɾɑn/.
4. **Noun stress shift:** *to put ON* (verb) vs. *a PUT-on* (a prank, noun, rare). *To put DOWN* vs. *a PUT-down* (an insult): first-syllable stress when it becomes a noun.
5. **Reduction in "put up with it":** often /pʊɾəˈpwɪðɪt/ in fast speech. Listen for the compressed rhythm, not separate words (Lesson 1: stress-timed).

## 10. Memory Technique

### Map the verb to a "board"
Imagine a board game. Each family is a move:

| Move | Particle | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Place a piece on the board | **on, up, in** | wear, erect, install |
| Move the piece later | **off** | postpone |
| Move it back where it was | **back** | return |
| Take it out of play | **out, down** | end / suppress |
| Slide it toward the other player | **forward, across** | propose / communicate |
| Join pieces | **together** | assemble |
| Set it aside | **aside, away** | store / save |

### The "suffer ladder" for three-part verbs
Remember the emotional arc:
1. **put up with** = tolerate (unwillingly)
2. **put down to** = explain it away
3. **put up to** = push someone into it

All three involve *dealing with something unwanted or pushing someone toward it*.

### Spaced-retrieval cards
Front: *I can't ___ ___ ___ this noise.* → **put up with**
Front: *She ___ it ___ ___ stress.* → **put it down to**
Review on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30. Always say the full sentence aloud; retrieval beats recognition.

### Contrast chart with TAKE (Lesson 45)
| Idea | TAKE | PUT |
|---|---|---|
| Clothes | take **off** (remove) | put **on** (wear) |
| Hobby | take **up** (start) | put **aside** (stop for now) |
| Responsibility | take **on** (accept) | put **off** (delay) |
| Statement | take **back** (retract) | put **forward** (propose) |

Learn them in pairs: opposites cement meaning.

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1:** **put + noun + particle** for presenting ideas: *put the idea forward, put the point across, put the case together.* Meetings and interviews run on these.

> **Pattern 2:** **put X down to Y** is the standard way to say "I think Y caused X." Equivalent: *attribute X to Y*, *blame Y for X* (more negative). Natives use it conversationally: *"I'd put it down to jet lag."*

> **Pattern 3:** **put up with + noun / -ing.** Never an infinitive. This connects to Lesson 43: after a preposition, use a noun or gerund.

> **Pattern 4:** **be put off (by/from)** is the common passive: *I was put off by the price.* / *She was put off from applying.* The agent is often a thing, not a person.

> **Pattern 5:** **put on + abstract noun** often means "pretend or produce": *put on a show, put on an act, put on a brave face, put on airs.*

> **Connection to Lesson 34:** *put pressure on, put an end to, put emphasis on* use the same placing idea with abstract objects. The phrasal verbs extend the pattern: you "place" ideas, pressure, and effort as if they were objects.

> **Connection to Lesson 44 (spectrum):** *put the book back* is transparent; *put up with* is fully idiomatic. You can't derive it from the parts, so treat it as a chunk (Lesson 3).

## 12. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1 — Transparent**
- Put your coat **on**.
- Please put the books **back**.
- He put **away** his tools.
- She put the phone **down**.

**Level 2 — Everyday**
- I keep **putting off** my dentist appointment.
- They **put up** a new fence.
- Can you **put out** the candles?
- I **put on** a few pounds this winter.

**Level 3 — Work and social**
- We **put together** a proposal in two days.
- I can't **put up with** his constant interruptions.
- Could you **put me up** for a night?
- She **put** her ideas **across** very clearly.
- Mark **put forward** a different plan.

**Level 4 — Abstract**
- I'd **put** his mood **down to** exhaustion.
- The smell of the food **put me off** completely.
- He was **put up to** it by his friends.
- The school **put on** a production of *Hamlet*.
- The government **put down** the protests with force.

**Level 5 — Native-like**
- "I've been **putting** this conversation **off** for weeks, but I owe you an honest answer."
- "Honestly, I put it down to nerves; she's usually much more confident than that."
- "I wouldn't **put it past** him" (= I think he's capable of it).
- "She **put in** long hours and finally **put** the deal **together**."
- "I don't want to **put you out**, but could I borrow your car for an hour?"

**Bonus: Fixed phrases with PUT**
- **put someone on the spot** — force a difficult answer
- **put your foot down** — be firm
- **put it past someone** — believe someone capable of something
- **put in a good word** — recommend someone
- **put two and two together** — deduce
- **put yourself in someone's shoes** — empathize
- **put your feet up** — relax
- **put something into perspective**

## 13. Collocations & Related Expressions

**Collocations**
- put **on** weight / a coat / a show / an act / the kettle / pressure
- put **off** a decision / a meeting / the inevitable / a trip
- put **up** a fight / a tent / a sign / prices / money / a guest
- put **out** a fire / a statement / a book / a hand
- put **down** a deposit / a rebellion / a phone / a pet
- put **together** a team / a report / a plan / a puzzle
- put **forward** a proposal / a candidate / a view / a theory
- put **across** a message / a point / an idea
- put **in** effort / hours / a request / a bid / a kitchen

**Contrast with similar verbs**
| Idea | Verb | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Tolerate | put up with / bear / stand / tolerate | *put up with* is conversational; *tolerate* is formal; *stand* usually negative ("I can't stand it") |
| Postpone | put off / postpone / delay / push back | *push back* is common in business; *put off* implies avoidance |
| Propose | put forward / suggest / propose | *put forward* is formal-neutral; *suggest* is lightest |
| Extinguish | put out / extinguish / snuff out | *put out* is everyday; *extinguish* is technical or formal |

## 16. Interview-Style Questions
Answer each in 3–5 sentences, using at least two PUT phrasal verbs.
1. "Tell me about something you've been putting off. Why?"
2. "Describe a time you had to put up with a difficult situation at work or school."

5. "What do you put down your success (or a failure) to?"

## 17. Speaking Practice

**Drill 1 — Linked chunks (five times each, aloud, at natural speed):**
> "Put it on." / "Put it off." / "I can't put up with it." / "I put it down to stress." / "Who put you up to this?"

**Drill 2 — Minimal pair /ʊ/ vs /ʌ/:** put–putt, full–fun, look–luck, foot–cut. Say each pair slowly, then in a sentence: "Put the putter on the table."

**Drill 3 — Opposites with TAKE.** Say the pair quickly: take off / put on, take out / put in, take up / put aside, take back / put forward.

**Drill 4 — 60-second complaint.** Record yourself complaining politely about a noisy neighbor, using *put up with, put off, put down to,* and *put someone out*. Check that you use *-ing* after *put up with*.

**Drill 5 — Role-play.**
Partner: "Why didn't you reply to my email?"
You: "Honestly, I **put it off** because I wasn't sure what to say. I put it down to stress, to be honest."

**Mini Quiz**
1. Three-part: *put up with, put down to, put up to*. *Put up with* is inseparable.
2. Because *with* is a preposition, so it needs a noun or gerund: *put up with waiting*.
3. Postpone: *put off the meeting* (object is an event or task). Discourage: *put me off* (object is a person; cause is usually a smell, price, or experience).

5. UK: *put up the rent*. US: *raise the rent* (though *put up* is understood).
6. /pʊt/, a short rounded vowel as in *foot*. Spanish speakers often say /pʌt/ ("putt").
7. *Tolerate* → *put up with*. *Postpone* → *put off*.
