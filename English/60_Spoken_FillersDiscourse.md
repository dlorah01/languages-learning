# Lesson 60 — Filler and Discourse Words
### Phase 6: Natural, Spoken English | Module 2 of 5

---

## 0. Where this fits
Lesson 59 gave you the words that adjust **how sure and how forceful** you sound. This lesson covers the words that manage **the flow of the conversation itself**: *well, so, you know, I mean, actually, anyway, right, okay, like, basically.* Linguists call them **discourse markers** (and, when they carry almost no meaning, **fillers**).

These words almost never appear in grammar books, yet a speaker without them sounds scripted, even if every sentence is correct. They also hide one of the biggest false friends for Spanish speakers: ***actually*** **≠** ***actualmente***.

Connections:
- **Lesson 51:** many of these are chunks (*you know, I mean, to be honest*).
- **Lesson 55:** they are mostly informal-to-neutral register.
- **Lesson 58:** discourse idioms (*by the way, long story short, at the end of the day*).
- **Lesson 59:** hedges and discourse markers overlap (*kind of, I guess, to be honest*).
- **Lessons 61–63 (next):** agreement, disagreement, small talk, and storytelling all depend on these words.

> **Core idea:** discourse markers are the "road signs" of speech. They tell the listener where you are going (*so, anyway*), what to expect (*actually, well*), and that you are still talking (*um, you know*).

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
Written language arrives finished. Spoken language is built **live**. While you speak, you are planning the next phrase, watching the other person's face, and trying to keep your turn. Fillers and markers are the tools that make that possible:

- **Time to think:** *um, uh, well, let me see, how can I put it.*
- **Direction signals:** *so, anyway, by the way, right, okay.*
- **Self-repair:** *I mean, or rather, sorry, that is.*
- **Connection with the listener:** *you know, right?, you see.*
- **Attitude:** *honestly, obviously, actually, basically.*

Imagine driving without indicators: you might still arrive, but everyone around you is confused. A response with no markers is understandable, but it feels **abrupt, robotic, or oddly formal.**

Spanish uses the same tools: *pues, bueno, o sea, entonces, a ver, ¿no?, ¿sabes?, este, digamos, la verdad, en fin.* The skills transfer, but the **words do not map one-to-one**, and Spanish fillers like *este* and *o sea* sound odd when dropped into English.

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 Terminology
| Term | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Filler (hesitation marker)** | sound or word that fills a pause | *um, uh, er (UK), hmm, ah* |
| **Discourse marker** | short word/phrase that organizes speech | *well, so, anyway, you know, I mean, right, okay, actually* |
| **Backchannel (response token)** | listener sounds that show attention | *mm-hm, uh-huh, yeah, right, I see, really?, wow* |
| **Turn-holding device** | keeps you in control of the floor | *um..., and, so..., let me think, the thing is* |
| **Check / tag** | asks for confirmation | *right?, you know?, okay?, you see?* |
| **Repair marker** | corrects or rephrases | *I mean, sorry, rather, or rather, what I meant was* |
| **Stance marker** | shows attitude | *honestly, obviously, seriously, basically, frankly* |

### 2.2 Functions of the main markers

#### (a) **Well**
| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| start an answer / think | *Well, it depends.* |
| soften disagreement | *Well, I'm not sure about that.* |
| show reluctance/disappointment | *Well, I suppose so.* |
| accept a situation | *Oh well, never mind.* |
| conclude | *Well, I should get going.* |
| resume after interruption | *Well, as I was saying…* |
| (also adverb) | *She plays well.* |

*Well* is the single most useful marker for beginning an answer. It buys time and softens whatever follows.

#### (b) **So**
| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| start a topic or story | *So, I was at the supermarket yesterday…* |
| resume after a pause | *So, where were we?* |
| conclude / draw consequence | *So, that's why I left.* |
| ask a question after a pause | *So, how was your trip?* |
| trail off and yield the turn | *I told him it was too late, so…* |
| end an exchange | *So, yeah. That's about it.* |

**Spanish trap:** Spanish *entonces* often = English **so**, not ***then***. *Then* means "after that" or "in that case": *First we ate, then we left. If you're tired, then go to bed.*
- ❌ *Then, what do you think?* (opening a question)
- ✅ *So, what do you think?*

#### (c) **You know / you see**
| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| appeal to shared knowledge | *He's that guy from accounting, you know?* |
| fill a pause while searching | *It's a, you know, a kind of tool.* |
| hint that the listener understands | *I was, you know, upset.* |
| check understanding (end) | *…and it's too late now, you know?* |
| *You know what?* | introduces a sudden decision or idea: *You know what? Let's go.* |
| *You know what I mean?* | check: *It's complicated, you know what I mean?* |
| *You see* | explains: *You see, the problem is the budget.* |

#### (d) **I mean**
| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| correct yourself | *It's on Tuesday, I mean Thursday.* |
| clarify/justify | *I mean, she's right, but she could have been nicer.* |
| soften | *I mean, it's okay, but not great.* |
| introduce an explanation | *I mean, what else can we do?* |

*I mean* is **not** the Spanish *o sea* for every context, but it is the closest everyday equivalent for self-repair and clarification.

#### (e) **Actually** (very high priority for Spanish speakers)
**Actually ≠ actualmente.**
| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| *actualmente* | **currently, at the moment, nowadays, at present** |
| *actual* (adj.) | **current, present** |
| *en realidad / de hecho / la verdad* | **actually, in fact, really, as a matter of fact** |

**Functions of *actually***
1. **Correct or contradict politely:** *I'm not from Bogotá, actually. I'm from Cali.*
2. **Give surprising/contrasting information:** *I actually like the rain.*
3. **Introduce a new idea/decision:** *Actually, let's go home.*
4. **Soften a refusal/disagreement:** *Actually, I'd rather not.*
5. **Emphasize reality vs expectation:** *It was actually quite easy.*
6. **Show you changed your mind:** *Actually, wait. I have a better idea.*

**Common errors**
- ❌ *Actually I live in Medellín.* (when simply stating a fact with no contrast)
- ✅ *I currently live in Medellín. / At the moment I live in Medellín.*
- ❌ *Actually, the price is 20 dollars.* (as simple information)
- ✅ *The price is currently 20 dollars.* / *It's actually 20, not 30.* (correcting an expectation)

Related: *in fact, as a matter of fact* (more emphatic), *in reality, to be precise, strictly speaking, technically.*

#### (f) **Anyway / anyways (US) / at any rate**
| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| return to the main topic | *Anyway, as I was saying…* |
| close a digression/conversation | *Anyway, I should go.* |
| dismiss | *Whatever happens, anyway, we'll manage.* |
| introduce the key point | *Anyway, the main thing is…* |

#### (g) **Right / okay / alright**
| Item | Uses |
|---|---|
| **Right** | acknowledge (*Right, I see*), start a new step (*Right, let's begin*), check (*That's the plan, right?*), UK: end of conversation (*Right, I'd better go*) |
| **Okay / OK** | agree, begin (*Okay, so…*), close (*Okay, bye!*), check (*Okay?*) |
| **Alright** | agree, begin, UK: also "hello" (*Alright?* = Hi, how are you?) |
| **All right** (two words) | formal spelling; also means "satisfactory" |

#### (h) **Like**
*Like* has become one of the most frequent words in informal spoken English (especially US, younger speakers). Functions:
| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| quotative (reports speech/thought) | *And she was like, "No way."* |
| approximator | *It cost like fifty bucks.* |
| hedge/softener | *It's, like, kind of weird.* |
| hesitation/filler | *I was, like, um, really tired.* |
| focus | *There was like nobody there.* |
| (standard uses) | *I like it. He looks like his father.* |

**Warning:** overuse, especially mid-sentence, is stigmatized in formal settings. For active use, prefer *kind of / sort of / around / about* and the quotative *was like* when telling a casual story. Understand all uses.

#### (i) **Basically / literally / obviously / honestly / seriously / totally**
| Word | Function | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| **basically** | summarize, simplify (*Basically, we ran out of money.*) | overuse sounds filler-like |
| **literally** | informal intensifier, often not literal (*I literally died laughing.*) | some speakers dislike this; use *really* in neutral contexts |
| **obviously** | "as everyone knows" (*Obviously, we'll need approval.*) | can sound condescending (*Obviously you didn't read it*) |
| **honestly** | sincerity (*Honestly, I loved it.*) | overuse questions your sincerity |
| **seriously** | emphasis or disbelief (*Seriously? That's amazing!*) | tone matters |
| **totally** | strong agreement (US) (*Totally!*) | US informal |
| **definitely** | strong agreement (*Definitely!*) | neutral |
| **absolutely** | strong agreement | neutral-strong |

#### (j) Sequencing and organizing speech
| Function | Markers |
|---|---|
| **Start** | *So, / Okay, so / Right, / Well, / Let me start by… / First of all…* |
| **Add** | *and, also, plus, on top of that, besides, what's more, another thing is…* |
| **Contrast** | *but, though, however, on the other hand, then again, having said that, still* |
| **Result** | *so, that's why, as a result, which means…, therefore (formal)* |
| **Example** | *for example, for instance, like, such as, say* |
| **Sequence** | *first, then, after that, next, and then, finally, in the end, eventually* |
| **Summarize** | *so basically, to sum up, in short, long story short, all in all* |
| **Digress** | *by the way, speaking of which, that reminds me, on another note* |
| **Return** | *anyway, as I was saying, to get back to the point, where was I?* |
| **Close** | *anyway, right, okay, so, well, that's about it, I should get going* |

#### (k) Backchannel: the listener's job
Listening in English is **active**. If you stay silent while someone tells a story, they may assume you're bored, confused, or not following.
| Signal | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Continuer** | *mm-hm, uh-huh, yeah, right, okay, I see, sure* |
| **Understanding** | *Oh, I see. / Got it. / Ah, okay. / That makes sense.* |
| **Surprise/interest** | *Really? / No way! / Seriously? / Wow! / Oh my God! / You're kidding!* |
| **Sympathy** | *Oh no. / That's awful. / I'm so sorry. / That sounds rough.* |
| **Agreement** | *Exactly. / Totally. / Absolutely. / I know, right?* |
| **Clarification** | *Sorry, what do you mean? / So you mean… ?* |

**Careful:**
- *Uh-huh* = yes/continue. *Huh?* = what? (confused). *Hm?* = pardon? Mixing them up causes confusion.
- *Yeah* during someone's turn usually means "I'm listening", **not** "I agree." Don't over-interpret.
- *Right* in the middle of someone's turn is "I follow", but *Right?* at the end of your own sentence asks for agreement.
- *Eh?* is "what?" in the UK and Canada, not the Spanish hesitation sound.

#### (l) Fillers (hesitation sounds)
- English fillers: **um, uh, er (UK), hmm**. Nasal *um* signals a longer pause; *uh* a shorter one.
- Spanish: *eh, este, mmm, o sea, pues.* Transfer to English **sounds strange** (*este* has no English meaning).
- Research suggests *um/uh* act as signals ("I'm about to pause") and help listeners predict what comes next. They are not signs of weakness in themselves.
- **Better than silence, worse than none, when overused.** Use a mix: a short *um*, a marker (*well, so*), a phrase (*let me think*), then speak.

### 2.3 "Hold the floor" phrases: your emergency kit
When you need thinking time, use a **real phrase** instead of a long silence or a Spanish filler.

| Situation | Phrases |
|---|---|
| **Buying time** | *Let me think… / Let me see… / Hmm, good question. / That's a good point. / Give me a second. / How can I put this? / What's the word? / It's on the tip of my tongue.* |
| **Rephrasing** | *What I mean is… / What I'm trying to say is… / Let me put it another way. / In other words…* |
| **Not knowing** | *I'm not sure, but I think… / I don't know off the top of my head. / I'd have to check.* |
| **Searching for a word** | *the... thing, you know, the... what do you call it? / It's like a... / It's a kind of…* |
| **Asking for help** | *How do you say…? / What's the word for…? / Is it… or…?* |
| **Interrupting politely** | *Sorry, can I just add something? / Sorry to interrupt, but… / Can I jump in here?* |
| **Not letting someone take the floor** | *Hold on, let me finish. / If I could just finish this point… / Just a second, I'm almost done.* |

### 2.4 Discourse markers in writing
- **Spoken markers** (*well, you know, I mean, like*) are mostly **not** used in formal writing.
- **Written equivalents** are more formal: *however, therefore, in addition, moreover, nevertheless, in conclusion* (Lesson 56).
- In **informal writing** (chat, email to friends, dialogue in fiction), spoken markers are acceptable and make text sound natural.

| Spoken | Written (formal) |
|---|---|
| *But* | *However* |
| *So* | *Therefore / Consequently* |
| *And also* | *Moreover / Furthermore* |
| *Anyway* | *In any case* |
| *Basically* | *In essence / Essentially* |
| *Actually* | *In fact / In reality* |
| *By the way* | *Incidentally* |
| *Anyway, to sum up* | *In conclusion* |

## 3. Why Native Speakers Do This
1. **Speech is planned in real time.** Markers give planning time without losing the turn.
2. **Turn-taking.** Conversation is a shared floor. Markers signal "I'm still talking", "I'm done", or "I want to speak."
3. **Politeness.** *Well* and *actually* cushion disagreement (Lesson 59).
4. **Cohesion.** Spoken English has little punctuation; markers do the work of commas, paragraphs, and connectors.
5. **Shared identity.** The kind of markers someone uses (*like, innit, y'know, mate, dude*) signals age, region, and group.
6. **Listener care.** Backchannels and checks show engagement and avoid misunderstandings.
7. **Habit.** Native speakers produce them automatically and often cannot say how often they use them.

Cross-linguistically, every language has these devices. English has fewer **obligatory** ones than some languages, but they are heard constantly, and a speaker who lacks them seems stiff. A speaker who relies on a single one (*like, you know*) seems unprepared.

---

## 4. When to Use / When Not To

**Use markers and fillers when**
- you need time to think (*well, let me think, um*);
- you want to start or close a topic (*so, anyway, right*);
- you disagree softly (*well, actually, I mean*);
- you're telling a story or chatting (*so, and then, like, you know*);
- you want to show you're listening (*mm-hm, really?, I see*);
- you want to sound natural, friendly, or casual.

**Reduce or avoid when**
- giving a **formal presentation, speech, or interview.** Use signposting (*Let me start with…, Moving on…*), pauses, and a few controlled fillers;
- **writing** formally (replace with *however, therefore, in fact*);
- you notice yourself using **one marker repeatedly** (*like, you know, basically, literally*). It distracts and signals insecurity;
- giving **precise instructions** (medical, legal, safety);
- speaking to **non-native listeners** who may find fast fillers confusing;
- **answering a yes/no question** that needs a clear yes/no.

**Practical rule:** in formal speech, replace most fillers with a **silent pause**. Native speakers also pause silently; short pauses (1–2 seconds) are fine and sound confident.

## 5. Frequency & Register

**Priority order for active use**
1. *well, so, okay, right, anyway* (organizing and turn management).
2. *you know, I mean, actually, basically* (clarifying and stance).
3. *um, uh* (hesitation).
4. *by the way, speaking of, that reminds me* (topic management).
5. *mm-hm, really?, I see, exactly* (backchannel).
6. *like* (understand fully; use sparingly).
7. *honestly, obviously, seriously, literally, totally* (stance; mind tone).

**Register**
| Level | Markers |
|---|---|
| **Very informal / youth** | *like, y'know, dude, literally, totally, whatever, so yeah, I'm like, innit (UK)* |
| **Informal–neutral** | *well, so, you know, I mean, anyway, actually, basically, right* |
| **Neutral–formal** | *as I was saying, to be honest, in fact, as a matter of fact, in other words, to be precise* |
| **Formal / written** | *however, therefore, moreover, in conclusion, incidentally, nevertheless* |

**Trend:** *like* and *literally* keep expanding, especially in US youth speech; *basically* and *obviously* are common in professional speech; *anyways* is spreading in US informal speech; *innit* (UK) and *fam*, *bruh*, *no cap* belong to specific social groups. For active learning, stay with the neutral markers.

## 6. American vs. British Notes

| Feature | US | UK |
|---|---|---|
| hesitation sound | *um, uh* | *um, er, erm* |
| "anyway" | *anyway, anyways* (informal) | *anyway* |
| "right" | *Right? / Okay?* checks | *Right, … (start/close); Alright? = hello; Right then* |
| "like" | very frequent quotative and filler | common among young speakers; *sort of* more typical for hedging |
| "you know" | *y'know* | *y'know; you know what I mean?* |
| "totally" | *Totally!* strong agreement | rare; *absolutely / definitely / exactly* |
| "mate / dude / bro" | *dude, man, bro* | *mate, pal, love (regional)* |
| "innit" | no | *innit* (= isn't it?) in London and youth speech |
| "to be fair" | less frequent | very frequent: *To be fair, he did try.* |
| "mind you" | rare | *Mind you, it was cheap.* |
| "fair enough" | common | very common |
| "oh well / oh dear" | *oh well* | *oh well / oh dear / oh God* |
| thanks/ending | *thanks, take care, have a good one* | *cheers, ta, ta-ra (North)* |
| "I guess" | very frequent | *I suppose* more typical |
| "pretty" (= fairly) | *pretty good* | *quite good, rather good* |
| "I'm good" | = no thanks / I'm fine | = I'm fine |
| "sure / no problem / you bet" | *sure, no problem, you bet* | *no problem, not at all, don't mention it* |
| "How are you?" | greeting; "Good, you?" | *You alright? How's it going? Alright?* |
| "Excuse me" | gets attention | *Sorry* as reflex; *Excuse me* more formal |

**Greeting formulas (important!)**
- *How are you? / How's it going? / What's up?* are **greetings, not questions**. Expected reply: *Good, thanks. You?* or *Not bad, you?*. Don't give a medical history.
- UK *You alright?* is also a greeting; reply *Yeah, you?*, not a literal "am I alright?".

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

### 7.1 *Actually* (the big one)
| ❌ Error | ✅ Natural |
|---|---|
| *Actually I live in Bogotá.* (simple fact) | *I currently live in Bogotá. / I live in Bogotá at the moment.* |
| *Actually the economy is bad.* (meaning "currently") | *The economy is currently bad. / Right now the economy is bad.* |
| *The actual president is…* (meaning "the current president") | *The current president is…* |
| *Actually we are studying English.* | *We are currently studying English.* |
| *I actual work at a bank.* | *I currently work at a bank.* |
| *Actually, I'm Colombian.* (answering "Where are you from?") | *I'm Colombian.* (No contrast, no *actually*.) |

Remember: **actually = contrary to expectation or correction**; **currently = at present.**

### 7.2 Spanish discourse markers transferred
| Spanish habit | ❌ English transfer | ✅ English |
|---|---|---|
| *este... este...* | *este... eh...* | *um... uh... well...* |
| *o sea* | *or be / or sea* | *I mean / that is / so* |
| *pues* | *then / well, then* | *well / so* |
| *entonces* (start) | *Then, ...* | *So, ...* |
| *bueno* (start) | *Good, ...* | *Well, ... / Okay, ...* |
| *a ver* | *To see...* | *Let's see... / Let me think... / Okay...* |
| *¿no?* (tag) | *No?* | *right? / isn't it? / don't you think?* |
| *¿sí?* (check) | *Yes?* | *Okay? / Right? / Does that make sense?* |
| *¿entiendes?* | *Do you understand?* (sounds like a test) | *You know what I mean? / Does that make sense?* |
| *la verdad...* | *The truth...* | *To be honest, ... / Honestly, ...* |
| *en fin* | *In end* | *Anyway, ... / In short, ...* |
| *digamos* | *Let's say* ✅ (fine in some contexts) | *Let's say..., sort of, so to speak* |
| *total, que...* | *Total, that...* | *So, ... / Anyway, ...* |
| *vamos, que...* | *We go, that...* | *I mean, ... / Basically...* |
| *ya* (backchannel) | *Already* | *Right / Okay / I see / Yeah* |
| *claro* | *Clear* | *Of course / Sure / Exactly / Right* |
| *vale* | *Valid* | *Okay / Alright / Sure* |
| *ay* | — | *Oh* |
| *¡Qué va!* | *What goes!* | *No way! / Not at all!* |

### 7.3 Behavior and listening
| ❌ Habit | Problem | ✅ Better |
|---|---|---|
| Silence while the other speaks | seems bored/unengaged | add *mm-hm, I see, really?* |
| Long silent pauses mid-turn | speaker seems stuck | *Let me think…, well…* |
| Answering *How are you?* with details | misreads greeting as question | *Good, thanks. You?* |
| Using *yes* as constant backchannel | may sound like agreement | vary: *I see, right, okay* |
| Interrupting at the first overlap (Spanish overlap is common) | may seem rude in some English settings | wait for a pause, use *Sorry, can I just…* |
| Never ending the conversation | awkward | *Anyway, I should get going. It was great talking to you.* |
| Ending abruptly | rude | *Well, I'd better go. Take care!* |
| *Yes, yes, yes* (rapid repetition) | may sound impatient or dismissive | *Yeah, I see.* |
| Over-nodding without sound on the phone | invisible | add verbal backchannels |

### 7.4 Overuse and wrong use
- ❌ *Like, I, like, was, like, really tired.* (like every word) → limit *like*.
- ❌ *Basically, basically, basically.*
- ❌ *You know, you know?* repeated at every clause.
- ❌ *Literally* with literal meaning wrongly emphasized: *I literally died* (when you didn't).
- ❌ *Actually* at the start of every answer.
- ❌ *Well* + long pause before every answer, interpreted as reluctance.
- ❌ *So yeah* used to end every story. (Natural once; repeated, it's a tic.)
- ❌ *Right?* after every sentence (sounds insecure).
- ❌ Translating *o sea que...* as *or so that...*

### 7.5 Intonation traps
- **Falling *Well.*** = reluctance or disapproval. **Rising *Well?*** = "so? what's your answer?"
- ***So?*** with sharp falling tone = "and what?" (rude). *So…?* rising/gentle = "go on."
- ***Actually*** with flat intonation sounds neutral; with a high pitch it sounds corrective or defensive.
- ***Really?*** with falling tone = disbelief/sarcasm; rising = genuine interest.
- ***Right.*** with falling tone = I got it; ***Right?*** rising = confirm.

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ **"Fillers are a sign of poor English."** — Native speakers use them constantly. The problem is overuse or using the wrong ones.
- ❌ **"Fluent speakers never pause."** — They pause often. Fluency means *managed* pauses.
- ❌ **"I should eliminate all fillers."** — Aim for fewer, better ones: *well, so, you know,* and silent pauses.
- ❌ **"*Actually* means *actualmente*."** — It does not. It marks contrast or correction.
- ❌ **"*Like* is meaningless."** — It has several functions (approximation, quotation, hedging), though overuse is a style issue.
- ❌ **"*Literally* must always be literal."** — In informal speech it is an intensifier. Prescriptive objections exist; in formal speech, use it literally.
- ❌ **"*So* at the start of a sentence is wrong."** — It is common and natural in speech.
- ❌ **"*And* and *but* can't begin a sentence."** — They can, and do, especially in speech.
- ❌ **"*Yeah* means yes."** — As a backchannel it means "I'm listening."
- ❌ **"Formal speech needs no markers."** — It needs formal ones: *Firstly, As I mentioned earlier, Turning now to…*
- ❌ **"Using many markers = fluent."** — Precision and variety, not quantity.
- ❌ **"Silence is polite."** — In English conversation, long silences feel uncomfortable (more than in some other cultures). A short *Hmm, let me think* is better.

| huh? | /hʌ/ | rising: "what?" |

### 9.2 Intonation = meaning
The same word can show very different attitudes by tone:
| Word | Tone | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *Well.* | falling, slow | reluctance, disapproval |
| *Well,* | rising-fall + continuation | "let me think" |
| *Okay.* | falling | I agree/accept |
| *Okay?* | rising | check |
| *Okay…* | falling-rising | doubtful, reluctant |
| *Right.* | falling | I understand |
| *Right?* | rising | confirm |
| *Really?* | rising | genuine interest |
| *Really.* | falling | skepticism or emphasis |
| *Sure.* | high-falling | enthusiastic yes |
| *Sure…* | low, falling-rising | hesitant yes |
| *Fine.* | falling, flat | may mean "not fine" |
| *Whatever.* | flat/falling | dismissive |

Practice with a recording: say each in three tones.

### 9.3 Spanish-speaker problem spots
- **Final consonants in short words:** *right, well, so, mean, know*: keep the final sound.
- **Vowel contrast /ʌ/:** *um, uh, but, just, much.* Not /a/ or /u/.
- **/h/ in *hmm, huh, honestly (silent)*:** pronounce *hmm* with lips closed.
- **/aɪ/ in *right, like, I mean, alright:* a clear diphthong.
- **/θ/ and /ð/ in *then, that, the, anything*:** tongue between teeth.
- **s-clusters:** *so, sure, sorry, seriously* with no "e" before them.
- **Fast chunks:** *you know what I mean* → "y'knowwhaddaImean", know the reduced form to understand it (not necessarily to copy it).
- **Rhythm:** English is stress-timed; markers are usually **unstressed and quick**; Spanish speakers often give them equal weight and slow them down, which sounds heavy.

## 10. Memory Technique

### 10.1 The "road signs" map
Group markers by the kind of sign they provide:
- 🟢 **Start / go:** *so, okay, right, well*
- 🔵 **Detour:** *by the way, speaking of which, that reminds me*
- 🟡 **Slow down / thinking:** *um, well, let me think, you know*
- 🔴 **Correction / stop and fix:** *I mean, actually, sorry, or rather*
- 🟣 **Return to route:** *anyway, as I was saying*
- ⚪ **End of route:** *so, anyway, right, well*

### 10.2 Replace, don't remove
Don't try to "stop saying este". **Replace** it with a chosen set. Choose three personal fillers and practice them until automatic:
1. Short hesitation: **um**
2. Thinking phrase: **well, let me think**
3. Organizing: **so**

Over time, your brain defaults to these.

### 10.3 Transcript analysis
Take 2 minutes of a real conversation (podcast, series, interview with transcript). Highlight every marker and note its function. Counting is revealing; you'll see how many different types occur and how short they are.

### 10.4 The 10-second pause practice
Practice answering questions with **silence + a clear answer** instead of fillers. 1–2 seconds of silence is normal and sounds thoughtful; a quick *Hmm* is enough.

### 10.5 Backchannel training
Listen to a 3-minute story. After each idea unit, produce a backchannel aloud (*mm-hm, I see, really?, wow*). This builds the habit of active listening.

### 10.6 Rewrite and speak
Take a written paragraph and convert it to a spoken version with natural markers. Record both. Then do the reverse: take a transcript and convert it into formal writing.

### 10.7 Spaced retrieval cards
- Front: *Spanish actualmente →* **currently / at the moment**
- Front: *Spanish entonces (start of a question) →* **So, …**
- Front: *Correct yourself mid-sentence →* **I mean, …**
- Front: *Return to the main point →* **Anyway, …**
- Front: *Politely correct someone →* **Actually, …**
- Front: *Need thinking time →* **Let me think… / Well…**
Review on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30.

### 10.8 Shadowing for rhythm
Shadow 1 minute of casual conversation. Focus on **speed and reduction** of markers (*y'know, I mean, kinda*). You don't need to copy exactly; you need to *hear* them.

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1: Answer opening = marker + core answer.** *Well, it depends. / So, basically, we need more time. / Honestly, I'm not sure.*

> **Pattern 2: Story = So + background + and then + the thing happened + so + result.** *So I was walking home, and then this guy starts shouting, so I just crossed the street.*

> **Pattern 3: Repair = statement + I mean + correction.** *It's on Tuesday, I mean Thursday.*

> **Pattern 4: Polite correction = Actually + new information.** *Actually, the meeting is at three.*

> **Pattern 5: Digression handling = by the way / speaking of → anyway.** *By the way, did you hear about Sam? … Anyway, back to the plan.*

> **Pattern 6: Closing = marker + reason + wish.** *Anyway, I should get going. It was great catching up!*

> **Pattern 7: Backchannel rhythm.** Respond at the end of each idea unit (not in the middle). Vary the tokens.

> **Pattern 8: Check = statement + tag.** *…so we'll start at nine, right? / That makes sense, doesn't it? / Does that make sense?*

> **Pattern 9: Hold the floor = marker + pause + phrase + continue.** *Well… let me think… I'd say it's about 60%.*

> **Pattern 10: Stance marker at the beginning.** *Honestly, / Obviously, / Frankly, / Basically,* + comma + statement.

> **Pattern 11: Combine, don't stack.** One or two markers per clause maximum: ✅ *Well, I mean, it's okay.* ❌ *Well, like, you know, I mean, basically, it's, like, okay.*

> **Pattern 12: Marker + question.** *So, what do you think? / Right, so what's next? / Okay, but why?*

> **Connections:** Lesson 59 (hedges share many forms), Lesson 55 (register), Lesson 61 (agreeing/disagreeing: *well, fair enough, I see what you mean*), Lesson 62 (small talk: *so, anyway*), Lesson 63 (storytelling: *so, and then, long story short*).

## 12. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1 — Basic**
- *Well, I don't know.*
- *So, what's your name?*
- *Okay, let's start.*
- *Really? That's great!*
- *Anyway, thanks for your help.*

**Level 2 — Everyday**
- *So, how was your weekend?*
- *I mean, it was fine, but nothing special.*
- *Actually, I'm from Cali, not Bogotá.*
- *Right, so we meet at six?*
- *By the way, did you call her?*

**Level 3 — Intermediate**
- *Well, to be honest, I'm not sure it's a good idea.*
- *It's, you know, kind of hard to explain.*
- *Basically, we ran out of time, so we left early.*
- *Anyway, as I was saying, the plan changed.*
- *I actually quite like it, believe it or not.*

**Level 4 — Advanced**
- *Honestly, I thought it'd be worse, but it was actually pretty good.*
- *I mean, I get where he's coming from, but, you know, it's not that simple.*
- *Right, okay, so the thing is, we're short on budget, so we'll have to cut something.*
- *It's not that I don't like her, it's more that we, I don't know, don't have much in common.*
- *Speaking of which, have you heard back from the client?*

**Level 5 — Native-like**
- "So, yeah, I get what you're saying, but, I mean, we've been over this, and, honestly, I don't think anything's changed."
- "Anyway, long story short, he never showed up, so we just, you know, ended up going without him."
- "I was, like, 'No way,' and she was like, 'Seriously, I'm not joking,' and I'm just standing there, like..."
- "Well, that's one way of looking at it. Mind you, I'd say it's a bit more complicated than that."
- "Right, well, I won't keep you. It was really good to catch up. Let's do this again soon."

### 12.1 Mini-dialogues

**1. Small talk, opening and closing**
> **A:** Hey! How's it going?
> **B:** Good, thanks. You?
> **A:** Not bad. Busy week. So, how was your trip?
> **B:** Oh, it was great. Actually, it was better than I expected.
> **A:** Nice! Anyway, I've got a meeting. Let's catch up soon.
> **B:** Definitely. Take care!

**2. Self-repair and clarification**
> **A:** When's the deadline?
> **B:** It's on the 15th, I mean the 16th. Sorry, I always mix them up.
> **A:** No worries. So it's Friday, right?
> **B:** Yeah, exactly.

**3. Polite correction with *actually***
> **A:** So you're from Medellín, right?
> **B:** Actually, I'm from Cali, but I live in Medellín now.
> **A:** Oh, sorry! My mistake.
> **B:** No problem at all!

**4. Hold the floor**
> **A:** What do you think we should do?
> **B:** Hmm, good question. Let me think… Well, I'd say we have two options. One is to wait, and the other is, you know, to act now.

**5. Returning to a topic**
> **A:** …and then the dog jumped in the lake! Oh, by the way, did you see the game last night?
> **B:** No, I missed it.
> **A:** Anyway, so the dog's soaking wet, and we're all laughing…

**6. Backchannels in a story**
> **A:** So I was on the bus yesterday…
> **B:** Mm-hm.
> **A:** …and this guy sits next to me, and he starts singing.
> **B:** No way!
> **A:** Yeah, and he was actually really good.
> **B:** Ha! That's amazing.

**7. Disagreeing politely**
> **A:** I think we should cancel the project.
> **B:** Well, I'm not sure. I mean, we've already invested a lot. Actually, what if we just pause it for a month?

### 12.2 Before and after (naturalness)
> ❌ *"Hello. I am fine. Actually I work in a bank. Then, what do you do?"*
> ✅ *"Hi! I'm good, thanks. I work at a bank at the moment. So, what do you do?"*

> ❌ *"Este... eh... the problem is... eh... that the... este... budget is... no?"*
> ✅ *"Well... the problem is, um, the budget is, you know, a bit tight."*

> ❌ *"Yes, yes, yes, I understand. Yes."* (as continuous backchannel on a call)
> ✅ *"Mm-hm... I see... Right... Okay, got it."*

### 12.3 Formal vs informal
| Informal speech | Formal writing |
|---|---|
| *Anyway, the main thing is we need more time.* | *In any case, the key issue is that additional time is required.* |
| *Basically, it didn't work.* | *In essence, the approach was unsuccessful.* |
| *Actually, we saw a rise.* | *In fact, there was an increase.* |
| *So, what happened was…* | *The sequence of events was as follows.* |
| *By the way…* | *Incidentally…* |

## 13. Collocations & Related Expressions

**Phrases for managing conversation**
- *to change the subject, to get back to the point, to go off on a tangent, to beat around the bush, to get to the point, to cut someone off, to interrupt, to chip in, to jump in, to have the floor, to take the floor, to hold the floor, to yield the floor, to have a word with someone*
- *to keep the conversation going, to run out of things to say, to break the silence, to fill an awkward silence, to make small talk, to bring up a topic, to drop the subject*
- *to speak up, to speak your mind, to say your piece, to get a word in edgeways (UK)/ get a word in edgewise (US)*
- *to put it another way, to put it bluntly, to put it mildly, to be fair, to be honest, to be precise, to be clear*

**Discourse markers by function (quick reference)**
| Function | Markers |
|---|---|
| Opening a turn | *Well, / So, / Okay, / Right, / Look, / Listen, / Honestly,* |
| Thinking | *Um, / Hmm, / Let me think, / How can I put it? / What's the word?* |
| Rephrasing | *I mean, / In other words, / What I mean is, / That is, / Or rather,* |
| Adding | *Also, / And, / Plus, / Besides, / What's more,* |
| Contrasting | *But, / Though, / Still, / On the other hand, / Then again, / Having said that,* |
| Concluding | *So, / Anyway, / In the end, / All in all, / Long story short,* |
| Topic shift | *By the way, / Speaking of which, / That reminds me, / On another note,* |
| Emphasis/attitude | *Honestly, / Seriously, / Obviously, / Frankly, / Clearly, / To be honest,* |
| Checking | *Right? / You know? / Okay? / Does that make sense? / See what I mean?* |
| Agreeing | *Exactly. / Absolutely. / I know, right? / Totally. / Fair enough.* |
| Closing | *Anyway, / Right, / Well, / So, / I should get going.* |

**Contrast groups**
| Items | Differences |
|---|---|
| *actually / currently / at the moment / nowadays* | *actually* = in fact; others = at present (*nowadays* = in these days vs. the past) |
| *eventually / finally / in the end / at last* | *eventually* = after a long time, as a result (**not** *eventualmente*); *finally* = after waiting; *in the end* = as a final outcome; *at last* = relief |
| *so / then / therefore / thus* | *so* informal; *then* = next/in that case; *therefore/thus* formal |
| *well / okay / right / alright* | *well* = pause/hesitation; *okay/right/alright* = transitions and agreement |
| *I mean / that is / in other words* | informal / formal-written / neutral |
| *anyway / besides / however* | return/dismiss / extra reason / contrast |
| *honestly / frankly / to be honest / to tell you the truth* | informal / formal-direct / neutral / informal |
| *really / actually / in fact / as a matter of fact* | *really* = truly/very; *actually* = contrary to expectation; *in fact/as a matter of fact* = stronger |
| *like / such as / for example / for instance* | *like* informal; others neutral/formal |
| *obviously / clearly / evidently / of course* | assumes shared knowledge; *evidently* formal; *of course* = naturally |
| *mm-hm / uh-huh / yeah / yep* | continuers vs. agreement (informal) |
