# Lesson 67 — Connected Speech: Linking, Reductions, Weak Forms
### Phase 7: Pronunciation & the Sound System | Module 4 of 5

---

## 0. Where this fits
Lesson 66 showed that English squeezes the small syllables between the beats. This lesson opens the hood and shows **exactly how** words are squeezed, joined, and altered when people really talk.

If you have ever thought, *"I know every word in that sentence, so why couldn't I understand it?"*, this is the lesson that answers the question. Written English is a row of separate words. Spoken English is a **stream of sound chunks** in which word boundaries often disappear.

Connections:
- **Lesson 64:** the schwa /ə/, the flap /ɾ/, and the sounds you need.
- **Lesson 65:** weak forms were introduced; here you get the full toolkit.
- **Lesson 66:** the rhythm that *demands* these changes.
- **Lessons 60 and 62:** *y'know, kinda, sorta, How ya doin'?* are connected speech in action.
- **Lesson 68 (next):** intonation, the melody over these chunks.
- **Phase 8:** listening and speaking, where you will use all of this.

> **Core idea:** In natural English, you don't say **words**; you say **chunks**. Inside a chunk, sounds **link**, **weaken**, **change**, or **disappear** according to regular rules. Learn the rules and "fast, mumbled" English becomes predictable.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
Say this slowly, word by word: **"Did. You. Eat. Yet."** Now say it the way a friend would across the kitchen: **"Jeet yet?"**

Nothing "wrong" happened. Four words became two chunks:
- *Did you* → "didja" → "ja"
- *eat yet* → "eat-yet"

Think of spoken English like **cursive handwriting**. In print, every letter stands alone. In cursive, letters connect, and some shapes change depending on their neighbors. Native speakers talk in "cursive." You have been learning English in "print."

A second image: **words are LEGO bricks in the dictionary, but in speech they are glued into larger pieces.** You can still take the pieces apart (that is what careful speech does), but the usual form is glued.

The good news: gluing follows **patterns**. Five processes explain most of what you hear:

| # | Process | One-line idea | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Linking** | final consonant jumps to the next vowel | *pick it up* → "pi-ki-tup" |
| 2 | **Glides** | /j/ or /w/ slips between two vowels | *go on* → "go-w-on" |
| 3 | **Elision** | a sound disappears | *next day* → "nexday" |
| 4 | **Assimilation** | a sound changes to match its neighbor | *ten bikes* → "tem bikes" |
| 5 | **Weak forms & reductions** | small words shrink | *want to* → "wanna" |

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 Why connected speech exists
Speech is produced by moving muscles. Stopping fully between words wastes effort, so the mouth **anticipates** the next sound (assimilation), **skips** what isn't needed (elision), and **glides** from one position to the next (linking). All languages do this. What differs is **which** processes each language uses, and how far it goes. English goes far because of the stress-timed rhythm from Lesson 66: unstressed material must be short.

### 2.2 Process 1 — Linking: consonant + vowel
When a word ends in a **consonant sound** and the next word begins with a **vowel sound**, the consonant attaches to the next syllable.

| Written | Sounds like | Note |
|---|---|---|
| pick it up | pi-ki-tup | in US speech the /t/ between vowels is a flap: "pi-ki-dup" |
| turn it off | tur-ni-toff | |
| check it out | che-ki-tout | |
| not at all | no-ta-tall | US: "no-da-dall" |
| look at it | loo-ka-tit | |
| half an hour | ha-fa-nour | |
| an old idea | a-nol-di-dea | |
| come on in | co-mo-nin | |
| What is it? | Wha-ti-zit? | |
| Can I help? | Ca-nigh help? | |

**Key point:** it is the *sound*, not the spelling. *Of* ends in /v/ (*a lot of it* → "a lo-to-vit"). *Church* ends in /tʃ/. A "silent e" word like *make* ends in /k/, so *make it* → "may-kit."

**Spanish connection (use what you have!):** Spanish does the same thing. *los amigos* is pronounced "lo-sa-mi-gos"; *el hombre* sounds like "e-lom-bre." Your brain already knows how to resyllabify. The problem in English is usually that you **stop** between words, or **add a vowel** after a final consonant ("pick-uh it-uh up"). Remove the extra vowel and the link happens by itself.

### 2.3 Process 2 — Linking: vowel + vowel (glides)
When a word ending in a **vowel** meets a word starting with a **vowel**, English slips in a tiny glide so the sounds don't collide.

| First vowel shape | Glide inserted | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| lips **spread/high front** (/i, eɪ, aɪ, ɔɪ/) | **/j/** ("y") | *see it* "see-y-it"; *I ask* "I-y-ask"; *they all* "they-y-all"; *the end* /ði-j-ɛnd/ |
| lips **rounded** (/u, oʊ, aʊ/) | **/w/** | *go on* "go-w-on"; *do it* "do-w-it"; *how are you* "how-w-are you"; *so I said* "so-w-I said" |

**Shortcut:** look at your lips. Spread → "y." Rounded → "w." You already do this automatically in your first language and, most of the time, in English; the lesson is simply to **let it happen** instead of putting a pause or a glottal stop in the gap.

### 2.4 Linking /r/ and intrusive /r/ (mainly British)
Standard British English (non-rhotic) drops *r* before a consonant or at the end of a phrase, **but pronounces it when a vowel follows**:
- *far away* → "far-r-away"; *here and there* → "here-r-and there" (**linking r**: the spelling has an *r*).
- *law and order* → "law-r-and order"; *the idea of it* → "idea-r-of it" (**intrusive r**: no *r* in the spelling).

American English is rhotic: it pronounces the *r* everywhere, so there is nothing special to link. Intrusive *r* is common in many British and other non-rhotic accents, and it is not something you need to produce. It helps to **recognize** it when you listen.

### 2.5 Process 3 — Elision: sounds that disappear
Between consonants, the sounds /t/ and /d/ (and sometimes /h/ and /v/) often vanish, especially in clusters of three or more consonants.

| Written | Typically said | Dropped |
|---|---|---|
| next day | "nexday" /nɛks deɪ/ | /t/ |
| last night | "lass night" /læs naɪt/ | /t/ |
| just now | "juss now" | /t/ |
| old man | "ol' man" /oʊl mæn/ | /d/ |
| grand piano | "gran piano" | /d/ |
| mostly | /ˈmoʊsli/ | /t/ |
| exactly (casual) | "exackly" | /t/ |
| Christmas, castle, listen, handsome | /ˈkrɪsməs, ˈkæsəl, ˈlɪsən, ˈhænsəm/ | silent letters (historic elision) |
| tell him, give her | "tell 'im," "gi-ver" | /h/ in unstressed pronouns |

**When it does NOT happen:** if the next word starts with a vowel, the consonant is kept and linked (*last of it*, *old uncle*). It is also kept in careful or emphasized speech.

### 2.6 Process 4 — Assimilation: sounds that change
A sound becomes more like the one that follows it.

**(a) Place assimilation** (common, mostly casual):
| Written | Casual sound | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| ten bikes | "tem bikes" | /n/ → /m/ before /b/ |
| in Paris | "im Paris" | /n/ → /m/ before /p/ |
| input | /ˈɪmpʊt/ | /n/ → /m/ |
| good boy | "goob boy" | /d/ → /b/ |
| that person | "thap person" | /t/ → /p/ |
| green card | "greeng card" | /n/ → /ŋ/ before /k/ |

You can **recognize** these; you don't need to produce them deliberately.

**(b) Coalescence (palatalization)**, very important for listening and also natural to produce:
When /t, d, s, z/ meet the word **you/your**, they merge into /tʃ, dʒ, ʃ, ʒ/.

| Written | Sounds like | IPA (approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| Did you...? | "didja" | /ˈdɪdʒə/ |
| Would you...? | "wouldja" | /ˈwʊdʒə/ |
| Don't you...? | "doncha" | /ˈdoʊntʃə/ |
| What you want | "whatcha want" | /ˈwʌtʃə/ |
| Nice to meet you | "nice to meechu" | /miːtʃu/ |
| Miss you | "mishu" | /ˈmɪʃu/ |
| As you know | "azhu know" | /əʒ ju/ |
| Got you | "gotcha" | /ˈɡɑtʃə/ |

### 2.7 Process 5 — Weak forms of function words
Grammar words (articles, prepositions, auxiliaries, pronouns, conjunctions) have a **weak form** used when they are unstressed (almost always) and a **strong form** used in special cases. Lesson 65 introduced the idea; here is the working list.

| Word | Weak form | Strong form | Example (weak) |
|---|---|---|---|
| a / an | /ə/, /ən/ | /eɪ/, /æn/ | a cup of tea |
| the | /ðə/ (before consonant) | /ðiː/ | the **BOOK** |
| to | /tə/ | /tuː/ | want to **GO** |
| of | /əv/ | /ɒv, ʌv/ | cup of **TEA** |
| and | /ən/, /n/ | /ænd/ | fish 'n' **CHIPS** |
| but | /bət/ | /bʌt/ | tired but **HAPPY** |
| or | /ər/ | /ɔːr/ | tea or **COFFEE** |
| for | /fər/ | /fɔːr/ | wait for **ME** |
| from | /frəm/ | /frʌm/ | from **BOGOTÁ** |
| at | /ət/ | /æt/ | at **SIX** |
| can | /kən/ | /kæn/ | I can **SWIM** |
| was / were | /wəz/, /wər/ | /wɑz/, /wɜːr/ | it was **LATE** |
| have / has / had | /əv, həv/, /əz/, /əd/ | /hæv, hæz, hæd/ | should have **GONE** |
| do / does | /də/, /dəz/ | /duː/, /dʌz/ | do you **KNOW**? |
| you / your | /jə/, /jər/ | /juː/, /jɔːr/ | see you **LATER** |
| him / her / them | /ɪm/, /ər/, /əm/ | /hɪm, hɜːr, ðɛm/ | tell them **NOW** |
| some | /səm/ | /sʌm/ | some **WATER** |
| than / that (conj.) | /ðən/, /ðət/ | /ðæn/, /ðæt/ | I think that **HE'S** right |

**When the strong form is required**
1. **At the end of a sentence/clause:** *Where are you **from**?* /frʌm/; *What are you looking **at**?* /æt/; *Yes, I **can**.* /kæn/; *She's taller than he **is**.* /ɪz/.
2. **For contrast or emphasis:** *I said **to**, not **from**.* *I **do** like it!*
3. **When the word is quoted or spoken alone:** *The word "of" is hard.*
4. **Before a pause or hesitation:** *I was going to... um...*

**Contractions** are weak forms that are also written: *I'm, you're, he's, we've, they'd, I'll, isn't, can't, won't.* Contractions are standard in speech and informal writing.

### 2.8 Common reduced forms ("spoken spellings")
These are written in informal text, lyrics, and dialogue. In speech, they are everyday.

| Full | Reduced | Approx. sound | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| going to (future) | **gonna** | /ˈɡʌnə/ | *I'm gonna call.* Not when "going" is a movement verb: *I'm going to the store* (usually not "gonna the store") |
| want to | **wanna** | /ˈwɑnə/ | *I wanna go.* (never "wanna to") |
| got to / have to | **gotta / hafta** | /ˈɡɑɾə/, /ˈhæftə/ | *I gotta leave.* |
| kind of / sort of | **kinda / sorta** | /ˈkaɪndə/, /ˈsɔrɾə/ | *It's kinda cold.* |
| a lot of / out of | **lotta / outta** | | *outta here* |
| let me / give me | **lemme / gimme** | | *Lemme see.* |
| don't know | **dunno** | /dəˈnoʊ/ | |
| should / could / would / must have | **shoulda / coulda / woulda / musta** | /ˈʃʊdə/ etc. | the real form is *should **have***, never "should of" |
| what are you | **whatcha** | | *Whatcha doing?* |
| what do you | **whaddaya** | | *Whaddaya want?* |
| how did you | **howdja** | | |
| because | **'cause / cuz** | /kəz/ | |
| them | **'em** | /əm/ | *Tell 'em.* |
| you all | **y'all** | | southern US |
| isn't it | **innit** | | British, informal |
| cup of (tea) | **cuppa** | | British |

**Written reminder:** never write *should of* / *would of* / *could of*. They sound like *should've* but the correct written form is *should have* / *should've*. Even native speakers make this spelling error, but it is still an error.

### 2.9 Other connected-speech features worth knowing
| Feature | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Flapping (US)** | /t, d/ between vowels (second unstressed) → quick /ɾ/ | *water, better, get up, what a day* |
| **Glottal stop** | /t/ replaced by a catch in the throat (UK widely; US before /n/) | UK *bottle* [ˈbɒʔl]; US/UK *button* [ˈbʌʔn̩] |
| **Syllabic consonants** | /l, n, m/ act as a syllable with no vowel | *little* /ˈlɪtl̩/, *button*, *rhythm* |
| **Unreleased stops** | final /p, t, k/ held without a puff | *stop that* |
| **Nasal release** | stop released through the nose | *sudden, hidden* |
| **Weakening of "t" in -nt-** | *twenty* → "twenny"; *Internet* → "innernet" (US casual) | |

### 2.10 How a whole sentence changes
Careful: **"What are you going to do when you get to the airport?"**
Natural: **"Whaddaya gonna do when ya get to the airport?"**
→ /ˈwʌɾəjə ˈɡənə ˈduː wɛn jə ˈɡɛt tə ði ˈɛrpɔːrt/

| Chunk | What happened |
|---|---|
| *What are you* → "whaddaya" | linking + flap + weak *are* + weak *you* |
| *going to* → "gonna" | reduction |
| *when you* → "wen-ya" | weak *you* |
| *get to* → "get-ta" | weak *to* |
| *to the airport* → "ta-thi-air-port" | weak *to*, *the* → /ði/ before a vowel, link |

## 3. Why Native Speakers Do This
1. **Rhythm demands it** (Lesson 66): fitting unstressed syllables between the beats requires them to be short and joined.
2. **Muscular economy:** the articulators glide instead of resetting.
3. **Predictability:** function words are highly predictable from context, so speakers can reduce them without losing meaning. This is information-theory in action: **the more predictable, the more reduced.**
4. **Chunk storage:** frequent phrases (*I don't know, what do you want, going to*) are stored as units, so they are pronounced as units.
5. **Social signaling:** reduced speech sounds relaxed and friendly; fully articulated speech sounds formal, careful, or distant.
6. **History:** many written forms (*Christmas, sandwich*) are fossilized reductions.
7. **Rule-governed, not lazy:** every process above is systematic, and native speakers acquire it as children without being taught.

## 4. When to Use / When Not To

**Use (produce) connected speech when**
- chatting with friends, colleagues, neighbors;
- in casual workplace talk, meetings, calls;
- you want to sound fluent and natural;
- you're reading a rhythmic sentence aloud naturally.

**Use fuller, clearer forms when**
- giving names, numbers, addresses, passwords, instructions;
- speaking on a bad line or in noise;
- addressing someone who is struggling to follow;
- formal presentations, ceremonies, legal or academic oral exams (reduced but not extreme);
- you need to stress a particular word.

**In writing**
- Contractions (*I'm, don't*) are fine in emails, messages, and informal writing.
- *gonna, wanna, gotta, kinda, dunno, lemme* belong in texts, dialogue, and lyrics, not in reports, cover letters, academic papers, or formal emails.
- Never write *should of*.

**Production priorities for you**
1. Weak forms of the 10 most frequent function words (*a, the, to, of, and, for, can, have, you, was*).
2. Consonant-vowel linking (remove the extra vowel).
3. *gonna, wanna, gotta* in casual speech.
4. Coalescence in *did you / don't you / nice to meet you*.
5. Everything else: **recognize**, don't force.

## 5. Frequency & Register

| Feature | In casual speech | Careful/formal speech | Writing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak forms (*to, of, a, and*) | nearly 100% | very high | not written (spelling unchanged) |
| Contractions (*I'm, don't*) | very high | high | fine in informal/neutral |
| C+V linking | constant | constant | n/a |
| Glides (/j/, /w/) | constant | constant | n/a |
| Coalescence (*didja*) | very common | less common | n/a |
| *gonna, wanna* | very common | occasional | informal only |
| *gotta, kinda, sorta, lemme, gimme, dunno* | common | rare | informal only, dialogue |
| Assimilation (*tem bikes*) | common | occasional | n/a |
| Elision (*nexday*) | very common | common | n/a |

Rule of thumb: **the more casual and fast, the more compression; the more important the information, the clearer.**

## 6. American vs. British Notes

| Feature | US | UK |
|---|---|---|
| /t/ between vowels | flap: *water* "wawder" | clear [t] or glottal stop in many accents |
| /r/ linking | none needed (rhotic) | linking and intrusive /r/ (*law-r-and order*) |
| *Tuesday, news, student* | /ˈtuːzdeɪ, nuːz/ (yod dropped) | /ˈtjuːzdeɪ/ or /ˈtʃuːzdeɪ/ (yod kept or coalesced) |
| *t* before /n/ | glottal in *button, written* | glottal in *button, written* |
| Casual reduced forms | *gonna, wanna, gotta, gotcha, lemme, whaddaya, y'all* | *gonna, wanna, innit, dunno, cuppa, wotcha, gerroff, d'you* |
| Glottal stop frequency | lower | higher, especially in London, Estuary English |
| "Dark l" / vocalized l | less | *milk* sounding like "miwk" in some UK accents |

Everything in this lesson works in both varieties. When you listen, expect the *flap* in the US and *glottal stops* in many UK accents; the rest is shared.

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

### 7.1 Production
| ❌ Habit | What happens | ✅ Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Pausing between every word | choppy, hard to follow | say each thought group as one unit |
| Adding a vowel after a final consonant ("pick-**uh** it-**uh** up") | blocks the link | end the consonant and move it to the next vowel |
| Full vowels in *to, of, and, for, the* | sounds stressed and slow | /tə, əv, ən, fər, ðə/ |
| Adding /e/ before *s*-clusters ("**es**chool," "**es**tudent") across word boundaries (*in school* → "in eschool") | extra syllable | *in-SKOOL*, link the /n/ to /s/ |
| Dropping or weakening final consonants (*bed*, *dog*, *work*) to "sound fast" | word identity lost | keep final consonants; **link** them instead of dropping them |
| Using a glottal catch between vowels (*go / on*) | broken flow | slide with /w/ or /j/ |
| Saying *wanna to / gonna to* | ungrammatical | *wanna go, gonna call* |
| Using *gonna* as a movement verb (*I'm gonna the store*) | wrong | *I'm going to the store* |
| Writing "should of" | spelling error | *should have / should've* |
| Using *gonna, wanna, dunno* in formal contexts | register mismatch | full forms in formal speech and writing |
| Over-reducing, trying to imitate rapid slang | unnatural, unclear | reduce the function words, keep the content words clear |
| Mispronouncing *did you* as "did-you" with a hard break | sounds careful and stiff | "didja" is fine in casual speech |

### 7.2 Listening (the biggest payoff)
| What you hear | What it is | Why you missed it |
|---|---|---|
| "Whaddaya want?" | What do you want? | linking + weak *do* and *you* |
| "Jeet?" | Did you eat? | coalescence + elision |
| "I dunno." | I don't know. | reduction |
| "Whatcha doin'?" | What are you doing? | weak words fused |
| "I'd've gone." | I would have gone. | double contraction, /əv/ |
| "He's gotta go." | He has got to go. | reduced *got to* |
| "Kinda tired." | Kind of tired. | reduced *of* |
| "Lemme check." | Let me check. | assimilation + elision |
| "Cuppa tea?" | A cup of tea? | linking + weak *of* |
| "A pair o' shoes" | a pair of shoes | /əv/ barely audible |

You can **understand** these only if you've **heard** them as a chunk. Studying them by dictation is the quickest fix (see Section 9).

### 7.3 Attitude traps
- **"I will speak carefully so I'm clear."** Over-careful speech is, ironically, harder for natives to follow because it breaks their rhythm expectations.
- **"Natives swallow words."** No: they follow rules. Once you know the rules, you hear the words.
- **"If I reduce, I'll sound uneducated."** Educated speakers of all kinds reduce constantly. What marks register is *which* reductions and *where*.

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ **"Reduced speech is slang or incorrect."** — Weak forms and linking are the standard pronunciation of every educated speaker.
- ❌ **"Native speakers are lazy."** — Every process is regular, learned in childhood, and required by the rhythm.
- ❌ **"The spelling shows how words sound."** — English spelling hides connected speech completely (*of* is /əv/; *did you* is "didja").
- ❌ **"I must reduce everything to sound native."** — Over-reduction is as odd as none. Focus on function words and a few high-frequency chunks.
- ❌ **"Linking means adding sounds."** — Linking *reconnects existing sounds*. Glides (/j/, /w/) are the only small additions.
- ❌ **"Gonna is a different word."** — It is the same *going to*, pronounced fast, in the future use.
- ❌ **"I can write 'should of' because that's what it sounds like."** — It sounds like *should've*. The written form is *should have*.
- ❌ **"Fast speech is the problem."** — Often the speed is normal; the issue is that you're listening for full forms.
- ❌ **"Connected speech only matters for listening."** — Producing weak forms and links makes **you** easier to follow, because it restores the rhythm.
- ❌ **"Spanish doesn't link words."** — It does (*los amigos* → "lo-sa-mi-gos"). The skill transfers.
- ❌ **"British English doesn't do this."** — It does it just as much, with different details (glottal stops, intrusive /r/).
- ❌ **"Assimilation (*ten bikes* → *tem bikes*) is an error."** — It is a normal casual variant. You may produce or simply recognize it.

## 9. Pronunciation Note: Training Method

### 9.1 The five-step routine (10–15 min/day)
1. **Dictate.** Listen to a 10–15 second clip of natural speech. Write what you think you hear. Don't look at a transcript yet.
2. **Compare.** Check the transcript. Underline every spot where you missed or mis-wrote words.
3. **Respell.** Write those spots as they sound ("whaddaya," "jeet," "gonna," "pi-ki-tup").
4. **Chunk and build backwards.** Say the phrase from the end: *…airport* → *to the airport* → *get to the airport* → *when you get to the airport* → full sentence. Backward build-up keeps the natural melody and the weak forms.
5. **Shadow and record.** Repeat with the speaker, then record yourself and compare.

### 9.2 Linking drill: "slide, don't stop"
Say **pick it up** with your finger sliding along a table in one motion: "pi-ki-tup." Then slow down but keep the slide. Do the same with *turn it off, check it out, not at all, what is it*.

### 9.3 Glide drill
Say each pair twice, feeling the lips: **see it** (spread → "y"), **go on** (round → "w"), **my own** ("y"), **how are** ("w"), **the end** ("y"), **do it** ("w").

### 9.4 Weak-form drill (tap on the capitals)
- a **CUP** of **TEA** → "a-CUP-əv-TEA"
- **FISH** and **CHIPS** → "FISH-n-CHIPS"
- I'm **WAIT**ing for **YOU**. → "I'm WAITing fər YOU"
- She **CAN** swim, but she **CAN'T** dive. (here *can* is stressed for contrast, so it takes the strong form /kæn/)
Keep the beat from Lesson 66 steady and shrink the lowercase words.

### 9.5 Coalescence mini-drills
Say quickly, five times each:
**Did you eat?** → "Jeet?"
**Don't you know?** → "Doncha know?"
**Would you mind?** → "Wouldja mind?"
**Nice to meet you.** → "Nice to meechu."
**See you tomorrow.** → "See ya tomorrow."

### 9.6 Reading with glue
Take a paragraph. Mark each link with ‿ , each weak function word with a small circle or lowercase, each beat with CAPS. Read it aloud so the link marks are connected.

> Can I **HAVE**‿a **CUP**‿of **TEA**‿if you've **GOT**‿any?

### 9.7 Dictation journal
Keep a notebook with three columns: **What I heard** | **What was said** | **Why** (linking, weak form, coalescence...). Five new entries a week; review them with spaced repetition (days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30).

### 9.8 Shadowing
Choose 30–60 seconds of natural conversation (podcast, interview, sitcom). Shadow with subtitles off, then on. Notice each place where the speaker's words merge.

### 9.9 Self-monitoring
Record yourself saying 10 sentences. Check: did I add vowels after final consonants? Did I pause between words? Did I use weak forms for *to, of, and, a*?

### 9.10 Trouble spots
- Pausing before vowel-initial words (*an / apple*).
- Pronouncing *to* /tu/ everywhere.
- "Pick-uh it-uh up" (vowel added).
- Overdoing reduction in names, numbers, instructions.
- Treating *gonna / wanna* as full words with *to* after them.

## 10. Memory Technique

### 10.1 Glue and Lego
In the dictionary, words are Lego bricks. In speech, they are glued into chunks. Practice with chunks, not bricks.

### 10.2 "Consonant hops, vowel catches"
A final consonant hops onto the next word's vowel. If the next word starts with a consonant, nothing hops.

### 10.3 Lips decide the glide
Spread lips → "y". Round lips → "w". Check in the mirror.

### 10.4 Rule of predictability
The more predictable a word is, the more it shrinks. *Of* in *cup of tea* is very predictable → /əv/. *Tea* is new information → full.

### 10.5 Respelling notebook
Write what you hear, not what is spelled: *Whaddaya, jeet, dunno, gonna, coulda, cuppa*. Your eyes will learn the sound.

### 10.6 You already do this in Spanish
Say *los amigos* → "lo-sa-mi-gos." That is exactly the same skill. Say it, then apply it to *pick it up*.

### 10.7 Spaced retrieval cards
- *What do you do with a final consonant before a vowel?* → **link it to the next vowel**
- *Glide for "go on"?* → **/w/ (lips rounded)**
- *Glide for "see it"?* → **/j/ (lips spread)**
- *"Did you" in fast speech?* → **"didja" /ˈdɪdʒə/**
- *Weak form of "of"?* → **/əv/**
- *Correct written form of "shoulda"?* → **should have / should've (never "should of")**
- *What is a strong form needed for?* → **end of clause, contrast, emphasis**
- *"Gonna" can replace...?* → **"going to" + verb (future), not movement**
Review on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30.

### 10.8 One-week challenge
Choose five chunks (e.g., *whaddaya, gonna, didja, kinda, pick it up*). Use each ten times this week in speaking, and spot them ten times in listening.

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1: C + V → link.** The consonant joins the next syllable.

> **Pattern 2: V + V → glide.** /j/ after spread vowels; /w/ after rounded vowels.

> **Pattern 3: C + C + C → the middle /t/ or /d/ may vanish.**

> **Pattern 4: /t, d, s, z/ + you → /tʃ, dʒ, ʃ, ʒ/.**

> **Pattern 5: Function words → weak forms (schwa) unless stressed, final, or contrasted.**

> **Pattern 6: Frequent combinations → fused forms (*gonna, wanna, gotta, kinda, dunno*).**

> **Pattern 7: Predictable → short; informative → clear.**

> **Pattern 8: Unstressed *h* in pronouns/auxiliaries disappears** (*tell 'im, give 'er, I'd've*).

> **Pattern 9: US /t/ between vowels → flap; UK → clear /t/ or glottal stop.**

> **Pattern 10: The faster and more casual, the more compression; the more important the word, the clearer.**

> **Pattern 11: Written forms stay the same.** Only the pronunciation changes (except in informal spellings).

> **Pattern 12: Contractions (*I'm, don't*) are written weak forms.**

> **Connections:** Lesson 64 (schwa, flap), Lesson 65 (weak forms, stress), Lesson 66 (rhythm), Lesson 60 (fillers: *y'know, kinda*), Lesson 62 (small talk: *How ya doin'?*), Lesson 68 (intonation), Phase 8 (listening).

## 12. Real-Life Examples

### 12.1 From careful to natural
| Careful | Natural (respelled) |
|---|---|
| What do you want to do? | Whaddaya wanna do? |
| Did you eat yet? | Jeet yet? |
| I don't know. | I dunno. |
| Let me see. | Lemme see. |
| I'm going to have to go. | I'm gonna hafta go. |
| Give me a minute. | Gimme a minute. |
| Would you like some more? | Wouldja like some more? |
| I could have told you. | I coulda told ya. |
| It's kind of expensive. | It's kinda expensive. |
| Nice to meet you. | Nice to meechu. |
| How are you doing? | How ya doin'? |
| Out of the question | Outta the question |
| What are you looking at? | Whatcha lookin' at? |
| A cup of tea | A cuppa tea |
| Tell them to wait. | Tell 'em to wait. |
| I would have gone if I had known. | I'd've gone if I'd known. |

### 12.2 Mini-dialogues (natural speech, written with respelling)
**1. Café**
> **A:** Whaddaya wanna get?
> **B:** I dunno. Maybe a cuppa coffee. You?
> **A:** I'm kinda hungry. Lemme check the menu.

**2. Plans**
> **A:** Wanna grab lunch?
> **B:** Sure! Where ya wanna go?
> **A:** I dunno, I'm easy. Anywhere's fine.

**3. At work**
> **A:** Didja send the report?
> **B:** Not yet. I'm gonna do it after lunch.
> **A:** Cool. Lemme know when it's done.

**4. Phone**
> **A:** Hey, whatcha up to?
> **B:** Not much. Just gotta finish this email.
> **A:** Okay, call me when ya get a chance.

**5. Missed it**
> **A:** I shoulda called you yesterday.
> **B:** It's okay. I coulda called you, too.

### 12.3 One long sentence, analyzed
> "I would have told you if I had known you were going to be there."
> Natural: /aɪd əv ˈtoʊldʒə ɪf aɪd ˈnoʊn jə wər ˈɡənə bi ðɛr/
> "I'd've toldja if I'd known ya were gonna be there."

### 12.4 Same words, different gluing (juncture)
| Phrase A | Phrase B |
|---|---|
| a nice house | an ice house |
| I scream | ice cream |
| that's tough | that stuff |
| a name | an aim |
| some others | so mothers |

Context and the exact timing of the consonants separate these; connected speech makes them close, and that is why context matters so much for listening.

### 12.5 Reading passage (with natural-speech spellings)
> "So I was walkin' to work yesterday, and I realized I'd left my phone at home. I was kinda annoyed, 'cause I had a meeting at nine. I couldn't just go back, so I borrowed a coworker's phone and called my wife. She said, 'Whaddaya want me to do?' I said, 'Could ya bring it to the office?' She was gonna be late, but she did it anyway. I owe her one!"

## 13. Collocations & Related Expressions

**Vocabulary**
- *connected speech, linking, elision, assimilation, coalescence, reduction, contraction, weak form, strong form, schwa, flap, glottal stop, juncture, resyllabification*
- *run words together, slur, swallow a sound, drop a sound, clip a word, blend, fuse, merge, glide, link*
- *sound fluent / natural / smooth / casual / formal / careful / clipped / mumbled*
- *a reduced form, a contracted form, a casual pronunciation, a spoken spelling, a fast-speech form*
- *catch (what someone said), make out (words), pick up (on sounds), tune in, get the gist*

**Useful expressions for real life**
- *Sorry, could you say that again?*
- *Sorry, I didn't catch that.*
- *Could you slow down a little?*
- *Did you say "can" or "can't"?*
- *I missed the last part.*
- *Do you mean...?*
- *What was that last word?*
- *Sorry, the line is bad. Could you repeat that?*

**Contrast groups**
| Items | Difference |
|---|---|
| *linking / elision / assimilation* | joining sounds / dropping sounds / changing sounds |
| *contraction / reduction* | written short form (*I'm*) / spoken weakening (even without writing) |
| *weak form / strong form* | unstressed pronunciation / stressed or final pronunciation |
| *slang / informal / reduced* | special vocabulary / casual register / pronunciation shortcut |
| *catch / get / understand* | hear the words / grasp the meaning / fully comprehend |

## 17. Speaking Practice

**Drill 1 — Slide, don't stop.** Say 10 linked phrases (*pick it up, turn it off, check it out, look at it, not at all...*) with a finger sliding across the table.

**Drill 2 — Glide mirror.** Using a mirror, say *see it, go on, my own, how are, do it, the end*. Check lips: spread = "y", round = "w".

**Drill 3 — Coalescence chain.** Repeat ten times: *Did you eat? / Don't you know? / Would you help me? / Nice to meet you. / See you later.*

**Drill 4 — Weak-form sentences.** Say these keeping beats on the capitals: *I want to GO to the STORE. / She can COME and STAY for a WEEK. / A CUP of TEA and a PIECE of CAKE.*

**Drill 5 — Careful to casual.** Take 10 sentences from Section 12.1 and say each first in careful form, then in natural form.

**Drill 6 — Backward build-up.** Choose three long sentences from a podcast transcript. Build each from the end; then say it at natural speed.

**Drill 7 — Shadow.** Shadow 60 seconds of a conversation, matching the glue. Mark the places where you couldn't keep up.

**Drill 8 — Role play.** With a partner, perform the dialogues in Section 12.2 in natural speech; then do them again in a formal style and notice the difference.

**Drill 9 — Clarity switch.** Say a sentence in casual style, then repeat it clearly for someone who didn't hear: *Whaddaya wanna do? → What do you want to do?*

## 18. Writing Practice
1. **Respelling log:** each day, write 5 phrases you heard in speech with a natural-sound spelling and the standard form.
2. **Two registers (120 words):** write the same message twice, once to a friend (with contractions and informal forms) and once to a manager (formal).
3. **Dialogue (12 lines):** write a casual conversation in which at least ten reduced forms appear, with a standard-English translation underneath.
4. **Explain (150 words):** explain to a Spanish-speaking friend why English words "run together" and how Spanish does the same thing.
5. **Listening reflection (150 words):** describe a moment this week when you didn't understand something, what the actual words were, and what process (linking, weak form, coalescence) hid them.
6. **Rule summary:** make a one-page cheat sheet with the five processes and three examples each, in your own words.
7. **Correct the text:** take a short informal message and rewrite it for a formal audience, replacing *gonna, wanna, gotta, kinda* and contractions where needed.
8. **Weekly goal:** write three sentences about what you will practice next week and how you will measure it.

**Mini Quiz**
1. A final consonant sound joins the vowel that starts the next word: *pick it up* → "pi-ki-tup."
2. /w/, because /oʊ/ has rounded lips.
3. /j/, because /i/ has spread lips.
4. A sound disappears: *next day* → "nexday" (the /t/ is lost).
5. /t, d, s, z/ + *you* merge into /tʃ, dʒ, ʃ, ʒ/: *did you* → "didja."
6. *to* /tə/; *of* /əv/.
7. At the end of a clause (*Where are you from?*), for contrast or emphasis, when quoted or isolated.
8. *Going to* + verb (future); not when *going* means movement (*I'm going to the store*).
9. *Should've* sounds like "should of," but the real words are *should have*.
10. It breaks the link, makes you sound choppy, and adds an extra syllable.
11. British English and other non-rhotic accents.
12. A quick tap of the tongue for /t/ or /d/ between vowels; American English (*water, better*).
13. Function words are more predictable, so reducing them loses little information, and it keeps the rhythm.
14. Any two: dictation, shadowing, respelling log, backward build-up, one-week chunk challenge.
