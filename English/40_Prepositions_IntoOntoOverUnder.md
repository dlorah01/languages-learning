# Lesson 40 — INTO / ONTO / OVER / UNDER / ABOVE / BELOW
### Phase 3: Prepositions | Lesson 4 of 7

---

## 0. Where this fits
This lesson covers two related pairs: the static/dynamic distinction (in/on vs. into/onto) and the vertical relationship prepositions (over/under, above/below) — genuinely useful additions to Lesson 38's IN/ON/AT foundation, with a particularly high-value grammatical rule around when "into/onto" are required versus when plain "in/on" suffice.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation

- **INTO / ONTO** = the **dynamic (movement)** versions of "in/on" — describing motion resulting in containment or contact, not just the resulting static state.
- **OVER / ABOVE** = higher than something, but with different implications: "over" often implies covering, spanning, or crossing; "above" is more purely about vertical position with no implication of contact/covering.
- **UNDER / BELOW** = the vertical opposites, with a parallel distinction: "under" often implies being covered/beneath something with potential contact; "below" is more purely about a lower vertical position, often on a scale.

---

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2a. INTO vs. IN — Static vs. Dynamic
- **IN** = the resulting static state (already inside): "The keys are in the box."
- **INTO** = the movement/action resulting in that state (entering): "She put the keys into the box." / "He walked into the room."
**Key rule:** use "into" specifically when describing the movement/transition itself, not merely the final location. Compare: "The cat is in the box" (static — no movement described) vs. "The cat jumped into the box" (dynamic — describing the movement that resulted in containment).

### 2b. ONTO vs. ON — Static vs. Dynamic
- **ON** = the resulting static state (already on the surface): "The book is on the table."
- **ONTO** = the movement/action resulting in that state: "She put the book onto the table." / "The cat jumped onto the table."
**Note:** in casual speech, "on" is often used even for dynamic movement where "onto" would be more technically precise ("she put the book on the table" is extremely common and fully acceptable, even though the action is dynamic) — "onto" is somewhat more emphasized/precise, especially for describing an action of climbing/jumping/moving upward onto something.

### 2c. OVER vs. ABOVE — Two Kinds of "Higher"
- **OVER** implies: directly above with potential contact/covering, or spanning/crossing something: "She put a blanket over the baby." (covering) / "The bridge goes over the river." (spanning/crossing) / "The plane flew over the city." (passing above, often close/covering the area)
- **ABOVE** implies: a purely vertical position, higher than something, without contact/covering, often used more for abstract scales/measurements: "The temperature is above freezing." / "Her score was above average." / "The painting hangs above the fireplace." (higher position, no contact/covering implied, unlike "over the fireplace," which might suggest closer proximity or even resting against it)

### 2d. UNDER vs. BELOW — Two Kinds of "Lower"
- **UNDER** implies: directly beneath, often with potential contact or covering: "The cat is under the table." / "She hid the letter under her pillow."
- **BELOW** implies: a purely vertical position, lower than something, often on a scale, without necessarily being directly beneath in a covering sense: "The temperature is below freezing." / "His score was below average." / "The valley is below the mountain." (a lower position, more about relative vertical placement on a broader scale than direct physical coverage)

---

## 3. The Key Diagnostic: Movement or State? Contact/Covering or Pure Position?
- **INTO/ONTO vs. IN/ON:** is the sentence describing the *movement* that results in a location, or just the *resulting static location itself*? Movement → into/onto. Static state → in/on.
- **OVER/UNDER vs. ABOVE/BELOW:** does the relationship imply potential contact, covering, or spanning? → over/under. Is it a purer vertical position, often on a scale or without contact? → above/below.

---

## 4. Why Native Speakers Maintain These Distinctions
The static/dynamic in/into and on/onto distinction lets English clearly separate "where something ends up" from "the process of it getting there" — a genuinely useful precision, especially in narrative/descriptive writing. The over/above and under/below distinctions let English communicate not just relative vertical position but also whether contact/covering/spanning is implied — additional spatial precision that Spanish's *sobre* and *debajo de* don't always split as cleanly.

---

## 5. When Each Matters Most
- **Into/onto** — valuable in narrative and descriptive writing/speech where the movement itself is the focus (a common feature of vivid storytelling).
- **Over/above and under/below** — valuable for precise physical descriptions and, importantly, for abstract scale comparisons (above/below average, temperature above/below freezing) — a genuinely high-frequency abstract use.

---

## 6. Frequency & Register
"In/on" remain more frequent overall in casual speech even for dynamic movement (as noted in Section 2b); "into/onto" appear more consistently in more careful/precise speech and writing, and are essential in certain fixed idiomatic verbs (get into trouble, run into someone, log into an account — many of these will resurface as phrasal verb-adjacent expressions in Phase 4). "Above/below" appear especially frequently in formal, scientific, and statistical contexts (above/below average, above/below sea level).

---

## 7. American vs. British Note
No major systematic differences in these core distinctions between American and British English.

## 8. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

| Mistake | Why | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "She jumped in the pool." (intending the dynamic movement of jumping into the water) | Spanish *saltó en la piscina* doesn't require this static/dynamic preposition distinction as strictly | "She jumped into the pool." (though "in" is also commonly heard in casual speech, "into" is more precise for the movement itself) |
| "The temperature is over freezing." (intending a scale comparison, not physical covering) | Confusing "over" (contact/covering/spanning) with "above" (pure scale position) | "The temperature is above freezing." |
| "He hid over the bed." (intending "under," a directional confusion) | Simple over/under confusion, sometimes from rushed translation | "He hid under the bed." |
| "Her score was under average." (intending a scale comparison, using "under" instead of the more natural "below" for this abstract context) | Using "under" (contact/covering sense) where "below" (pure scale) is more natural | "Her score was below average." (though "under" is also understandable and sometimes used, "below" is the more precise, natural choice for scale comparisons) |

---

## 9. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ "Into/onto are always mandatory whenever movement is described." — As Section 2b notes, casual speech frequently uses plain "in/on" even for dynamic movement ("put it on the table," not strictly requiring "onto") — into/onto add precision but aren't always obligatory.
- ❌ "Over and above are always interchangeable, and so are under and below." — Section 2c-2d show a genuine distinction (contact/covering/spanning vs. pure vertical position/scale), even though there's some overlap flexibility in certain contexts.

---

## 10. Pronunciation Note
"Into" and "onto" are typically pronounced as fused, single-stress units in natural speech ("IN-too," "ON-too") rather than as two separately-stressed words — worth practicing as unified chunks (per Lesson 3's method) rather than "in" + "to" pronounced separately.

---

## 11. Memory Technique
**The "movement vs. snapshot" test** for into/onto vs. in/on: does the sentence describe a movement/transition (a video, per Lesson 6's aspect imagery) or a static resulting state (a photo)? Movement → into/onto. Static photo → in/on.

**For over/above and under/below:** ask "does this imply touching/covering/spanning, or just relative vertical position on a scale?" Touching/covering/spanning → over/under. Pure position/scale → above/below.

---

## 12. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern:** [in/on] = static resulting state (photo). [into/onto] = the movement/transition itself (video) — directly extending Lesson 6's aspect imagery (simple = photo, continuous = video) to this preposition pair.

> **Pattern:** [over/under] = implies contact, covering, or spanning. [above/below] = implies pure vertical position, often on a scale, without necessarily implying contact.

---

## 13. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1:**
- "The ball rolled into the hole." (movement)
- "The ball is in the hole." (static state)

**Level 2 — over vs. above, contact vs. pure position:**
- "She put a jacket over her shoulders." (covering/contact)
- "The plane flew above the clouds." (pure vertical position, no contact/covering implied)

**Level 3 — under vs. below, same distinction:**
- "The dog is hiding under the porch." (covering/contact — beneath a physical structure)
- "The temperature dropped below zero." (pure scale position, no physical covering)

**Level 4 — combining all six prepositions correctly in one sophisticated, natural passage:**
- "The cat jumped onto the shelf, knocked a book into the trash can below, and then climbed above the bookcase, safely out of reach — while the dog, still confused, kept sniffing under the couch where the toy had rolled." (onto = dynamic surface contact; into = dynamic containment; below = pure vertical position; above = pure vertical position; under = covering/contact)
