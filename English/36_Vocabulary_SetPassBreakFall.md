# Lesson 36 — SET / PASS / BREAK / FALL
### Phase 2: High-Frequency Verb System | Lesson 8 of 8 (Final Lesson of Phase 2)

---

## 0. Where this fits
This closes Phase 2's eight-lesson high-frequency verb arc. These four verbs round out the set with genuinely useful figurative extensions: SET (establishing/fixing something in place), PASS (moving by/transferring/succeeding), BREAK (separating/interrupting/violating), and FALL (descending, but also a specific "become" niche, extending Lesson 35's "become" reference chart).

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation

- **SET** = establishing/fixing something into a particular state or position, often with a sense of finality or intentionality.
- **PASS** = moving by, transferring from one person/state to another, or succeeding at a test/threshold.
- **BREAK** = separating into pieces, interrupting continuity, or violating a rule/agreement.
- **FALL** = descending, but also — connecting directly to Lesson 35's "become" chart — a specific niche for falling into a state (fall asleep, fall in love, fall ill).

---

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2a. SET — Establish, Fix, Arrange
- **Literal:** "Set the table." (arrange) / "Set the box down." (place, similar to "put")
- **Fixed collocations:** set a goal/target, set a record, set an example, set a date, set a limit, set the pace, set someone free, set something in motion (start a process), set up (previewed here, fully covered as a phrasal verb in Phase 4)
- **Set + adjective (a narrow "become"-adjacent use):** "The jelly needs to set." (solidify — a narrow, specific physical/chemical usage, not a general "become" pattern like turn/run)

### 2b. PASS — Move By, Transfer, Succeed
- **Literal:** "The car passed us on the highway." (move by)
- **Transfer:** "Pass the salt, please." / "She passed the message on to her boss."
- **Succeed (at a test/threshold):** "I passed my exam." (contrast with "fail")
- **Time passing:** "Time passes quickly when you're busy."
- **Fixed collocations:** pass judgment, pass the time, pass away (a euphemism for die — culturally important, covered further in Phase 10's cultural pragmatics), pass out (faint — previewed here, fully covered as a phrasal verb in Phase 4)

### 2c. BREAK — Separate, Interrupt, Violate
- **Literal:** "He broke the glass." (separate into pieces)
- **Interrupt:** "Let's take a break." (Lesson 30 callback — this specific collocation) / "The news broke the silence."
- **Violate:** "break a rule," "break a promise" (note the contrast with Lesson 34's "keep a promise" — break is the direct opposite), "break the law"
- **Fixed collocations:** break the ice (ease social tension), break a record, break down (previewed here, fully covered as a phrasal verb in Phase 4 — both literally, for machines, and figuratively, for emotional collapse), break even (financially, neither profit nor loss), break news (announce, often as "breaking news")

### 2d. FALL — Descend, and a Specific "Become" Niche
- **Literal:** "She fell down the stairs."
- **Become/enter a state (a specific, fixed niche, extending Lesson 35's chart):** fall asleep, fall in love, fall ill, fall silent, fall behind (in progress/schedule)
- **Fixed collocations:** fall apart (literally or emotionally/structurally), fall through (a plan failing to happen — previewed here, fully covered as a phrasal verb in Phase 4), fall for something (be deceived by, or romantically fall for someone)

---

## 3. Extending Lesson 35's "Become" Chart — FALL Joins the Niche List
Lesson 35 established get (general default), turn (color/weather/age), and run (depletion) as the three main "become" categories. FALL adds a fourth specific niche: **entering a state, often suddenly or completely** — fall asleep, fall ill, fall silent, fall in love. This pattern typically implies a more sudden or complete transition than "get" would suggest: "I fell asleep" (sudden, complete) vs. "I'm getting sleepy" (gradual, ongoing) — genuinely different nuances, not just stylistic variants.

---

## 4. Why Native Speakers Maintain These Specific Niches
As with Lesson 35's turn/run niches, English's "become" vocabulary crystallized around specific verbs for specific semantic categories (sudden/complete states → fall; gradual depletion → run; color/weather/age → turn), likely for historical/idiomatic reasons rather than a single derivable logical system — reinforcing this entire phase's central lesson: high-frequency verb meaning is stored and retrieved as fixed chunks (Lesson 3), with a general default pattern plus specific memorized exceptions layered on top.

---

## 5. When Each Matters Most
- **Set (goals, records, examples)** — common in professional, motivational, and achievement-related contexts.
- **Pass (exams, time, judgment)** — everyday academic/evaluative contexts, plus the important "pass away" euphemism for sensitive contexts.
- **Break (rules, promises, records)** — common in describing violations, negative outcomes, and achievements (breaking records).
- **Fall (asleep, in love, ill)** — extremely common in everyday descriptions of sudden state changes.

---

## 6. Frequency & Register
All four verbs and their collocations are used across every register. "Pass away" specifically is a register-sensitive euphemism — more appropriate/gentle than the blunt "die" in most social contexts, worth flagging for its cultural/pragmatic sensitivity (expanded further in Phase 10).

---

## 7. American vs. British Note
No major systematic differences in these core meanings and collocations between American and British English.

---

## 8. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

| Mistake | Why | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "I need to establish a goal for this year." (using an overly formal/literal translation instead of the natural collocation) | Direct translation from *establecer una meta* | "I need to set a goal for this year." (the natural, high-frequency English collocation) |
| "I get asleep quickly." (using the general "get" pattern instead of the specific "fall" niche) | Not knowing the fixed "fall asleep" collocation | "I fall asleep quickly." |
| "He violated his promise." (technically understandable but not the natural, idiomatic choice) | Using an overly formal/literal verb instead of the natural collocation | "He broke his promise." (the natural, idiomatic collocation — "violated" sounds legally/formally stiff for this everyday context) |
| "She died last week" used in a context calling for more sensitivity/euphemism (e.g., speaking to a grieving family member) | Not knowing the culturally-preferred softer alternative | "She passed away last week." (a gentler, more socially appropriate phrasing in sensitive contexts) |

---

## 9. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ "Set, pass, break, and fall are only used in their most literal, physical senses." — As this lesson shows extensively, all four have significant figurative/idiomatic extensions that are at least as important as (often more frequent than) their literal meanings.
- ❌ "Fall asleep and get sleepy mean the same thing." — "Fall asleep" describes the sudden, complete transition into sleep itself; "get sleepy" describes the gradual, ongoing feeling of tiredness beforehand — genuinely different points in the same process.
- ❌ "Pass away is just a fancier way to say die, with no real functional difference." — It carries genuine pragmatic/emotional softening value in sensitive contexts (grief, condolences) — the choice between "die" and "pass away" is a meaningful register/sensitivity decision, not an arbitrary stylistic swap.

---

## 10. Pronunciation Note
Fixed collocations in this lesson (break the ice, fall asleep, pass away) are best learned and produced as complete chunks with natural stress typically on the second element (the ICE, aSLEEP, aWAY) — worth practicing as full rhythmic units rather than word-by-word constructions.

## 11. Memory Technique
**Extend Lesson 35's three-column "Become" chart into a four-column chart:** GET (general default), TURN (color/weather/age), RUN (depletion), FALL (sudden/complete state entry: asleep, ill, silent, in love). This completes the "become" reference system across this entire phase, giving you one comprehensive, memorized structure covering the vast majority of "become" situations in English.

---

## 12. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern:** Fixed collocations continue to dominate high-frequency verb usage (per this entire phase's central theme) — SET a goal, PASS an exam, BREAK a promise, FALL asleep are not derivable from first principles; they must be learned and drilled as complete chunks (Lesson 3's method), the same strategy that has applied to every verb in this phase.

---

## 13. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1:**
- "Set the alarm for 7 AM." (arrange/fix)
- "I passed my driving test." (succeeded)

**Level 2 — break vs. keep, direct opposites (Lesson 34 callback):**
- "She always keeps her promises." vs. "He constantly breaks his promises." (direct antonym pair)

**Level 3 — fall for a specific, sudden state, contrasted with gradual "get":**
- "I fell ill right before the trip." (sudden, complete) vs. "I've been getting sicker all week." (gradual, ongoing)

**Level 4 — combining all four verbs correctly in one sophisticated, natural passage:**
- "After she broke her leg, doctors set a new goal for her recovery; she passed every milestone faster than expected, and by spring, she'd fallen back in love with running — something she never thought possible after the accident." (broke = literal injury, set a goal = established, passed = succeeded at, fallen back in love = entered a state again)
