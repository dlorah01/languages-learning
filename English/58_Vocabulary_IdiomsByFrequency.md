# Lesson 58 — Idioms by Frequency
### Phase 5: Vocabulary as a System | Module 8 of 8 (Phase capstone)

---

## 0. Where this fits
This is the last lesson of Phase 5. Lessons 51–57 built the vocabulary system: chunks, collocations, synonyms, register, academic and business English. **Idioms are the final layer**: chunks whose meaning is *not* the sum of their words.

Most idiom books give you a list of 500 expressions and no way to decide which ones matter. That is the wrong approach. An idiom you will never hear costs the same memory space as one you will hear every day. This lesson therefore sorts idioms **by frequency**, in three tiers:

| Tier | Name | What you do with it |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Must-know** (very common) | Understand instantly **and** use actively |
| **2** | **Nice-to-know** (common in specific contexts) | Understand easily; use a few that suit you |
| **3** | **Rare / literary** | Recognize when you meet them; don't study in advance |

> **Core idea:** you need to *understand* far more idioms than you *use*. A learner who uses 30 idioms perfectly sounds more natural than one who uses 150 awkwardly.

**Connections:** Lesson 51 (idioms are chunks), Lesson 55 (idioms are mostly informal–neutral register), Lesson 57 (business idioms), Phase 4 (many idioms are built on the same verbs as phrasal verbs). Phase 6 (Natural Spoken English) will put idioms into real conversation.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
An idiom is a phrase where **the picture is not the meaning**.

- *It's a piece of cake.* You are not talking about cake. You mean "it's very easy."
- *He kicked the bucket.* No bucket was involved. It means "he died."
- *Let's call it a day.* Nobody is naming a day. It means "let's stop working now."

Native speakers don't "translate" these; they store each as **one unit**, exactly like a single word. When you hear *call it a day*, you don't decode *call + it + a + day*. You retrieve one meaning. Your goal is the same: store idioms as whole chunks, with their meaning, register, and a typical situation.

Idioms are also **compressed stories or images**. *Bite the bullet* carries a mini-picture (a soldier enduring pain without anesthetic) and an attitude (accept something unpleasant with courage). That is why they are useful: one phrase brings a picture, a feeling, and a judgment at once.

You already use idioms in Spanish all the time: *costar un ojo de la cara*, *ser pan comido*, *meter la pata*, *tirar la toalla*. English works the same way. The difficulty is that **images rarely match** between languages, and a few look identical but are not.

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 What counts as an idiom?
Linguists use a spectrum, from fully transparent to fully opaque:

| Type | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Transparent** | Meaning guessable from the words | *keep an eye on, on the other hand, in the long run* |
| **Semi-transparent** | The image helps once you know the meaning | *hit the nail on the head, a blessing in disguise, break the ice* |
| **Opaque** | Meaning not recoverable from the words | *kick the bucket, spill the beans, red herring* |

Related categories (all are "fixed expressions"):
- **Idioms proper:** *it's raining cats and dogs, under the weather.*
- **Proverbs:** *Better late than never. Actions speak louder than words.*
- **Similes:** *as cool as a cucumber, as busy as a bee, like water off a duck's back.*
- **Binomials (fixed pairs):** *sooner or later, back and forth, odds and ends, safe and sound, black and white, give and take.*
- **Phrasal verbs with idiomatic meaning:** *give up, run out of* (Phase 4).
- **Discourse idioms:** *by the way, to be honest, to cut a long story short.*

### 2.2 How fixed are idioms?
Idioms are **partly flexible**. Three rules cover most cases.

1. **Tense and person can change.** *bite the bullet* → *He bit the bullet. We'll have to bite the bullet.*
2. **Possessive and pronoun slots change.** *lose one's temper* → *She lost her temper. I lost my temper.* In dictionaries, *one's* / *someone's* marks the slot.
3. **The core words usually cannot change.** *Hit the nail on the head* ≠ *hit the hammer on the nail.* *Spill the beans* ≠ *drop the beans.*

Some idioms resist the passive or other transformations:
- ✅ *The beans were spilled.* (works; some idioms keep meaning in the passive)
- ❌ *The bucket was kicked.* (loses the idiom)
- Speakers sometimes **play** with idioms for humor: *We have bigger fish to fry* → *We have bigger fish to fry, and they're in the freezer.* This is creative, not a pattern for learners.

### 2.3 Idioms by meaning area
The same few areas generate most English idioms. Grouping them by area is the best way to learn them.

| Area | Typical images |
|---|---|
| **Difficulty / ease** | *a piece of cake, a walk in the park, an uphill battle, a tough nut to crack* |
| **Effort** | *go the extra mile, burn the midnight oil, pull your weight, give it your best shot* |
| **Time** | *once in a blue moon, in the nick of time, around the clock, behind the times, sooner or later* |
| **Money** | *cost an arm and a leg, break the bank, make ends meet, a rip-off, on the house* |
| **Communication** | *beat around the bush, get to the point, read between the lines, spill the beans, drop a hint* |
| **Decisions / risk** | *sit on the fence, jump the gun, play it safe, take a gamble, burn your bridges* |
| **Success / failure** | *hit the jackpot, back to square one, fall flat, hit a wall, steal the show* |
| **Emotions** | *over the moon, down in the dumps, on edge, drive someone up the wall, get cold feet* |
| **Relationships** | *see eye to eye, bury the hatchet, hit it off, get on someone's nerves* |
| **Knowledge / attention** | *ring a bell, keep in mind, get the hang of, learn the ropes, in the dark* |
| **Problems / trouble** | *in hot water, a can of worms, the last straw, a blessing in disguise* |

### 2.5 How to decode an unfamiliar idiom (in 4 steps)
1. **Check the context.** What is the topic? Is the tone positive or negative?
2. **Picture the literal image.** *Burn bridges*: destroy the way back.
3. **Find the underlying metaphor.** Destroying the way back → ruin relationships so you can't return.
4. **Test it in the sentence.** *If you insult your boss, you're burning your bridges* → makes sense.

If the meaning is still unclear, ask: *"Sorry, what does '…' mean?"* This is normal and natural. Natives ask each other this too.

### 2.6 Where do idioms come from?
Knowing sources helps memory:
- **Sports:** *ballpark figure, hit a home run, touch base* (baseball, US); *sticky wicket, on the back foot* (cricket, UK); *the ball is in your court* (tennis); *throw in the towel* (boxing); *jump the gun* (races).
- **Sea and ships:** *learn the ropes, show someone the ropes, sail close to the wind, all hands on deck, a loose cannon, in the same boat, rock the boat*.
- **Animals:** *let the cat out of the bag, a wild goose chase, a red herring, the elephant in the room, the lion's share*.
- **Food:** *a piece of cake, spill the beans, in a nutshell, bring home the bacon, a hard nut to crack, the icing on the cake*.
- **Body:** *cost an arm and a leg, keep an eye on, cold feet, get something off your chest, pull someone's leg, give someone a hand*.
- **Weather:** *under the weather, a storm in a teacup, save for a rainy day, steal someone's thunder*.
- **Money and commerce:** *in the red/black, break the bank, cost a fortune, pay through the nose*.
- **Bible, Shakespeare, myths:** *a good Samaritan, the writing on the wall, Achilles' heel, Pandora's box, break the ice (Taming of the Shrew), wild goose chase (Romeo and Juliet)*.

## 3. Why Native Speakers Do This
1. **Economy:** an idiom packs a picture, an attitude, and a conclusion into 3–4 words.
2. **Shared culture:** using an idiom signals "we know the same things." It builds friendliness and belonging.
3. **Softening:** idioms are often less blunt than literal statements. *He's not the sharpest tool in the shed* is gentler (and funnier) than *He's stupid.* *We're not on the same page* is gentler than *You're wrong.*
4. **Vividness and humor:** idioms make speech more lively and memorable.
5. **Automaticity:** fluent speakers retrieve them as whole units, which saves processing time during real-time speech (Lesson 51).
6. **Convention:** often there is no neutral equivalent that "sounds right." *At the end of the day* has no short literal replacement with the same flavor.

But native speakers also **overestimate how often they use idioms**. Corpus studies show a typical speaker produces a few idioms per minute in casual speech, mostly from a small set of very common ones (Tier 1). The long tail of colorful idioms is rare.

## 4. When to Use / When Not To

**Use idioms when**
- the idiom is **Tier 1** and you're confident of its meaning, tone, and form;
- the context is **informal or neutral**: conversations, workplace chat, emails to colleagues, storytelling;
- you want to sound **warm, natural, or humorous**;
- you are **reporting** what others said, in their style;
- the listener shares your culture or is a native speaker.

**Avoid idioms when**
- you are **writing academically or formally** (reports, theses, legal text). Use precise literal language (Lesson 56);
- you are speaking to **non-native speakers** or in an international team. Idioms are a common cause of misunderstanding;
- you're **not sure** about the meaning, the register, or the grammar;
- the idiom is **clichéd** (*think outside the box, at the end of the day, win-win*) and used repeatedly; it can sound lazy;
- the topic is **serious or sensitive** (illness, death, grief): *kick the bucket* is inappropriate for a funeral;
- you would be **translating from Spanish**: the image may not exist in English;
- you are **mixing** idioms (*we'll burn that bridge when we come to it*): a classic error (see 8).

**Practical rule:** use at most **one idiom per minute of speech** and at most **one or two per page of writing** unless you're writing informally or creatively.

## 5. Frequency & Register

**Frequency (practical order of study)**
1. **Discourse idioms** (*by the way, to be honest, at the end of the day, on the other hand, sooner or later*).
2. **Everyday action/state idioms** (*call it a day, under the weather, no big deal, take it easy, make sense*).
3. **Work idioms** (*on the same page, back to square one, the ball is in your court, keep in the loop*).
4. **Problem and emotion idioms** (*bite the bullet, the last straw, over the moon, drive up the wall*).
5. **Colorful idioms** (Tier 2).
6. **Literary/allusive idioms** (Tier 3).

**Register**
| Register | Typical idioms |
|---|---|
| **Neutral** | *in the long run, keep in mind, on the other hand, make sense, take into account* |
| **Informal** | *piece of cake, hit the sack, drive me up the wall, no big deal, over the moon* |
| **Slang / very informal** | *chill out, freak out, hang out, bummer, kick back* |
| **Business-friendly** | *on the same page, ballpark figure, move the goalposts, touch base, the bottom line* |
| **Literary / elevated** | *Achilles' heel, Pandora's box, a Pyrrhic victory, a sea change* |

Most idioms live in the **informal-to-neutral** zone (levels 2–3 of Lesson 55). Very few belong in formal academic writing.

**Trend:** Sports and military metaphors (*a game-changer, a long shot, the front line*) are strong in business English. Social-media-era idioms (*spill the tea, ghost someone, cancel*) change fast; learn them by listening, not from lists.

## 6. American vs. British Notes

| Meaning | US | UK |
|---|---|---|
| a big fuss about nothing | *a tempest in a teapot* | *a storm in a teacup* |
| wasting effort | *beating a dead horse* | *flogging a dead horse* |
| cause problems | *throw a wrench in the works* | *throw a spanner in the works* |
| be the best | *take the cake* | *take the biscuit* |
| protect from bad luck | *knock on wood* | *touch wood* |
| easy | *a piece of cake; a walk in the park* | *a piece of cake; a doddle; easy-peasy* |
| rough estimate | *ballpark figure* | *ballpark figure; rough estimate* |
| unexpected thing | *out of left field* | *out of the blue* (also used in US) |
| be tired | *beat; wiped out* | *shattered; knackered (informal)* |
| quickly | *in a jiffy* | *in a tick; in a jiffy* |
| it's simple | *it's a no-brainer* | *it's a no-brainer; there's nothing to it* |
| and that's it | *and that's that* | *and Bob's your uncle* |
| unreliable | *flaky* | *dodgy* |
| very good | *awesome; the real deal* | *brilliant; smashing (older)* |
| very expensive | *cost an arm and a leg* | *cost the earth; cost a bomb* |
| cheap | *dirt cheap* | *dirt cheap; cheap as chips* |

**Sources differ:** US idioms draw heavily on **baseball, football, and the Wild West** (*step up to the plate, strike out, touch base, Monday-morning quarterback, a whole new ballgame, shoot from the hip*). UK idioms draw on **cricket, tea, and pub culture** (*bowled over, on a sticky wicket, not my cup of tea, a different kettle of fish*). Both varieties share the Tier 1 core.

**Practical tip:** if you aren't sure which variety your listener uses, choose Tier 1 idioms shared by both, and avoid heavily regional ones (*Bob's your uncle, the whole nine yards*) until you've heard them in the wild.

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

### 7.1 Literal translation from Spanish
| Spanish idiom | ❌ Literal English | ✅ English equivalent |
|---|---|---|

| *Más vale tarde que nunca* | *more worth late than never* | **better late than never** |

| *Estar como una cabra* | *be like a goat* | **be crazy / be nuts** |
| *Echar una mano* | *throw a hand* | **lend a hand / give someone a hand** |
| *Tener mala leche* | *have bad milk* | **have a nasty streak / be bad-tempered** |
| *Salirse con la suya* | *go out with his own* | **get your own way** |
| *Quedarse en blanco* | *stay in white* | **go blank / draw a blank** |
| *Poner los pelos de punta* | *put hairs on end* | **make your hair stand on end / give you goosebumps** |
| *Hacer castillos en el aire* | *make castles in the air* | **build castles in the air / daydream** |
| *Estar en el quinto pino* | *be at the fifth pine* | **be in the middle of nowhere** |
| *Cuando las ranas críen pelo* | *when frogs grow hair* | **when pigs fly** |
| *Ni de broma* | *not even of joke* | **no way / not on your life** |

**Three types of correspondence**
1. **Same image** (*dar en el clavo*, *matar dos pájaros*, *tirar la toalla*): transfer directly.
2. **Different image, same meaning** (*pan comido*/*piece of cake*): learn the English one.
3. **No equivalent**: use a literal paraphrase.

> **Never invent an idiom by translating.** If it isn't in a dictionary or corpus, don't use it.

| *Drive me up to the wall.* | *drive me up the wall* | no *to* |
| *To pull my leg.* | *You're pulling my leg.* | tense/form |
| *I'm agree with you at the end of the day.* | *I agree with you.* | grammar first |

### 7.3 Mixed idioms (mixed metaphors)
| ❌ Mixed | ✅ Fix |
|---|---|
| *We'll burn that bridge when we come to it.* | *We'll cross that bridge when we come to it.* |
| *That's the tip of the iceberg in the ocean.* | *That's the tip of the iceberg.* |
| *He's a wolf in sheep's skin.* | *a wolf in sheep's clothing* |
| *It's a drop in the sea.* | *a drop in the ocean* (or *a drop in the bucket*, US) |
| *She hit the nail in the coffin.* | *the final nail in the coffin* / *hit the nail on the head* |
| *We are in the same pond.* | *in the same boat* |
| *To kill two birds with a stone.* | *with one stone* |
| *Take it with a pinch of salt* | ✅ UK also; US: *a grain of salt* |

### 7.4 Pragmatic mistakes
- **Overuse:** *At the end of the day, in the long run, it is what it is, to be honest, no big deal…* in a single paragraph sounds like a parody.
- **Wrong register:** *The patient kicked the bucket* in a medical report; *Mr. Smith has passed away* is correct.
- **Humor mismatch:** *It's raining cats and dogs* in a serious weather report sounds childish.
- **Forcing idioms:** inserting one in every sentence makes you sound like a textbook.
- **Using idioms with non-native colleagues:** they may nod without understanding. Check: *Does that make sense?*
- **Mispronouncing connected phrases**, e.g., *piece of cake* as three separate words with strong stress.
- **Believing "more idioms = more advanced."** Precision and naturalness matter more than quantity.

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ **"Native speakers talk in idioms all the time."** — Idioms are frequent but a small set dominates (Tier 1). Most speech is literal.
- ❌ **"I must learn hundreds of idioms to be fluent."** — No. A core of ~100 Tier 1 idioms, plus the ability to decode others, is enough for B2–C1.
- ❌ **"Idioms are slang."** — Slang is informal vocabulary of specific groups; idioms are fixed expressions across registers. Some idioms are slangy, many are neutral.
- ❌ **"If I understand each word, I understand the idiom."** — By definition, not always. *Spill the beans* with perfect vocabulary still needs learning.
- ❌ **"Idioms can't change at all."** — Tense, person, and possessives can change; core words cannot.
- ❌ **"All idioms are informal."** — Many are neutral (*in the long run, take into account, make sense*); some are formal/literary (*Achilles' heel*).
- ❌ **"Every Spanish idiom has an English twin."** — Some do; most don't.
- ❌ **"Textbook idioms are the most useful."** — Textbooks love *raining cats and dogs* and *kick the bucket*; real speech favors *no big deal, at the end of the day, sort of, you know*.
- ❌ **"Using idioms shows high proficiency."** — Using the *right* idioms in the *right* context does; random use shows the opposite.
- ❌ **"I can guess any idiom from the picture."** — Sometimes, but the literal image can mislead (*a red herring* has nothing to do with fish).
- ❌ **"Business idioms are always professional."** — Many are clichés; overuse is mocked ("corporate speak").

## 9. Pronunciation Note

### 9.1 Idioms as one rhythm unit
Idioms are spoken as **one fluent chunk**, with connected speech (Lesson 5 and Phase 7). Stress falls on the **key content words**; function words reduce.

| Idiom | Rhythm (CAPS = stressed) | Connected-speech notes |
|---|---|---|
| a piece of cake | a PIECE of CAKE | /əˈpiːs əv ˈkeɪk/ → *of* reduces to /əv/ or /ə/ |
| under the weather | UNDer the WEATHer | *the* → /ðə/; *weather* /ˈwɛðər/ (not /ˈwɪðər/) |
| cost an arm and a leg | COST an ARM and a LEG | linking: *cost-an*, *arm-and*, *and-a* /ən/ |
| at the end of the day | at the END of the DAY | very fast: /ətðiˈɛndəvðəˈdeɪ/ |
| hit the nail on the head | HIT the NAIL on the HEAD | linking *hit-the*; *on the* weak |
| kill two birds with one stone | KILL two BIRDS with one STONE | *with one* /wɪθ wʌn/ |
| it's not rocket science | it's NOT ROCKet SCIence | stress on *rock-* and *sci-* |
| once in a blue moon | ONCE in a BLUE MOON | *once in a* linked: /wʌnsɪnə/ |
| the last straw | the LAST STRAW | *last straw* → /læstˈstrɔː/ (t is often dropped/merged) |
| bite the bullet | BITE the BULlet | linking *bite-the* |
| spill the beans | SPILL the BEANS | |
| break the ice | BREAK the ICE | *break-the* ; /ðə/ + vowel → /ði/ |

### 9.2 Intonation
- Idioms often come with **expressive intonation** to show attitude: *It was a PIECE of CAKE!* (rising-falling, surprise/pride) vs. *Yeah… piece of cake.* (flat, ironic).
- When used ironically, the speaker may **stretch** the stress or pause: *Oh, that's a... blessing in disguise.*
- Discourse idioms (*by the way, at the end of the day*) are pronounced quickly, with a **tune that rises slightly** before the main clause.

### 9.3 Common pronunciation problems (Spanish speakers)
- **Initial s-clusters:** *spill the beans, stone, straw, speak your mind, steal the show* → no "e" before *s*.
- **/θ/ and /ð/:** *the, weather, with, both, though, think outside the box.*
- **Word-final consonants in linked phrases:** *last straw, cost an arm, kick the bucket, at the end of the day.*
- **/ɪ/ vs /iː/:** *hit the sack* (not "heat"), *fill in / feel in*, *bit the bullet* vs. *beat the bullet*.
- **/æ/ in *back, hang, bank, hand, cat, rat*:** *back to square one, hang out, break the bank.*
- **/ɜːr/ in *work, world, learn, burn, turn*:** *burn the midnight oil, learn the ropes, turn a blind eye.*
- **Silent letters:** *knock on wood* /nɑːk/, *the whole nine yards* (silent *w* in *whole*), *a wild goose chase*.
- **Vowel reduction:** *piece of cake* → "piece-uh-cake."

### 9.4 Stress in idioms: a rule
In most idioms, **the last noun gets the strongest stress**: *a piece of CAKE, cost an arm and a LEG, kick the BUCKet, spill the BEANS, hit the NAIL on the HEAD* (the last stressed word takes the nuclear stress). In fixed binomials, stress the **second** element: *sooner or LATer, back and FORTH, safe and SOUND, black and WHITE.*

## 10. Memory Technique

### 10.1 Learn idioms in **scenes**, not lists
For each Tier 1 idiom, anchor it to one **personal scene** and one **image**:
- *a piece of cake* → picture a slice of cake and your last easy exam.
- *bite the bullet* → picture yourself paying a big bill you've been avoiding.
- *under the weather* → picture yourself in bed with tea on a rainy day.

The more personal and vivid the scene, the stronger the memory (elaborative encoding).

### 10.2 The idiom card (from Lesson 51's six fields)
| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| **Idiom** | bite the bullet |
| **Meaning** | accept something unpleasant and get on with it |
| **Register / tier** | neutral / Tier 1 |
| **Typical context** | money, medical, work decisions |
| **Example (mine)** | *I'll bite the bullet and call the landlord today.* |
| **Spanish note** | no direct equivalent; closest: *tragar saliva / apechugar* |

### 10.3 Group by theme, not by alphabet
Learn **5 idioms per week**, all from one theme (money, effort, communication…). Related items reinforce each other; unrelated ones interfere.

### 10.4 Tell the story
Write a 5-sentence story using 5 idioms from one theme. A narrative creates a retrieval path:
> *When my boss gave me the project, I was in over my head. I decided to bite the bullet and ask for help. My colleague showed me the ropes, and soon I got the hang of it. In the end it was a piece of cake, and we hit the nail on the head with the client.*

(Keep this as a memory exercise; in real writing, 5 idioms in 5 sentences is too many.)

### 10.5 Spaced retrieval
Review: day 1, 3, 7, 14, 30. Make the prompts **situation → idiom**, not idiom → meaning:
- *Your plan has to be abandoned and you must start again.* → *back to square one.*
- *A decision is difficult.* → *a tough call.*
This trains **production**, which is where idioms usually fail.

### 10.6 The "hear it / use it" rule
Only add an idiom to your active list after you've **heard or read it in at least two real contexts**. This avoids rare textbook idioms and guarantees you know the register.

### 10.7 The "three uses" challenge
Use each new idiom **three times within a week**: once in speaking, once in writing, once in a message to a friend or language partner. Then it is yours.

### 10.8 Contrast pairs
Learn easily confused idioms together:
- *spill the beans* (reveal a secret) vs. *let the cat out of the bag* (accidentally).
- *a blessing in disguise* (bad → good) vs. *a double-edged sword* (good and bad at the same time).
- *back to square one* (start again) vs. *back to the drawing board* (redesign).
- *break the ice* (start a conversation) vs. *break the bank* (spend too much).
- *on the fence* (undecided) vs. *sit tight* (wait).
- *the last straw* (final trigger) vs. *the tip of the iceberg* (small visible part).

### 10.9 Collect from real sources
Keep an "idiom log" from series, podcasts, news, and workplace messages. For each, record where you heard it, who said it, and the tone. After 4 weeks you'll have a personalized, high-frequency list.

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1: Learn the verb–noun image families.** *hit* (the nail on the head, the road, the sack, the jackpot, the books, the roof), *break* (the ice, the bank, the news, even, a record), *cut* (corners, a long story short, to the chase, it fine), *take* (for granted, it easy, a rain check, with a grain of salt), *get* (the hang of, cold feet, the wrong idea, a grip, the picture).

> **Pattern 2: Body-part idioms.** *keep an eye on, give a hand, pull someone's leg, cost an arm and a leg, cold feet, get something off your chest, head over heels, turn a blind eye, face the music, keep your chin up.* Spanish has many body idioms, but the body parts differ; check.

> **Pattern 3: Binomials have a fixed order.** *black and white, back and forth, ups and downs, pros and cons, odds and ends, rise and fall, give and take.* You can't reverse them: ❌ *white and black* (idiom sense), ❌ *forth and back*.

> **Pattern 4: Similes with *as … as*.** *as cool as a cucumber, as busy as a bee, as good as gold, as old as the hills, as clear as mud (ironic).* Learn them as ready-made, not as templates for inventing new ones.

> **Pattern 5: "Possessive slot" idioms.** *lose one's temper, make up one's mind, speak one's mind, have a change of heart, change one's mind, keep one's cool, pull one's weight.* Always match the possessive to the subject: *He lost his temper*, not ❌ *He lost the temper.*

> **Pattern 6: Comparative idioms.** *better late than never, the more the merrier, the sooner the better, easier said than done, better safe than sorry.* They use compact grammar; keep the structure as is.

> **Pattern 7: Negative idioms for warnings.** *Don't count your chickens before they hatch. Don't put all your eggs in one basket. Don't judge a book by its cover. Don't cry over spilled milk.*

> **Pattern 8: Idioms with *it*.** *call it a day, make it, cut it out, give it a go, face it, hit it off, take it easy, beat it, fake it till you make it.* The *it* has no referent; it's part of the chunk.

> **Pattern 9: Idioms for introducing a statement** (discourse markers): *to be honest, to tell you the truth, the thing is, the fact of the matter is, let's face it, to cut a long story short, long story short, as a matter of fact, in other words, to put it mildly.* These are the most useful for speaking fluently (Phase 6).

> **Pattern 10: Intensifier idioms** (links to Lesson 53): *dead tired, dirt cheap, bone dry, stone cold, wide awake, fast asleep, bitterly cold, pitch black, sick and tired, crystal clear, razor-sharp.*

> **Connection to Lesson 50 & Phase 4:** phrasal verbs are the "small idioms"; *get over, put up with, come up with* are semantically idiomatic.

> **Connection to Lesson 55:** register labels for idioms; Tier 1 are mostly levels 2–3.

> **Connection to Lesson 57:** business jargon is a sub-family of idioms (*touch base, circle back, ballpark*). Prefer Tier 1 plain forms when writing to international readers.

> **Connection to Phase 6:** hedging and softening (Lesson 59) uses discourse idioms (*to be honest, sort of, kind of*).

## 12. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1 — Basic**
- *It's a piece of cake.*
- *I'm a bit under the weather today.*
- *Let's call it a day.*
- *No big deal.*
- *By the way, what time is the meeting?*

**Level 2 — Everyday**
- *I haven't seen him in ages; he called me out of the blue.*
- *We don't see eye to eye on politics, but we get along.*
- *This phone cost me an arm and a leg.*
- *I eat out once in a blue moon.*
- *Keep me posted on what happens.*

**Level 3 — Intermediate**
- *At the end of the day, it's your decision.*
- *I've been burning the midnight oil to finish the report.*
- *He got cold feet and canceled the trip.*
- *Don't jump the gun; we don't have the results yet.*
- *It was a blessing in disguise: I lost the job but found a better one.*

**Level 4 — Advanced**
- *Hiring more people is only a drop in the ocean; we need a structural solution.*
- *We're between a rock and a hard place: either we cut costs or we lose the contract.*
- *Everyone knows the schedule is unrealistic, but no one wants to mention the elephant in the room.*
- *The failed audit was the last straw; the board finally acted.*
- *Rather than guess, we should take the forecast with a grain of salt until the data is confirmed.*

**Level 5 — Native-like**
- "It's not that I disagree; I just think we're putting the cart before the horse."
- "Honestly, it's a long shot, but we've got nothing to lose, so let's give it a go."
- "The first draft was a bit rough, but with a few tweaks we'll have it in good shape by Friday."
- "I wouldn't hold my breath. Last time they promised a refund, it took six months."
- "Her speech stole the show, but, to be fair, the bar wasn't exactly high."

### 12.1 Mini-dialogues

**1. At work**
> **A:** How's the report going?
> **B:** To be honest, I bit off more than I could chew. I've been burning the midnight oil all week.
> **A:** Do you want a hand?
> **B:** That would be great. Let's go through it together and call it a day by six.

**2. Between friends**
> **A:** Did you hear? Marta and Luis broke up.
> **B:** No way! Out of the blue?
> **A:** Apparently, the last straw was when he forgot her birthday.
> **B:** Wow. Well, don't spill the beans that I told you.

**3. Decision**
> **A:** Should we take the job offer in Medellín?
> **B:** It's a tough call. On the one hand, the salary is higher; on the other hand, we'd leave our families.
> **A:** Let's sleep on it and decide tomorrow.

**4. Learning**
> **A:** Is the new software hard?
> **B:** At first I was lost, but once I got the hang of it, it was a piece of cake.

**5. Health**
> **A:** You look pale.
> **B:** I'm a bit under the weather. I think I'll hit the sack early.
> **A:** Take it easy. Get some rest.

### 12.2 Idioms in context: reading
> *"When Ana started at the company, she didn't know the ropes, and she was in over her head. Instead of beating around the bush, she asked her manager for help. It was a tough call, but it turned out to be a blessing in disguise: within three months she had the hang of the work, and at the end of the day her honesty earned her respect."*

### 12.3 Before and after (naturalness)
> ❌ *"I want to explain you something, I think this project is not good because it costs too much money and we must take a decision now."*
> ✅ *"To be honest, I think this project is too expensive. We need to make a decision, and I'm afraid it's going to cost us an arm and a leg."*

> ❌ *"It is very easy, it is a cake piece."*
> ✅ *"It's a piece of cake."*

## 18. Writing Practice
1. **Idiom log (weekly):** Add 5 new Tier 1 idioms with meaning, register, example, and Spanish note. Review with spaced repetition.
2. **Short story (150 words):** Write a short narrative using 4 idioms from one theme. Underline them.
3. **Informal email (100 words) to a friend:** Use 2–3 idioms naturally (no more).
4. **Formal rewrite:** Take an informal paragraph full of idioms and rewrite it in formal, literal language (academic or business register). *"We're back to square one"* → *"We must begin the process again."*
5. **Informal rewrite:** Take a formal paragraph and make it conversational with a few appropriate idioms.
6. **Compare cultures (200 words):** Write about three Spanish idioms and their English equivalents (or lack of them). Explain the images.
7. **Cliché audit:** Review a text you wrote last month. Count the idioms and clichés. Replace half with precise literal language.
8. **Phase 5 capstone paragraph (150–200 words):** Write a paragraph on a topic of your choice that deliberately combines: two collocations (Lessons 52–53), a precise synonym choice (54), consistent register (55), one nominalization (56), one business chunk (57), and **one** idiom (58). This integrates the whole phase.
