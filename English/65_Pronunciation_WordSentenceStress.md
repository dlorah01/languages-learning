# Lesson 65 — Word Stress and Sentence Stress
### Phase 7: Pronunciation & the Sound System | Module 2 of 5

---

## 0. Where this fits
Lesson 64 gave you the **sounds** of English. This lesson gives them **shape**: which syllable in a word is strong, and which words in a sentence carry the beat.

If you could fix only one thing in your pronunciation, the evidence from teaching and from research on intelligibility points here. A listener can forgive a slightly wrong vowel. A **wrongly stressed word** (*im-por-TAN-te* instead of *im-POR-tant*) can be hard to recognize at all, and a sentence in which every word is equally strong is tiring to follow and hides what matters.

Connections:
- **Lesson 64:** schwa /ə/, the vowel that appears in unstressed syllables, and the stress marks ˈ and ˌ.
- **Lesson 44–50 (phrasal verbs):** the particle carries the stress (*pick UP*).
- **Lesson 52–54 (collocations, synonyms):** many words you learned there have stress patterns you should now check.
- **Lesson 59–61:** hedges, discourse markers, and contrast are marked by stress and pitch.
- **Lesson 61:** contrastive stress (*I agree it's EXPENSIVE, but not TOO expensive*).
- **Lesson 66 (next):** rhythm and timing, the "metronome" that stress creates.

> **Core idea:** English has a **strong–weak** pulse. Stressed syllables are long, clear, and higher in pitch; unstressed syllables are short, relaxed, and often reduced to /ə/. This happens inside words (**word stress**) and across sentences (**sentence stress**).

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
Say the word *banana* in Spanish: *ba-NA-na*. Say it in English: *buh-NA-nuh*. The stressed syllable is the same, but in English the first and last syllables almost disappear: /bəˈnænə/. The Spanish version gives every syllable a clear vowel and roughly equal time. The English version **squeezes** the unstressed ones.

Now a sentence. In Spanish you might say each word with similar weight: *Quiero ir a la tienda para comprar pan.* In English a native speaker says:

> I **WANT** to **GO** to the **STORE** to **BUY** some **BREAD**.

The words in bold are the **content words** (they carry meaning). The little words (*I, to, to, the, to, some*) are squeezed so much that *to* sounds like "tə", *the* like "thə", and *some* like "səm".

You can think of English as a **melody with strong beats**. Listeners tune in to the beats. If you place the beats correctly, you can mispronounce a few vowels and still be understood. If you place them in the wrong places, even perfect vowels won't save you.

Three things make a syllable **stressed**:
1. **Longer** (it takes more time).
2. **Louder** (a bit more energy).
3. **Higher in pitch** (or a pitch change).
And one more, which is the most important for Spanish speakers:
4. **Clearer vowel quality** (a full vowel like /æ/ or /ɑː/ instead of /ə/).

Unstressed syllables are short, quiet, low or flat in pitch, and use reduced vowels (/ə/, /ɪ/).

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 Word stress: the basics
- Every English word of two or more syllables has **one primary stress** (marked ˈ in IPA).
- Longer words may also have a **secondary stress** (marked ˌ): *ˌeducation* /ˌɛdʒəˈkeɪʃən/.
- **One-syllable words** are not "stressed" on their own; they become stressed or unstressed depending on the sentence (Section 2.7).
- Stress is **part of the word's identity**. Moving it makes the word hard to recognize and often changes its vowels.

**Notation used here**
| Style | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| CAPS | stressed syllable | *im-POR-tant* |
| IPA ˈ ˌ | primary / secondary stress | /ɪmˈpɔːrtənt/, /ˌɛdʒəˈkeɪʃən/ |
| O o | big/small dot pattern | *important* = o O o |
| Syllable counts | | *important* = 3 syllables |

### 2.2 Spanish stress vs. English stress
**Spanish:** stress is largely **predictable from spelling**.
- Words ending in a vowel, *n*, or *s* are stressed on the second-to-last syllable (*CA-sa, ha-BLAN, CA-sas*).
- Other words are stressed on the last syllable (*ha-BLAR, ciu-DAD*).
- Exceptions are marked with a **written accent (tilde)**: *te-LÉ-fo-no, a-DIÓS*.

**English:** stress is **not marked in spelling** and is not fully predictable. It must be learned **word by word**, but there are strong patterns (Section 2.3–2.5). A dictionary shows the stress with ˈ.

**The main danger for you:** because many English words have Spanish cognates, you apply the Spanish stress to the English word:

| *animal* | a-ni-MAL | **AN-i-mal** /ˈænəməl/ |
| *doctor* | doc-TOR | **DOC-tor** /ˈdɑːktər/ |

| *democracia* | de-mo-CRA-cia | **de-MOC-ra-cy** /dɪˈmɑːkrəsi/ |
| *interesante* | in-te-re-SAN-te | **IN-ter-est-ing** /ˈɪntrəstɪŋ/ |

### 2.3 Patterns that predict word stress

#### Pattern A: Two-syllable words
| Type | Typical stress | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Nouns** | first syllable | TAble, DOCtor, MOTHer, WINdow, PROblem |
| **Adjectives** | first syllable | HAPpy, CLEver, NAtive, QUIet |
| **Verbs** | second syllable | beGIN, reLAX, forGET, deCIDE, inVITE |
| **Prepositions/adverbs** | second syllable | aROUND, beFORE, beLOW, uPON |

Exceptions: verbs that stress the first (*ANswer, ENter, OFfer, VISit, LISten, TRAvel, FINish, PUNish*), nouns that stress the second (*hoTEL, poLICE, maCHINE, caREER, guiTAR, deSIGN, reSULT*).

#### Pattern B: Noun/verb (or noun/adjective) pairs: stress shift
In many two-syllable words with the same spelling, the **noun/adjective** stresses the **first** syllable and the **verb** the **second**. The vowel often changes too.

| Noun/adjective (first) | Verb (second) |
|---|---|
| REcord /ˈrɛkərd/ | reCORD /rɪˈkɔːrd/ |
| PREsent /ˈprɛzənt/ | preSENT /prɪˈzɛnt/ |
| OBject /ˈɑːbdʒɛkt/ | obJECT /əbˈdʒɛkt/ |
| INcrease /ˈɪnkriːs/ | inCREASE /ɪnˈkriːs/ |
| PROduce /ˈproʊduːs/ (n.) | proDUCE /prəˈduːs/ |
| CONtract /ˈkɑːntrækt/ | conTRACT /kənˈtrækt/ |
| PERmit /ˈpɜːrmɪt/ | perMIT /pərˈmɪt/ |
| REbel /ˈrɛbəl/ | reBEL /rɪˈbɛl/ |
| PROject /ˈprɑːdʒɛkt/ | proJECT /prəˈdʒɛkt/ |
| CONflict /ˈkɑːnflɪkt/ | conFLICT /kənˈflɪkt/ |
| SUBject /ˈsʌbdʒɪkt/ | subJECT /səbˈdʒɛkt/ |
| CONtent /ˈkɑːntɛnt/ (n.) | conTENT /kənˈtɛnt/ (adj.: satisfied) |
| REfuse /ˈrɛfjuːs/ (garbage) | reFUSE /rɪˈfjuːz/ (v.) |
| DEsert /ˈdɛzərt/ (n.) | deSERT /dɪˈzɜːrt/ (v.: abandon) |
| MINute /ˈmɪnɪt/ (n.) | minUTE /maɪˈnuːt/ (adj.: tiny) |

Not all pairs shift (*answer, visit, promise, reply, support* keep their pattern).

#### Pattern C: Suffixes that **pull** stress to the syllable just before them
| Suffix | Examples (stress marked) |
|---|---|
| **-tion / -sion** | eduCAtion, inforMAtion, deCIsion, conVERsion |
| **-ic / -ical** | ecoNOmic, graPHic, poLITical, hisTORical |
| **-ity** | poSSiBILity, abILity, uniVERsity, reALity |
| **-ial / -ian / -ious / -ual / -eous** | esSENtial, muSIcian, amBItious, inDIVidual, couRAgeous |
| **-ology / -ography / -ometer** | bioLOgy, phoTOgraphy, thermOMeter |

#### Pattern D: Suffixes that **do not change** the stress
-ing, -ed, -er, -or (usually), -ly, -ness, -ment, -ful, -less, -hood, -ship, -dom, -ish, -able (mostly), -s/-es.
- *deVELop → deVELoping → deVELopment*
- *Comfort → COMfortable*
- *Happy → HAPpiness → HAPpily*

#### Pattern E: Suffixes that **attract** stress (they carry it themselves)
-ee, -eer, -ese, -ette, -esque, -ique, -oo:
- *emploYEE, refuGEE, engiNEER, volunTEER, JapaNESE, cigarETTE, techNIQUE, picturESQUE, bamBOO, taTOO.*
(Exceptions: *COFfee, COMmittee, PEDigree*.)

#### Pattern F: Compounds
- **Compound nouns**: stress the **first** element: *GREENhouse, BLACKboard, TOOTHbrush, AIRport, BOOKstore, COFFee shop, WHITE House, HOT dog.*
- **Adjective + noun phrase**: stress the **noun** (the second): *a green HOUSE (painted green), a hot DOG (a warm dog), a black BOARD.*
- **Compound adjectives/verbs**: often stress the **second** element: *old-FASHioned, well-KNOWN, bad-TEMpered, overLOOK, underSTAND.*
- **Phrasal verbs**: stress the **particle** (Phase 4): *pick UP, turn DOWN, give IN.* But the **noun** form: stress the first: *a TAKEaway, a BREAKdown, a CHECKout, a SETback.*

#### Pattern G: Words with the same letters but different meaning
| Word | Stress A | Stress B |
|---|---|---|
| *dessert / desert* | deSSERT /dɪˈzɜːrt/ (sweet) | DEsert /ˈdɛzərt/ (sand) |
| *invalid* | inVALid /ɪnˈvælɪd/ (not valid) | INvalid /ˈɪnvəlɪd/ (sick person) |
| *compact* | comPACT (adj./v.) | COMpact (n.) |
| *contrast* | conTRAST (v.) | CONtrast (n.) |

### 2.4 Secondary stress and reduced vowels
- Long words have a main stress (ˈ) and sometimes a weaker secondary stress (ˌ):
  - *eduˌcation* /ˌɛdʒəˈkeɪʃən/ — secondary on *ed-*, primary on *-ca-*.
  - *communiˌcation* /kəˌmjuːnəˈkeɪʃən/.
- All other syllables are **unstressed** and their vowels reduce to /ə/, /ɪ/, or sometimes /i/ and /ɚ/.

**The stress–vowel connection (a crucial idea):** when stress moves, the vowels change.
- PHOtograph /ˈfoʊtəɡræf/
- phoTOGraphy /fəˈtɑːɡrəfi/
- photoGRAPHic /ˌfoʊtəˈɡræfɪk/

Notice: the first syllable has /oʊ/ in *photograph* (stressed) but /ə/ in *photography* (unstressed). If you stress the wrong syllable, the whole word's vowel pattern changes.

Other pairs: *pronounce* /prəˈnaʊns/ → *pronunciation* /prəˌnʌnsiˈeɪʃən/ (the *-nounce-* vowel changes from /aʊ/ to /ʌ/); *different* → *difference*; *economy* /ɪˈkɑːnəmi/ → *economic* /ˌɛkəˈnɑːmɪk/.

### 2.6 Sentence stress: content words vs. function words
In connected speech:
- **Content words** (carry meaning) are **stressed**: nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs, question words, negatives (*not, don't, can't*), demonstratives (*this, that*), numbers.
- **Function words** (grammar glue) are **unstressed** and **reduced**: articles (*a, the*), prepositions (*to, for, of, at, in*), pronouns (*I, you, he, them*), auxiliaries (*is, are, have, do, will*), conjunctions (*and, but, or*), and the verb *to be* unless emphasized.

Compare:
> I **WANT** to **GO** to the **STORE** to **BUY** some **BREAD**.
> (content words: want, go, store, buy, bread; reduced: I, to, to, the, to, some)

> She **CAN'T** **COME** to the **PARty**.
> The word *can't* is stressed because negation carries meaning.

> **WHAT** did you **DO** last **WEEKend**?

**Result:** English sounds like a sequence of strong beats with weak syllables squeezed in between. This is what makes it **stress-timed** (Lesson 66).

**The *can / can't* contrast** is the most important weak-form issue for Spanish speakers:
- *I **can** swim.* → /aɪ kən ˈswɪm/ (weak *can*; the stress falls on *swim*)
- *I **can't** swim.* → /aɪ ˈkænt ˈswɪm/ (strong *can't* with a full vowel; both words stressed)
If you pronounce affirmative *can* with a full /æ/ vowel, listeners may hear *can't*. Use the reduced form in statements, and stress *can't* clearly in negative ones. (Exception: short answers like *Yes, I **can**.* use the strong form.)

When function words **are** stressed:
- at the end of a sentence: *Where are you **from**?* /frʌm/
- in a short answer: *Yes, I **am**. / Yes, I **can**.*
- for contrast: *I said **to**, not **from**.*
- when emphasized for meaning: *I **do** like it.*

### 2.8 The nuclear stress (focus): the most important word
In each **thought group** (a chunk of speech between pauses) there is one syllable that carries the **main** stress and the main pitch movement. This is the **nuclear stress** or **focus**.

**Default position (neutral focus):** the **last content word** of the phrase.
- *I'm going to the **SUPERmarket**.*
- *She works at a **BANK**.*
- *Where do you **LIVE**?*

**Shifted position (contrastive or emphatic focus):** the speaker moves the main stress to the word that is **new, corrected, or contrasted**.
> A: Did you buy the **red** car?
> B: No, I bought the **BLUE** car.

> A: Did **Ana** buy the car?
> B: No, **LUIS** bought it.

> A: Did you **sell** the car?
> B: No, I **BOUGHT** it.

**Given information is de-stressed.** If a word was just mentioned or is obvious, it carries less stress:
> A: What did you eat?
> B: I ate **PASta**.
> (not "I ATE pasta" unless contrasting with another verb)

**Meaning changes with stress.** Say this sentence seven times, stressing each word in turn:
> **I** didn't say she stole the money. (someone else said it)
> I **DIDN'T** say she stole the money. (I deny saying it)
> I didn't **SAY** she stole the money. (I implied it, wrote it, but didn't say it)
> I didn't say **SHE** stole the money. (someone else stole it)
> I didn't say she **STOLE** the money. (she borrowed it, found it…)
> I didn't say she stole **THE** money. (some specific money)
> I didn't say she stole the **MONEY**. (something else)

This is exactly how English speakers show contrast without extra words.

### 2.9 Stress in common sentence types

| Type | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Statement** | nuclear stress on last content word, falling pitch | I live in **BoGOTá**. ↘ |
| **Yes/no question** | nuclear stress on last content word, rising pitch | Do you like **COFfee**? ↗ |
| **Wh- question** | stress on question word and final content word, falling | **WHERE** do you **LIVE**? ↘ |
| **Command** | stress on the verb and object | **CLOSE** the **DOOR**. ↘ |
| **Negative** | negative word is stressed | I **DON'T** **KNOW**. / It's **NOT** here. |
| **List** | stress each item; rise on each except last (fall) | I bought **APples ↗, BaNAnas ↗, and ORanges ↘**. |
| **Numbers** | stress the number | It costs **FIFty** dollars. |
| **Tag question** | falling = confirmation, rising = genuine question | It's **COLD**, isn't it? ↘ |
| **Spelling/phone numbers** | each digit stressed in groups with a rising/falling contour | 3-1-**0**, 5-5-**5**, 1-2-**3**-4 |

### 2.10 Thought groups (sense groups)
Speech is broken into **thought groups** (also called intonation phrases or tone units): short chunks, 3–7 words, each with one main stress. Pauses fall between groups, not inside them.

> When I was **YOUNG** | my **FAther** | worked in a **BANK** | in **BoGOTá**.

Rules of thumb:
1. Pause at punctuation and between clauses.
2. Do not pause between an article and its noun, a preposition and its object, or an auxiliary and its main verb.
3. Each group has one main stress, normally on its last content word.
4. Chunks help the listener (and you) process speech.

### 2.11 Stress and emotion
Emphasis can add feeling:
- *That's **REALLY** good!* (enthusiasm)
- *I'm **SO** tired.* (strong feeling)
- *It was **NOT** funny.* (strong negative)
- *I **LOVE** it!*
Also extra length: *It's sooo good.* But use sparingly; constant emphasis sounds artificial.

### 2.12 Stress and grammar
Stress helps listeners hear grammar:
- **Past tense -ed** is often weak: *walked* /wɔːkt/, but if the final sound is dropped, the tense is lost.
- **Third person -s**: *she works* /ʃi wɜːrks/; if missing, grammar and meaning change.
- **Contractions**: *I'm, we'll, they've* are unstressed pieces of larger chunks.
- **Auxiliaries**: usually weak in affirmative statements (*I have been waiting* /aɪ əv bɪn ˈweɪtɪŋ/) but strong in emphatic ones (*I **have** been waiting!*).

## 3. Why Native Speakers Do This
1. **Stress-timed rhythm.** English organizes speech around stressed syllables that come at roughly regular intervals. Unstressed syllables are compressed to fit between them (Lesson 66).
2. **Information structure.** Speakers put emphasis on the **new or important** information. Stress is a tool to tell the listener "listen here."
3. **Vowel reduction saves effort.** The relaxed /ə/ lets speakers fit many syllables between beats without losing time.
4. **Disambiguation.** Stress distinguishes pairs (*REcord/reCORD*) and contrasts (*the red car / the BLUE car*).
5. **History.** English stress patterns come from Germanic (initial stress) and from French/Latin borrowings (later stress). Two systems overlap in the same vocabulary, which explains why stress seems irregular.
6. **Listener processing.** Listeners rely on stressed syllables as anchors to segment continuous speech into words. A misplaced stress removes the anchor.
7. **Naturalness.** Native speakers feel a sentence is "off" when stress is flat or misplaced, even if all sounds are correct.

## 4. When to Use / When Not To

**Apply word stress rules**
- always: every multi-syllable word has a stress;
- check a dictionary when learning a new word (look for ˈ);
- group new vocabulary by stress pattern.

**Apply sentence stress**
- in speaking, shadowing, reading aloud, presenting;
- when you want to be clear about what is important;
- to correct a misunderstanding (shift focus).

**Don't**
- over-stress every word (choppy, robotic);
- stress function words unless you have a reason;
- exaggerate: stressed syllables should be clearer, not shouted;
- try to apply a rule to every word (there are exceptions; check);
- forget the vowels: stress without reduction still sounds foreign.

**Practical balance.** Aim for **content words clear, function words quick**. Think of function words as the "glue" that you say quickly and lightly.

## 5. Frequency & Priority

**What to prioritize**
1. **Stress of the high-frequency words you say daily** (*important, interesting, comfortable, different, family, problem, computer, information, education*).
2. **Nuclear stress** on the final content word in each phrase.
3. **Weak forms** of *to, of, and, a, the, for, can, have, was*.
4. **Schwa** in unstressed syllables.
5. **Noun/verb stress pairs** (*record, present, increase, object*).
6. **Suffix patterns** (*-tion, -ic, -ity*).
7. **Compound nouns.**
8. **Contrastive stress** for corrections and clarity.
9. **Secondary stress** in long words.

**Register note.** Stress and reduction increase with informality and speed. In careful speech (presentations, phone calls with poor audio), use slightly fuller forms but keep the stress pattern.

### 7.5 Cultural/learning pitfalls
- **Expecting a written mark.** English has no accent marks; the dictionary shows stress. Don't add a Spanish tilde in your head.
- **Equating stress with volume alone.** Stress is length + pitch + vowel clarity + loudness.
- **Fear of reducing vowels.** Reduction sounds "lazy" to Spanish ears but is correct in English.
- **Over-correcting** into shouting stressed syllables. Make them longer and clearer, not louder.
- **Neglecting practice with whole sentences.** Word stress improves quickly in isolation but breaks down in sentences unless you practice both.

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ **"English stress is random."** — It has strong patterns (suffix rules, noun/verb pairs, compounds); exceptions exist.
- ❌ **"Stress is just loudness."** — It's mostly length, pitch, and vowel clarity.
- ❌ **"Every word in a sentence is stressed."** — Only content words (and function words with a reason) are.
- ❌ **"Reducing vowels is sloppy."** — It is standard and necessary for natural rhythm.
- ❌ **"If I pronounce all the sounds, stress doesn't matter."** — Misplaced stress can make a word unrecognizable.
- ❌ **"Spanish and English stress rules are similar."** — Spanish stress is spelling-predictable; English is lexical.
- ❌ **"The nuclear stress is always on the last word."** — It's the last content word by default and moves for focus.
- ❌ **"Stress and intonation are the same."** — Stress marks which syllable is prominent; intonation is the pitch melody of the whole phrase (a later Phase 7 lesson).
- ❌ **"Unstressed syllables disappear."** — They are shortened, not deleted (except in some very casual speech).
- ❌ **"British and American stress are totally different."** — Mostly the same with a small set of differing words.
- ❌ **"I'll never get rhythm because I'm Spanish."** — Learners improve with training, shadowing, and awareness.
- ❌ **"A dictionary stress mark is optional."** — It is the quickest guide you have.

## 9. Pronunciation Note: Training Method

### 9.1 How to hear stress
1. **Tap or clap** on the stressed syllable while saying the word: *im-POR-tant* (tap on POR).
2. **Hum the rhythm** with the word's syllables: *mm-MM-mm* for *important*.
3. **Stretch it.** Say the stressed syllable with a rubber-band stretch: *im-PORRRR-tant*.
4. **Whisper test.** Whisper the word; the stressed syllable still stands out by length and force.
5. **Contrast test.** Say the word twice: once correctly, once with Spanish stress. Hear the difference.
6. **Dot pattern.** Write o (small) and O (big): *important* = oOo; *banana* = oOo; *photograph* = Ooo; *photography* = oOoo; *education* = ooOo.

### 9.2 The five cues of a stressed syllable
| Cue | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Length** | make it longer |
| **Pitch** | make it higher or a pitch movement |
| **Loudness** | a little louder |
| **Vowel quality** | full, clear vowel |
| **Consonant clarity** | clearer, e.g., aspirated /p t k/ (Lesson 64) |

### 9.3 Stress patterns (dot codes) by syllable count
| Pattern | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Oo** | TAble, HAPpy, DOCtor, MOTHer |
| **oO** | beGIN, reLAX, forGET, aROUND |
| **Ooo** | FAMily, ANimal, HOSpital, VEGetable |
| **oOo** | imPORtant, baNAna, toMAto, reMEMber |
| **ooO** | engiNEER, volunTEER, JapaNESE |
| **Oooo** | NECessary, LIbrary (US) |
| **oOoo** | phoTOgraphy, ecoNOmy, deVELopment, exPEriENCE |
| **ooOo** | eduCAtion, inforMAtion, situAtion |
| **ooOoo** | uniVERsity, possiBILity |
| **oOooo** | vocABulary |

### 9.4 Reading dictionary stress
1. Find ˈ (primary) and ˌ (secondary).
2. Say the syllable **after** ˈ longer and clearer.
3. Reduce vowels in syllables without marks.
4. Practice the word in a phrase: *an im-POR-tant ME-eting*.

### 9.5 Sentence-stress drills
**Telegram method.** Reduce a sentence to its content words, say it rhythmically, then add the function words quickly between the beats.
- Telegram: *WANT GO STORE BUY BREAD*
- Full: *I **WANT** to **GO** to the **STORE** to **BUY** some **BREAD**.*

**Tap-and-speak.** Tap a steady beat (about one per second) and make each stressed syllable fall on a tap, squeezing the unstressed ones in between.

**Shadowing.** Shadow short audio, mimicking where the stresses fall (Lesson 66 expands this).

**Mark and read.** Take any paragraph, underline the stressed syllables, then read it aloud, strongly on the marks and lightly elsewhere.

### 9.6 Spanish-speaker trouble spots
- **Giving every vowel a full quality** (syllable-timed habit).
- **Moving stress to the last syllable** of cognates.
- **Stressing grammatical endings** (-ing, -ed, -ly).
- **Not reducing function words.**
- **Falling pitch on every content word** (staccato sound).
- **Not emphasizing the new/corrected word.**

### 9.7 Tools
- Cambridge / Oxford Learner's / Longman online dictionaries (stress marks and audio).
- YouGlish (stress in real speech).
- Praat or phone apps with pitch display (visual feedback on pitch and length).
- Recording and comparing with native audio.

## 10. Memory Technique

### 10.1 Stress families
Group words by their stress pattern and learn them as families:
- **-tion family (stress before suffix):** education, information, communication, situation, organization, relation.
- **-ic family:** economic, historic, scientific, specific, electric.
- **-ity family:** ability, possibility, university, opportunity, personality.
- **-ee/-eer family:** employee, refugee, engineer, volunteer.
- **Compound noun family:** GREENhouse, BLACKboard, BOOKstore, TOOTHbrush.
- **Noun/verb family:** REcord/reCORD, PREsent/preSENT, INcrease/inCREASE.

### 10.2 Sound the shift
Memorize one word whose stress moves: *PHOtograph – phoTOGraphy – photoGRAPHic*. It teaches you three things at once: stress shifts, vowels change, suffixes pull stress.

### 10.3 Drawing the rhythm
Write each new word with big and small circles above the syllables (●○○, ○●○). Keep a "rhythm notebook." Seeing the shape helps your memory.

### 10.4 Mnemonics
- "Nouns lead (first), verbs follow (second)" for two-syllable pairs.
- "-tion, -ic, -ity: stress comes **before** the suffix."
- "Content words **speak**, function words **whisper**."
- "Can = quiet /kən/, can't = loud /kænt/."
- "The last content word is the star" (nuclear stress).

### 10.5 Spaced retrieval cards
- Front: *im?por?tant* → **im-POR-tant**
- Front: *Noun: record* → **REcord**; *Verb: record* → **reCORD**
- Front: *Which syllable before -tion is stressed?* → **the one right before it**
- Front: *Weak form of "to"* → **/tə/**
- Front: *"I can swim" vs "I can't swim"* → **/kən/ vs /kænt/**
- Front: *Default sentence stress?* → **last content word**
Review on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30.

### 10.6 The weekly stress list
Each week, choose 15 words you use often, look up their stress, mark the pattern, and use each in a sentence you will actually say. After 8 weeks you will have 120 high-frequency words with correct stress.

### 10.7 Listening hunts
Choose a podcast or video. Pick one sentence and mark: stressed words, reduced words, nuclear stress. Then say it with the same rhythm.

### 10.8 Movement
Use your body: tap a finger, nod, or sway on stressed syllables. Movement ties rhythm to memory.

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1: Two-syllable noun/adjective → first; verb → second.** TAble, HAPpy / beGIN, deCIDE.

> **Pattern 2: Noun/verb pairs shift stress.** REcord / reCORD, PREsent / preSENT.

> **Pattern 3: Stress falls before -tion, -sion, -ic, -ical, -ity, -ial, -ian, -ious, -ual.**

> **Pattern 4: Stress-neutral suffixes** (-ing, -ed, -er, -ly, -ness, -ment, -ful, -less) leave stress where it was.

> **Pattern 5: Stress-attracting suffixes** (-ee, -eer, -ese, -ette, -esque, -ique, -oo) carry the stress.

> **Pattern 6: Compound nouns → first element.** GREENhouse; **adjective + noun → noun.** green HOUSE.

> **Pattern 7: Phrasal verbs stress the particle; their noun forms stress the first part.** pick UP / a PICKup.

> **Pattern 8: Stress changes vowel quality.** stressed = full vowel; unstressed = /ə/ or /ɪ/.

> **Pattern 9: Content words stressed, function words reduced.**

> **Pattern 10: Nuclear stress = last content word by default; moves for contrast.**

> **Pattern 11: Given information is de-stressed; new information is stressed.**

> **Pattern 12: Thought groups: one main stress per group, pause only at boundaries.**

**Mini Quiz**
1. Length, pitch, loudness, and vowel clarity.
2. They apply Spanish stress rules (spelling-based, usually penultimate or marked with accents) to English cognates.
3. The syllable immediately before the suffix.
4. Nouns stress the first syllable, verbs the second: REcord / reCORD.
5. /tə/ and /əv/.
6. Weak /kən/ vs strong /kænt/: using the wrong form changes meaning.
7. The last content word of the thought group.
8. When the speaker contrasts, corrects, or emphasizes a word.
9. They reduce, usually to /ə/ or /ɪ/.
10. Compound nouns stress the first element; adjective + noun phrases stress the noun.
11. On the particle (pick UP); the noun form stresses the first element (a PICKup).
