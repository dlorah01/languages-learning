# Lesson 7 — Present Tenses: Simple, Continuous, Perfect, Perfect Continuous
### Phase 1: Grammar | Block A (Tense, Aspect, Mood, Voice) | Lesson 2 of 7

---

## 0. Where this fits
Lesson 6 gave you the grid: 2 tenses × 4 aspects. This lesson fills in the **present row** completely — all four aspect combinations, with full rules, the critical stative-verb exception, and the errors Spanish speakers make most often in this exact row (since Spanish's simple present covers ground that English splits across simple and continuous).

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation

Picture "now" as a single vertical line on a timeline. The four present aspects are four different camera angles on that line:
- **Simple present** — a wide-angle shot that isn't really about *this instant* at all; it's about patterns, facts, and truths that hold *around* now (habits, schedules, general truths).
- **Present continuous** — a close-up, zoomed right into this instant, catching something mid-motion.
- **Present perfect** — the camera is on "now," but showing a flashback reel of something from before that still matters.
- **Present perfect continuous** — the camera is on "now," showing a flashback reel of something that's been running continuously up to this point.

The single biggest reframe for Spanish speakers: **simple present is NOT the general-purpose "present tense."** It's specifically for facts/habits/routines. "Right now" almost always needs continuous.

---

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2a. Present Simple
**Form:** base verb (+s for he/she/it)
**Core meaning:** habitual actions, general truths/facts, permanent states, scheduled/timetabled events (even future ones).

- Habit: "I go to the gym three times a week."
- General truth: "Water boils at 100°C."
- Permanent state: "She works as an engineer."
- Fixed schedule (even referring to the future): "The train leaves at 6 PM tomorrow."

### 2b. Present Continuous
**Form:** am/is/are + verb-ing
**Core meaning:** action in progress right now, temporary situations (even if not literally happening this second), and pre-arranged future plans.

- Right now: "I'm writing an email."
- Temporary situation (broader "now," not this literal second): "I'm reading a great book these days." / "She's staying with her parents this month."
- Pre-arranged future: "We're meeting the client on Friday." (a fixed, arranged plan — contrast with "will," covered in Lesson 9)

### 2c. Present Perfect
**Form:** have/has + past participle
**Core meaning:** a past action connected to the present — either its result matters now, or it's part of an ongoing timeframe that includes now.

- Life experience (unspecified time): "I've been to Japan twice."
- Result matters now: "I've lost my wallet." (implication: I still don't have it)
- Unfinished time period including now: "I've read three books this month." (the month isn't over)
- With "for/since" (duration up to now): "I've lived here since 2019." / "I've known her for ten years."

### 2d. Present Perfect Continuous
**Form:** have/has been + verb-ing
**Core meaning:** an action that started in the past and is still ongoing (or just stopped, with visible present-moment effects), emphasizing duration/process rather than result.

- Ongoing duration: "I've been studying for three hours." (still going, or just finished — the emphasis is on the process/duration)
- Visible present effect: "I've been running." (why are you sweaty right now? — explains a present state via a recently-ongoing action)

---

## 3. THE Critical Exception: Stative Verbs
A specific class of verbs — describing **states, not actions** — generally reject the continuous aspect entirely, even when talking about "right now." This trips up Spanish speakers constantly because Spanish doesn't have this restriction the same way.

**Categories of stative verbs (with examples):**
| Category | Verbs |
|---|---|
| Mental states | know, believe, think (=opinion), understand, remember, realize, doubt, suppose |
| Emotions/preferences | want, love, hate, like, prefer, need, wish |
| Possession | have (=own), own, belong, possess |
| Senses (as involuntary perception) | see, hear, smell, taste (as passive perception, not active testing) |
| Other states | seem, appear, contain, consist, cost, weigh, matter, mean |

- ❌ "I am knowing the answer." → ✅ "I know the answer."
- ❌ "I am wanting coffee." → ✅ "I want coffee."
- ❌ "This box is containing books." → ✅ "This box contains books."

**Important nuance:** some of these verbs *can* take continuous when used with a different, action-based meaning:
- "I think it's a good idea" (stative — opinion) vs. "I'm thinking about it" (active — the process of considering)
- "I see what you mean" (stative — understanding) vs. "I'm seeing a doctor next week" (active — meeting/dating, a different sense of "see")
- "This tastes great" (stative — passive perception) vs. "I'm tasting the soup to check the salt" (active — the deliberate act of testing)

---

## 4. When Each Aspect Matters Most / When Not To Use It

- Use **simple present** for facts/habits — NOT for "right now" actions (the #1 error).
- Use **continuous** for genuine in-progress or temporary situations — NOT for stative verbs (the #2 error) and NOT for permanent facts.
- Use **present perfect** when the past connects meaningfully to now (result, unfinished period, life experience, duration with for/since) — NOT for a fully closed-off, specific-time past event (that's simple past, Lesson 8: "I saw him yesterday," never "I've seen him yesterday" with a specific past time marker).
- Use **present perfect continuous** when duration/process is the point — less critical to nail immediately than the simple/continuous/perfect distinctions above.

---

## 5. Frequency & Register
Simple present and present continuous are extremely frequent in all registers, all day, every day. Present perfect is very common in everyday conversation (life experience, recent news, results) and even more prominent in British English (Lesson 6's US/UK note). Present perfect continuous is somewhat less frequent but entirely natural and unmarked for register — it's not formal or informal, just less needed than the other three.

---

## 6. American vs. British Note
As flagged in Lesson 6: British English favors present perfect more strictly with "just," "already," "yet," and recent-past news, where American English commonly allows simple past instead:
- UK: "I've already finished." / US: "I already finished." (both natural in US English; UK strongly prefers present perfect here)
- UK: "Have you eaten yet?" / US: "Did you eat yet?" (both common in US English; UK strongly prefers present perfect)
This doesn't mean American English "doesn't use" present perfect — it absolutely does (life experience, unfinished time periods) — the difference is specifically about these adverbs (just/already/yet) and very recent news.

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

| Mistake | Why | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "I work on it right now." | Spanish present covers both habitual and in-progress meaning | "I'm working on it right now." |
| "I am understanding the lesson." | Treating stative verb "understand" as if it takes continuous | "I understand the lesson." |
| "I live here since 2020." | Using simple present instead of present perfect for a duration continuing into now | "I've lived here since 2020." |
| "I have seen him yesterday." | Using present perfect with a specific past time marker (yesterday) | "I saw him yesterday." (simple past — the time is specified and closed, covered fully in Lesson 8) |
| "She is having a car." (meaning ownership) | Using continuous with "have" in its stative (possession) sense | "She has a car." (contrast: "She's having lunch" — action sense of "have," which DOES take continuous) |

---

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ "Present continuous always means literally this exact second." — It also covers broader "these days" temporary situations and pre-arranged future plans (Section 2b).
- ❌ "Present perfect is basically the same as simple past, just fancier." — They express genuinely different things: present perfect connects to now (result/ongoing relevance); simple past is a closed, finished, often time-stamped event. Mixing them up changes meaning, not just style.
- ❌ "All verbs can take continuous if I want to emphasize something." — Stative verbs are a real grammatical category with a real restriction, not a stylistic option.

---

## 9. Pronunciation Note
The auxiliary "have/has" in present perfect reduces heavily in natural speech: "I've," "she's," "we've" — and even further in fast speech, "have you" often becomes something close to "/əv juː/" or even drops toward "you" with only a trace of "have." If you're listening for a clear, separate "have," you may miss it; the aspect is signaled more by the past participle than by a clearly audible auxiliary.

---

## 10. Memory Technique
For stative verbs, use this filter question: **"Is this something I'm actively *doing*, or something that's simply *true* about me/it right now?"** If it's a truth/state (I know, I want, I have, it seems), simple present. If it's an active process (I'm doing, I'm working, I'm eating), continuous.

---

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern:** Present perfect almost always pairs conceptually with **"relevance to now."** If you can mentally add "...and that matters right now" to the sentence and it makes sense, present perfect is probably correct. "I've lost my keys (...and that matters right now, I can't get in)" ✅. "I've seen him yesterday (...and that matters right now)" ❌ — doesn't work, because "yesterday" already closes off the event as a specific finished point, which conflicts with perfect's "connects to now" logic. This is why perfect and specific past-time markers (yesterday, last week, in 2019) never combine — full contrast coming in Lesson 8.

> **Pattern:** "for" + duration, "since" + starting point — both trigger perfect aspect (present perfect or present perfect continuous), because both describe a span connecting past to now: "for three years," "since 2020," "for a while," "since I moved here."

---

## 12. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1:**
- "I drink coffee every morning." (habit — simple)
- "I'm drinking coffee right now." (in progress — continuous)

**Level 2 — stative exception:**
- ❌ "I'm loving this song" (technically nonstandard, though very common in casual/marketing English as a deliberate stylistic break — more on "grammatical" vs. "attested casual usage" below) → standard form: "I love this song."

*(Side note: "I'm loving it" — as in the McDonald's slogan — is a well-known deliberate, marked exception used for stylistic effect; it's recognized as nonstandard even by native speakers, which is exactly why it stands out as catchy. Don't take it as a model for standard stative-verb usage.)*

**Level 3 — present perfect vs. simple past, meaning genuinely changes:**
- "I lived in Paris." (simple past — closed chapter, implies I don't live there anymore, or at minimum treats it as a finished, separate period)
- "I've lived in Paris." (present perfect — connects to now, often implies I still live there, or at least frames it as part of my ongoing life experience without closing it off)

**Level 4 — present perfect continuous nuance:**
- "I've written three chapters." (present perfect simple — focus on the result/achievement: three chapters exist)
- "I've been writing all day." (present perfect continuous — focus on the process/duration: emphasizing the ongoing effort, not necessarily how much got finished)

## 13. Related Expressions / Terms
- **Stative verbs** (this lesson's key new term) vs. **dynamic/action verbs**.
- **For vs. since:** "for" + duration (for two years), "since" + starting point (since 2022) — both common present perfect triggers.
- **Already / yet / just:** classic present perfect (UK) / simple past-or-perfect (US) adverbs (Section 6).
