# Lesson 73 — Academic Writing Conventions
### Phase 9: Writing | Module 2 of 4

---

## 0. Where this fits
Lesson 72 taught the fast, polite, action-oriented style of professional email. Academic writing sits at the other end of the spectrum: **slower, more precise, more cautious, and built around an argument.** It is the style of university essays, theses, reports, research articles, and many formal professional documents (white papers, policy briefs, grant proposals).

For a Spanish-speaking university-level learner, this is both an advantage and a challenge:
- **Advantage:** you already know thousands of academic words through Latin cognates (*analyze, hypothesis, significant, contribute*).
- **Challenge:** the **organization, sentence length, level of directness, and punctuation** of English academic prose differ from typical Spanish academic prose. If you transfer Spanish patterns, your writing may be correct and still feel "off" to an English-speaking reader or examiner.

Connections:
- **Lesson 71:** the five levels of "translated" apply to essays as much as to speech.
- **Lesson 59:** hedging and softening become *academic caution* (*may, tend to, suggest*).
- **Lesson 72:** the "bottom line first" idea returns as the **thesis statement and topic sentences**.
- **Lessons 6–28 (grammar):** articles, tenses, passives, and clauses matter more on the page.
- **Phase 5 (vocabulary):** word formation, collocations, and register.
- **Lesson 74 (next):** casual and personal writing, the opposite end.
- **Lesson 75:** tone, style, and clarity editing, which you will use to polish academic drafts.

> **Core idea:** English academic writing is **reader-responsible**: the writer must make the structure and the argument obvious. Say what you will argue, argue it in clear steps with evidence, and tell the reader how each step connects.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
Imagine guiding a visitor through a building. In some traditions, the guide enjoys the tour: exploring side rooms, adding background, building to the main hall. In English academic tradition, the guide hands over a **map at the door** (the thesis), walks a **straight route** (one idea per paragraph), and **points out every turn** (transitions). The visitor should never wonder where they are or why.

A second image: **a courtroom.** You make a claim, present evidence, explain how the evidence supports the claim, and consider objections. You do not simply state an opinion, and you do not overclaim.

Compare:
> ❌ *Since the beginning of time, education has been very important for humanity, and many people think different things about it, so in this essay I will talk about some aspects of education.*
> ✅ *This essay argues that early-childhood education has a greater long-term effect on social mobility than university funding, and it proposes that governments reallocate part of their higher-education budgets accordingly.*

The first is a warm-up. The second is a **map**.

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 Features of academic English
| Feature | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Formal register** | no slang, no contractions, no phrasal-verb overload | *investigate* rather than *look into* (usually) |
| **Precision** | exact words, specific claims | *a 12% increase*, not *a lot more* |
| **Caution (hedging)** | claims are proportionate to the evidence | *The results suggest that…* |
| **Objectivity** | focus on ideas, not feelings | *This approach has limitations.* |
| **Explicit structure** | thesis, topic sentences, signposts | *First, … Second, … Finally, …* |
| **Evidence** | sources, data, examples | *(Author, Year)* |
| **Cohesion** | clear links between sentences | *however, therefore, this suggests* |
| **Concision** | no padding | *because*, not *due to the fact that* |
| **Consistency** | tense, spelling variety, citation style | all UK or all US |

### 2.2 Contrastive tendencies: English vs. Spanish academic prose
Researchers in *contrastive rhetoric* have described differences in how academic texts are organized across languages and cultures. These are **tendencies, not rules**, they vary by discipline and writer, and the field itself is debated. Still, they explain common reader reactions to Spanish-influenced English essays.

| Tendency in many English academic texts | Tendency in some Spanish academic texts | Effect if you transfer |
|---|---|---|
| Thesis stated early, clearly | Thesis may emerge gradually; background first | English reader asks, "What's the point?" |
| One main idea per paragraph, topic sentence first | Paragraphs can develop several related ideas | paragraph feels unfocused |
| Shorter to medium sentences (often 15–25 words) | Longer sentences with many subordinate clauses are valued | sentences are hard to follow |
| Direct link of every part to the thesis | Digressions and context can show erudition | reader loses the thread |
| Writer is responsible for clarity | Reader is expected to work harder | English reader sees "unclear writing" |
| Plain, precise vocabulary preferred | Elaborate, ornate style can signal sophistication | sounds inflated or vague |
| Limited repetition of the same point | Repetition and paraphrase for emphasis | sounds redundant |
| Explicit transitions | Transitions often implicit | argument seems disconnected |

The point is not that one is better. It is that you need the English pattern when writing for English-speaking readers.

### 2.3 Essay structure
```
Introduction   hook/context → background (brief) → thesis statement
Body paragraph 1   topic sentence → evidence → explanation → link
Body paragraph 2   topic sentence → evidence → explanation → link
Body paragraph 3   topic sentence → evidence → explanation → link
(Counter-argument and response, if needed)
Conclusion     restate thesis (new words) → summarize → implications/next steps
```

**Introduction (about 10% of the text)**
1. **Context/hook:** one or two sentences placing the topic (avoid "Since the beginning of time…" and "In today's society…").
2. **Background:** what the reader needs to know, briefly.
3. **Gap or problem:** what is unresolved or debated.
4. **Thesis:** your main claim (and, often, the plan of the essay).

**Conclusion (about 10%)**
1. Restate the thesis in fresh words (not a copy).
2. Summarize the key points (briefly).
3. Say why it matters (implications, limits, further research).
4. **No new evidence.**

### 2.4 Thesis statements
A good thesis is **arguable, specific, and focused.**

| ❌ Weak | Problem | ✅ Strong |
|---|---|---|
| This essay is about social media. | topic only | This essay argues that social media increases political polarization mainly by algorithmic filtering rather than by user choice. |
| Climate change is bad. | obvious, vague | Carbon pricing is more effective than subsidies for reducing emissions in developing economies because it generates revenue for adaptation. |
| There are advantages and disadvantages of remote work. | no position | Although remote work improves productivity for individual tasks, it weakens mentoring and long-term innovation. |

**Formula:** *Although/While [counterpoint], [your claim] because [main reason(s)].*

### 2.5 Paragraphs: the PEEL pattern
**P**oint → **E**vidence → **E**xplanation → **L**ink

> **Point:** Remote work improves productivity for tasks that require concentration.
> **Evidence:** In a company survey of 1,200 employees (invented for practice), 68% reported fewer interruptions at home.
> **Explanation:** Fewer interruptions allow longer periods of focus, which are essential for complex analysis.
> **Link:** However, this advantage must be weighed against the loss of informal collaboration.

**Topic sentence rules:** state the paragraph's single claim; connect to the thesis; avoid starting with a source or a statistic alone.

**Paragraph length:** usually 100–200 words (5–9 sentences). A one-sentence paragraph is rarely adequate; a 400-word paragraph is usually two paragraphs.

### 2.6 Cohesion: linking ideas
Academic prose uses **connectors** and **reference words**, but not too many.

| Function | Connectors |
|---|---|
| Adding | *in addition, furthermore, moreover, also* (*moreover/furthermore* sparingly) |
| Contrasting | *however, in contrast, nevertheless, whereas, while, although* |
| Concession | *although, even though, despite, in spite of* |
| Cause | *because, since, as, due to, owing to, as a result of* |
| Result | *therefore, thus, consequently, as a result, hence* |
| Example | *for example, for instance, such as, namely* |
| Sequence | *first, second, finally, subsequently, then* |
| Emphasis | *indeed, in fact, notably* |
| Summary | *in conclusion, overall, to sum up, in short* |
| Reformulation | *in other words, that is, i.e.* |

**Cautions**
- *However* usually begins a sentence followed by a comma, or sits after a semicolon: *…; however, …*.
- *Moreover/furthermore* are formal and often overused; vary them.
- *On the other hand* needs a partner (*On the one hand…*), or use *In contrast*.
- *Because* is perfectly acceptable at the start of a sentence in academic English.
- Avoid *Also* at the start of a sentence in formal writing; *In addition* is safer.

**Reference words** (*this, these, such, the latter, the former*): always make clear what they refer to (*This finding…, not just This…*).

### 2.7 Hedging and strength of claims
Academic writers match the **strength of the language to the strength of the evidence.**

| Strength | Examples |
|---|---|
| Strong (well-established) | *The data show…, It is clear that…, X causes Y.* |
| Moderate | *The evidence suggests…, This indicates that…, It is likely that…* |
| Weak (tentative) | *It may be that…, One possible explanation is…, This could imply…* |
| Limited | *tends to, appears to, seems to, in most cases, to some extent* |

| ❌ Overclaim | ✅ Calibrated |
|---|---|
| Social media destroys democracy. | Social media may contribute to political polarization. |
| All students prefer online classes. | Most students in the sample preferred online classes. |
| This proves that the theory is right. | These findings support the theory. |
| Everybody knows that… | It is widely accepted that… (with a source) |

**Hedging tools:** modal verbs (*may, might, could*), verbs (*suggest, indicate, appear, tend*), adverbs (*probably, possibly, generally, relatively*), phrases (*to some extent, in most cases*), and impersonal structures (*It seems that…*).

*Don't over-hedge:* a sentence with five hedges says nothing. One or two per claim is enough (Lesson 59).

### 2.8 Objectivity, voice, and "I"
- **First person:** acceptable in many humanities and social science writing (*I argue that…*), and in some sciences for methods (*We measured…*). Check your discipline and your instructor.
- **The passive** is common in methods and results (*Samples were collected…*), but overuse makes writing heavy. Prefer active voice when the agent matters (*The authors argue…*).
- **Avoid** emotional or judgmental words (*terrible, amazing, obviously*), rhetorical questions (*Is this fair?*), and direct address (*you*).
- **Avoid** overgeneralization (*people, everyone, society says*) unless supported.

### 2.9 Sentences: clarity over length
**Principles**
1. **Put the main idea in the main clause.**
2. **Keep subject and verb close.**
3. **Prefer 15–25 words on average**, with variety.
4. **One idea per sentence** when ideas are complex.
5. **Known information first, new information last** (end-weight).

| ❌ Overloaded | ✅ Clearer |
|---|---|
| The government, which in the last decade has been implementing, with varying degrees of success, a series of policies whose aim is the reduction of inequality, has not managed to... | Over the last decade, the government has implemented several policies to reduce inequality. However, their success has been limited. |
| It is a fact that many people nowadays think that education is important. | Education is widely seen as essential to economic opportunity. |

### 2.10 Vocabulary: academic style
**Register shifts**
| Informal | Academic |
|---|---|
| a lot of | many / a large number of / considerable |
| get | obtain / receive / become |
| look into | investigate / examine |
| show | demonstrate / indicate / illustrate |
| big | substantial / considerable / significant |
| bad | negative / detrimental / adverse |
| thing | factor / aspect / element |
| kids | children / young people |
| about | approximately / regarding |
| use | employ / utilize (sparingly) |
| enough | sufficient |
| find out | determine / identify |
| start | begin / initiate |
| need | require |

But remember Lesson 71: **do not inflate**. *Use* is usually better than *utilize*. Academic English values clarity; Latinate words are fine when they are precise.

**Academic Word List (AWL).** Coxhead's Academic Word List (2000) collects about 570 word families that appear across university texts (e.g., *analyze, concept, data, environment, factor, indicate, interpret, significant, method, research, theory*). Many are cognates for you. Learn their **collocations and grammar**, not just meaning.

**Nominalization (turning verbs/adjectives into nouns)** is typical:
- *The government decided → The government's decision*
- *Prices increased sharply → There was a sharp increase in prices*
Use it for concision and formality, but avoid chains of nouns (*the implementation of the evaluation of the reduction of…*).

**Reporting verbs** (to introduce sources):
| Neutral | *states, notes, observes, reports, describes* |
|---|---|
| Strong | *argues, claims, maintains, contends, asserts* |
| Evidence-based | *demonstrates, shows, finds, reveals, confirms* |
| Tentative | *suggests, proposes, implies, hypothesizes* |
| Critical | *questions, challenges, disputes, overlooks* |

Choose carefully: *claims* may imply doubt; *demonstrates* implies strong support.

### 2.11 Grammar points that matter on the page
| Area | Guideline |
|---|---|
| **Tense** | Present for general truths and your argument; past for completed studies; present perfect for ongoing relevance (*Researchers have shown…*). Be consistent. |
| **Articles** | Generic plural nouns take no article (*Education improves…*). *The* for specific, previously mentioned things. Don't write *the society*, *the life* for general ideas. |
| **Uncountable nouns** | *research, evidence, information, advice, knowledge, feedback, equipment* are uncountable (*much research*, *a piece of evidence*). |
| **Subject–verb agreement** | *data* is plural in formal science (*the data show*); many now accept singular. Follow your field. |
| **Prepositions** | *different from/than (US), interested in, depend on, result in/from, contribute to, focus on, access to.* |
| **Relative clauses** | *that* (restrictive, no comma); *which* (non-restrictive, with comma). |
| **Parallelism** | Keep lists in the same form (*reading, writing, and speaking*, not *reading, to write, and speaking*). |
| **Dangling modifiers** | ❌ *Using this method, the results improved.* ✅ *Using this method, researchers improved the results.* |
| **Contractions** | Avoid in formal academic writing (*do not*, not *don't*). |

### 2.12 Punctuation: where Spanish habits differ
| Issue | Spanish tendency | English academic norm |
|---|---|---|
| **Comma after introductory phrases** | less used | *However,* … *In 2020,* … *Although X,* … |
| **Comma between subject and verb** | sometimes used before long subjects | ❌ never (*The students, wrote*) |
| **Comma splice** | commas can join clauses | ❌ *The results were clear, they supported the theory.* → ✅ use a period, semicolon, or conjunction |
| **Serial (Oxford) comma** | normally not used | optional in English; common in US (*A, B, and C*); be consistent |
| **Semicolon** | used in lists and long sentences | joins closely related independent clauses; separates complex list items |
| **Colon** | introduces lists, quotes | introduces an explanation after a complete clause |
| **Quotation marks** | « » or " " | " " (US) or ' ' (UK, single); periods/commas inside (US) |
| **Decimals/thousands** | 1.250,5 | 1,250.5 |
| **Question marks** | ¿…? | only ? at the end |
| **Capitalization** | titles capitalize only the first word | Title Case in many English titles (check style) |
| **Dates and numbers** | 5 de mayo de 2025 | 5 May 2025 / May 5, 2025; spell out one to nine in many styles |

### 2.13 Using sources: quoting, paraphrasing, citing
**Why cite:** to give credit, support claims, show you know the field, and avoid plagiarism.

**Three ways to use a source**
| Method | When | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Quote** | exact wording matters; striking phrase | quotation marks + citation + page (where required); keep short |
| **Paraphrase** | most of the time | restate in your own words and structure + citation |
| **Summary** | to condense a long source | main ideas only + citation |

**Paraphrasing well** means changing both **words and structure**, keeping meaning, and citing. Replacing a few words is not enough (patchwriting).

> *Original (invented for practice):* "Students who receive regular feedback show improved performance over a semester." (Alvarez, 2021)
> ❌ Patchwriting: *Students who get regular feedback show better performance during a semester.* (too close)
> ✅ Paraphrase: *Alvarez (2021) found that frequent feedback is associated with better results across a term.*

**Citation systems** (follow your institution's guidance)
| Style | Typical fields | In-text example |
|---|---|---|
| **APA** (7th ed.) | social sciences, education, psychology | (Alvarez, 2021) / Alvarez (2021) |
| **MLA** (9th ed.) | humanities, languages, literature | (Alvarez 45) |
| **Chicago** | history, humanities (notes-bibliography or author–date) | footnote¹ / (Alvarez 2021) |
| **IEEE / Vancouver** | engineering, medicine | [1] |

**Plagiarism** includes copying text, close paraphrase without citation, reusing your own previous work without permission, and using AI-generated or translated text without acknowledgment where rules require it. Universities take it seriously; learn your institution's policy. Note that translating a source from Spanish into English still requires citation.

**Reference list vs. bibliography:** a reference list includes only works cited; a bibliography may include works consulted.

### 2.14 Types of academic texts
| Type | Purpose | Key features |
|---|---|---|
| **Argumentative essay** | persuade with reasons | thesis, evidence, counter-argument |
| **Compare/contrast** | analyze similarities/differences | clear criteria, balanced structure |
| **Cause/effect** | explain relationships | careful claims about causation |
| **Problem/solution** | propose solutions | evaluate options |
| **Literature review** | synthesize research | organize by theme, not by source |
| **Report** | present findings | sections: intro, methods, results, discussion |
| **Abstract** | summarize | 150–250 words; background, aim, method, results, conclusion |
| **Research article (IMRaD)** | present research | Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion |
| **Reflective writing** | analyze experience | personal voice, but analytical |
| **Critical review** | evaluate a text | summary + judgment with evidence |

## 3. Why Native Speakers Do This
1. **Educational tradition.** English-speaking schools teach the five-paragraph essay and thesis-driven writing early, so readers expect them.
2. **Reader efficiency.** Academics read huge volumes; a clear thesis and topic sentences let them skim.
3. **Scientific culture of caution.** Claims must be proportionate to evidence, so hedging is a sign of rigor, not weakness.
4. **Objectivity norm.** Shifting attention from the writer to the evidence supports claims of neutrality.
5. **Citation culture.** Intellectual property and attribution are central, and plagiarism is a major offense.
6. **Style guides.** APA, MLA, and Chicago standardize format so readers can find sources and compare studies.
7. **Peer review.** Writing is evaluated by strangers, so it must be self-explanatory.

## 4. When to Use / When Not To

**Use academic conventions when**
- writing essays, theses, dissertations, journal articles, conference papers;
- writing formal reports, white papers, policy briefs;
- applying for scholarships or graduate programs (statements often blend academic and personal style);
- presenting research orally (the structure carries over).

**Adapt when**
| Context | Adjust |
|---|---|
| Business report | shorter, more action-oriented, executive summary first |
| Blog or newsletter | conversational, fewer citations, more first person |
| Personal statement | personal voice, specific stories, still clear structure |
| Email to a professor | polite, brief (Lesson 72), not essay style |
| Conference abstract | strict word limits; high density |

**Don't**
- imitate old-fashioned or inflated prose;
- pile up long Latinate noun phrases;
- hedge every sentence;
- start paragraphs with "In today's society…";
- use contractions, slang, rhetorical questions in formal work;
- cite without understanding the source;
- rely on translation tools without checking register and collocations.

## 5. Frequency & Priority

**Priorities for you (in order)**
1. **Thesis statement and topic sentences.**
2. **Paragraph structure** (PEEL).
3. **Sentence length control** (break long sentences).
4. **Hedging and calibration** (match claims to evidence).
5. **Accurate punctuation** (comma after introductory phrases, no comma splices).
6. **Cohesion** (transitions, reference words).
7. **Articles, uncountables, prepositions** in abstract writing.
8. **Citation and paraphrasing** skills.
9. **Academic vocabulary and collocations.**
10. **Proofreading and consistency** (spelling variety, tense, style).

## 6. American vs. British Notes
| Feature | US | UK |
|---|---|---|
| Spelling | *analyze, organization, behavior, center* | *analyse, organisation, behaviour, centre* |
| Dates | *May 5, 2025* | *5 May 2025* |
| Quotation marks | " " double (single inside) | ' ' single (double inside) |
| Punctuation with quotes | periods/commas inside quotes | logical punctuation (inside only if part of the quote) |
| Serial comma | common in many guides (*A, B, and C*) | often omitted |
| Terminology | *thesis* (master's) / *dissertation* (doctoral) | *dissertation* (undergraduate/master's) / *thesis* (often doctoral) — varies |
| Department terms | *professor, faculty, grade, major* | *lecturer, staff, mark, course/degree* |
| Preferred styles | APA, MLA, Chicago | Harvard (author–date), Oxford footnotes, APA |
| Tone | direct, clear thesis | similar; sometimes more understated |
| Words | *different from/than*, *fall*, *gotten* | *different from/to*, *autumn*, *got* |

Choose one variety and keep spelling, punctuation, and vocabulary consistent (set your word processor's language accordingly).

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

### 7.1 Structure and argument
| ❌ Habit | Effect | ✅ Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Starting with "Since the beginning of time…" / "In today's society…" | vague, clichéd | start with a specific context or problem |
| Thesis at the end, or missing | reader lost | thesis at the end of the introduction |
| Paragraphs with several ideas | unfocused | one idea per paragraph |
| No topic sentences | hard to skim | state the claim first |
| Long background before the point | slow | brief background |
| Describing instead of analyzing | no argument | add "so what?" explanation |
| Quoting long passages | thin analysis | paraphrase; short quotes |
| Repeating the same idea in synonyms | redundant | say it once, then develop |
| Concluding with new ideas | untidy | summarize and close |
| Overusing "In my opinion" | weak, personal | present reasoning and evidence |
| Implicit transitions | disconnected | add connectors |

| ❌ | ✅ |
|---|---|
| However the results were clear. | However, the results were clear. |
| The results were clear, they supported the theory. | The results were clear; they supported the theory. |
| The students, who passed the exam, were happy. (restrictive meaning) | The students who passed the exam were happy. |

| It is important to note that, the results… | It is important to note that the results… |

### 7.4 Attitude traps
- **"Longer, more complex sentences show intelligence."** In English academic writing, clarity shows intelligence.
- **"More Latin words = more academic."** Precision matters more than length.
- **"If I paraphrase, I don't need to cite."** You always cite ideas.
- **"Hedging is weak."** It is a sign of careful thinking.
- **"My first draft should be perfect."** Academic writing is revised in stages.
- **"I will write in Spanish and translate."** It usually keeps Spanish structure; plan in English with outlines.

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ **"Academic English means complicated English."** — It means precise, organized English.
- ❌ **"Never use 'I'."** — Many fields allow it; check your discipline.
- ❌ **"Never use the passive."** — It is useful in methods and results; just don't overuse it.
- ❌ **"Never start a sentence with 'because' or 'however'."** — Both are acceptable (*However,* needs a comma).
- ❌ **"Never end a sentence with a preposition."** — A style myth; avoid awkwardness, not prepositions.
- ❌ **"One paragraph = five sentences."** — Length depends on development.
- ❌ **"The conclusion repeats the introduction."** — It restates and extends; it doesn't copy.
- ❌ **"Quotes make an essay look academic."** — Analysis does.
- ❌ **"Citing many sources guarantees quality."** — Selection and synthesis matter.
- ❌ **"Using a thesaurus makes writing sophisticated."** — Wrong words appear; check collocations.
- ❌ **"A comma goes wherever you pause."** — Commas follow grammar, not breath.
- ❌ **"Hedges mean I'm unsure of my argument."** — They show proportion between claim and evidence.
- ❌ **"AI or translation tools make my text academic."** — They can help with wording, but you remain responsible for accuracy, sources, and originality.

## 9. Pronunciation Note → Training Method (for writing)

### 9.1 Plan before drafting: the one-page outline
1. **Question or topic.**
2. **Thesis** (one sentence).
3. **3–4 main points**, each with one sentence of support and one source/example.
4. **Counter-argument** and your response.
5. **Conclusion idea.**
Outline in English, in short phrases (not Spanish sentences).

### 9.2 Reverse outline (after drafting)
For each paragraph, write one phrase summarizing its point in the margin. Check: does each paragraph have one idea? Do the points support the thesis in a logical order? Are there gaps or repetitions? Fix the structure before editing sentences.

### 9.3 Four editing passes
1. **Content and argument:** thesis, evidence, logic.
2. **Structure:** paragraphs, transitions, order.
3. **Sentences:** length, clarity, active/passive, repetition.
4. **Words and mechanics:** collocations, articles, prepositions, punctuation, spelling, citations.
Do one pass at a time.

### 9.4 The "so what?" test
After each piece of evidence, ask "So what?" and write the explanation. If you can't, the evidence may not belong.

### 9.5 Sentence-splitting drill
Take your longest sentences (30+ words). Split each into two or three. Then recombine the best into a 20-word sentence.

### 9.6 Hedge calibration
For each claim, ask: how strong is my evidence? Choose the verb (*shows / suggests / may indicate*) accordingly.

### 9.7 Model analysis
Take two academic texts from your field (journal articles). Highlight thesis, topic sentences, hedges, reporting verbs, connectors. Add the best phrases to a bank (*academic phrasebank*).

### 9.8 Paraphrase practice
Read a paragraph, close the text, write the idea from memory, then compare with the original and check for accuracy and for copied phrases.

### 9.9 Read aloud
Reading your text aloud (Lesson 66) reveals rhythm problems, overlong sentences, and missing connectors.

### 9.10 Feedback
Ask a teacher, writing center, or peer for feedback with specific questions: *Is my thesis clear? Which paragraph is hardest to follow?* Record patterns in an error log.

## 10. Memory Technique

### 10.1 Map, route, signposts
Thesis = map. Paragraphs = route. Transitions = signposts.

### 10.2 PEEL
**P**oint, **E**vidence, **E**xplanation, **L**ink.

### 10.3 "So what?"
After every fact: *So what?* Write the answer.

### 10.4 The 3-C check
**Clear** (one idea per sentence/paragraph), **Concise** (no padding), **Calibrated** (claims match evidence).

### 10.5 Comma rule of thumb
Comma after introductory phrase; no comma between subject and verb; no comma splice.

### 10.6 Reporting verb ladder
*notes → argues → claims → demonstrates*: from neutral to strong.

### 10.7 Spaced retrieval cards
- *Where does the thesis go?* → **end of the introduction**
- *What does PEEL stand for?* → **Point, Evidence, Explanation, Link**
- *Hedging purpose?* → **match claim strength to evidence**
- *"The data ___"* → **show (formal plural)**
- *Spanish "actualmente"?* → **currently / at present** (not "actually")
- *Spanish "carrera" (university)?* → **degree / program / major**
- *Comma splice fix?* → **period, semicolon, or conjunction**
- *Paraphrase requires?* → **new words, new structure, same meaning, plus citation**
Review on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30.

### 10.8 Phrasebank
Keep a categorized list: introducing a topic, stating a thesis, describing evidence, hedging, contrasting, concluding. Review monthly.

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1: Thesis early; every paragraph supports it.**

> **Pattern 2: One idea per paragraph; the topic sentence states it.**

> **Pattern 3: Claim → evidence → explanation → link.**

> **Pattern 4: Calibrate claims with hedging.**

> **Pattern 5: Shorter sentences, main idea first.**

> **Pattern 6: Explicit connectors and reference words create cohesion.**

> **Pattern 7: Formal register: no contractions, slang, or rhetorical questions.**

> **Pattern 8: Use precise, plain academic vocabulary; avoid inflation.**

> **Pattern 9: Generic nouns: no article (*Education…*, *Society…*).**

> **Pattern 10: Punctuation follows grammar: comma after introductory phrases, no splices.**

> **Pattern 11: Cite ideas as well as quotations; paraphrase fully.**

> **Pattern 12: Revise in layers: argument → structure → sentences → words.**

## 12. Real-Life Examples

### 12.1 Introduction: before and after
> ❌ *Nowadays, technology is very important in the life of the people and many experts say different opinions about if it is good or bad. In this essay I am going to talk about the advantages and disadvantages of technology in education.*
> ✅ *Digital tools have become widespread in classrooms, yet their effect on learning remains disputed. This essay argues that technology improves learning only when it supports active, collaborative tasks, and that its benefits are smaller when it replaces teacher interaction.*

### 12.2 Body paragraph (PEEL), with invented evidence
> **Technology is most effective when it promotes collaboration.** In a hypothetical case study of two schools, students who used shared online documents completed group projects 20% faster than those who worked individually (invented for practice). This suggests that technology can reduce coordination costs and encourage participation. However, the same advantage is unlikely when students use devices mainly for passive consumption of content.

### 12.3 Hedging examples
| Claim | Calibrated version |
|---|---|
| Online learning is worse than classroom learning. | Online learning may be less effective for younger students, although evidence is mixed. |
| This policy caused unemployment to fall. | This policy appears to have contributed to a fall in unemployment. |
| It is obvious that inequality is increasing. | Available data indicate that inequality has increased over the past decade. |

### 12.4 Reporting verbs in sentences
- *Smith (2020) **argues** that…*
- *The authors **demonstrate** that…*
- *Lopez and Kim **suggest** that…*
- *Some researchers **question** whether…*
- *According to Alvarez (2021), …*
(All names here are invented for practice.)

### 12.5 Transitions in a mini-paragraph
> Remote work has clear benefits. **First**, it reduces commuting time. **In addition**, many employees report higher job satisfaction. **However**, these advantages are not evenly distributed; **for example**, workers with limited space at home may find it difficult to concentrate. **Therefore**, employers should offer flexible options rather than a single policy.

### 12.6 Conclusion: before and after
> ❌ *In conclusion, as I said before, technology has advantages and disadvantages and everybody has to decide for himself. Also, I think that more research is necessary about other aspects not mentioned here.*
> ✅ *In conclusion, technology can enhance learning when it fosters collaboration, but it cannot replace skilled teaching. Policy-makers should therefore invest in teacher training alongside devices. Future research could examine long-term effects across different age groups.*

### 12.7 Sentence improvement
| Before | After |
|---|---|
| In relation with the problem of the unemployment in the youth, it can be said that it is a thing that has many causes. | Youth unemployment has several causes. |
| It is evident that the majority of the persons consider that the education is fundamental. | Most people regard education as fundamental. |
| The realization of the study was done by the researchers during three months. | The researchers conducted the study over three months. |

### 12.8 Abstract model (150–200 words, invented example)
> *Background:* Remote work expanded rapidly after 2020, but its effects on employee well-being remain unclear. *Aim:* This study examines the relationship between remote-work frequency and reported job satisfaction. *Method:* A survey of 400 employees (hypothetical) was analyzed using regression. *Results:* Moderate remote work (2–3 days per week) was associated with the highest satisfaction. *Conclusion:* Flexible, hybrid arrangements may balance autonomy and collaboration.

## 13. Collocations & Related Expressions

**Academic vocabulary**
- *argue, claim, contend, maintain, assert, suggest, propose, hypothesize, demonstrate, illustrate, indicate, reveal, imply, infer, conclude, acknowledge, critique, evaluate, justify, support, challenge*
- *a claim, an argument, a thesis, a hypothesis, evidence, data, findings, results, a source, a citation, a reference, a bibliography, an abstract, a literature review, methodology, a limitation, an implication*
- *evidence suggests, evidence supports, findings indicate, data show, results confirm, studies have shown, research has found, it can be argued that, it is widely believed that, this raises the question of*
- *peer-reviewed, scholarly, empirical, theoretical, qualitative, quantitative, rigorous, robust, significant, substantial, marginal, plausible, persuasive, compelling, tentative*

**Useful academic phrases**
| Function | Phrases |
|---|---|
| Introducing | *This essay examines… / This paper argues that… / The aim of this study is to…* |
| Background | *Recent research has focused on… / It is widely accepted that…* |
| Gap | *However, little attention has been paid to…* |
| Thesis | *This essay argues that… / The central claim of this paper is that…* |
| Evidence | *For instance, … / Evidence for this comes from… / This is supported by…* |
| Explaining | *This suggests that… / This is because… / This implies…* |
| Contrast | *Nevertheless, … / In contrast, … / While X argues…, Y claims…* |
| Concession | *Although it is true that…, … / Admittedly, … , …* |
| Limitation | *A limitation of this study is… / These findings should be interpreted with caution.* |
| Concluding | *In conclusion, … / Overall, … / Taken together, these findings suggest…* |

**Contrast groups**
| Items | Difference |
|---|---|
| *thesis / dissertation / essay* | varies by country: long research document (master's/doctoral) / shorter text |
| *argue / claim / suggest / demonstrate* | present reasons / assert / propose tentatively / show with strong evidence |
| *paraphrase / summarize / quote* | restate ideas / condense / copy exactly |
| *reference list / bibliography* | works cited / works cited and consulted |
| *abstract / summary / conclusion* | short overview of the whole paper / condensed content / final section |
| *affect / effect* | verb (influence) / noun (result) |
| *principal / principle* | main (adjective/noun) / rule |
| *e.g. / i.e.* | for example / that is |
