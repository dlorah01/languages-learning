# Lesson 45 — Phrasal Verbs with TAKE
### Phase 4: Phrasal Verbs | Module 2 of 7

---

## 0. Where this fits
Lesson 30 taught you TAKE as a high-frequency verb (take a break, take time, take place). Lesson 44 gave you the **transparency spectrum** for phrasal verbs, using GET. This lesson applies that same lens to TAKE, and adds one new grammar skill that matters for *every* phrasal verb from now on: **separability** — where the object goes. Lessons 46–50 will rely on it.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
Picture TAKE as a hand that **moves something toward itself or into its own control**. The particle then tells you *what happens next*:

- **take + off / out / away / down** → the hand **removes** something (from a body, a place, a surface).
- **take + on / over / up** → the hand **accepts** something (a job, a challenge, a responsibility, a hobby).
- **take + in** → the hand pulls something **inside** (food, information, a person, a deception).
- **take + after / to / for** → the hand reaches toward a **person** (resemblance, liking, mistaken identity).
- **take + back / apart** → the hand **reverses or dismantles** (retract a statement, return a product, disassemble a machine).

You don't memorize 40 separate verbs. You memorize **five families** and let the particle's direction predict the meaning.

---

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 Five families

| Family | Core idea | Phrasal verbs |
|---|---|---|
| **Removal** | something leaves its place | take **off**, take **out**, take **away**, take **down** |
| **Accepting** | something comes under your control | take **on**, take **over**, take **up**, take **up on** |
| **Absorbing** | something goes inside | take **in**, take **aback** |
| **Relationship** | between people | take **after**, take **to**, take **for** |
| **Reversal / dismantling** | undo or break apart | take **back**, take **apart** |

### 2.2 Meanings with examples

**Removal**
- **take off** — (clothes) remove: *Take off your shoes.* / (plane) leave the ground: *The flight took off late.* / (business, idea) suddenly succeed: *Her podcast really took off.* / (time) have as a break: *I'm taking Friday off.*
- **take out** — remove from inside; also invite on a date; also obtain: *He took out a loan.*
- **take away** — remove or subtract: *Take away the cost of shipping and it's cheap.* Also the key idea behind a "takeaway" (a main lesson): *the key takeaway.*
- **take down** — lower something, dismantle, or write notes: *Let me take down your number.* / *They took down the post.*

**Accepting**
- **take on** — accept a task, employee, opponent, or quality: *We took on two new clients.* / *She took on too much.*
- **take over** — assume control: *He took over the company in 2019.*
- **take up** — begin a hobby/activity; occupy space or time: *She took up climbing.* / *This sofa takes up half the room.*
- **take up on** — accept an offer someone made: *I'll take you up on that offer.* (Three words — very natural in speech.)

**Absorbing**
- **take in** — absorb information; deceive; let someone stay; make clothes narrower: *I couldn't take it all in.* / *He was taken in by a scam.*
- **take aback** — shock or surprise (almost always passive): *I was taken aback by her tone.*

**Relationship**
- **take after** — resemble a family member in character or looks: *You take after your father.*
- **take to** — start liking: *I took to him immediately.* (Also: begin a habit — *She took to running at dawn.*)
- **take for** — mistakenly believe someone is something: *I took him for a teacher.*

**Reversal / dismantling**
- **take back** — retract, return, or recall: *I take it back.* / *I'm taking this shirt back to the store.*
- **take apart** — disassemble, or demolish an argument/opponent: *He took the engine apart.* / *The lawyer took his story apart.*

### 2.3 Separability — the new grammar skill

Phrasal verbs behave in three ways with objects:

| Type | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Separable** | A noun object can go **in the middle or after**. A **pronoun** object must go **in the middle**. | *take off your coat / take your coat off / take it off* ✅ — *take off it* ❌ |
| **Inseparable** | The object always comes **after** the whole verb. | *take after her mother* ✅ — *take her mother after* ❌ |
| **Intransitive** | No object at all. | *The plane took off.* |

**Which TAKE verbs are which?**

| Phrasal verb | Type | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| take off (clothes) | Separable | *take it off* |
| take off (plane / idea) | Intransitive | *Sales took off.* |
| take out | Separable | *take the trash out / take it out* |
| take away | Separable | *take them away* |
| take down | Separable | *take it down* |
| take on | Separable | *take them on* (clients) |
| take over | Both | *take over the company / take it over / The new CEO took over.* |
| take up | Separable | *take it up* (hobby) |
| take up on | Special | Pattern: **take someone up on something** |
| take in | Separable | *take it in* |
| take aback | Separable (passive usage) | *It took me aback.* |
| take after | **Inseparable** | *take after him* |
| take to | **Inseparable** | *take to it* |
| take for | **Inseparable** (+ person + noun) | *take him for a fool* |
| take back | Separable | *take it back* |
| take apart | Separable | *take it apart* |

**Rule of thumb:** verbs of *relationship between people* (after, to, for) are inseparable. Verbs of *moving objects* (off, out, away, down, back, apart) are almost always separable.

## 3. Why Native Speakers Use Them
Phrasal verbs with TAKE cover the exact territory where Latinate alternatives feel stiff:

| Phrasal (natural) | Formal alternative (stiffer) |
|---|---|
| take on a project | undertake a project |
| take over | assume control of |
| take down | dismantle / record |
| take in information | assimilate information |
| take after | resemble |
| take back a statement | retract a statement |

Natives choose the phrasal version by default; the Latinate one signals **writing, law, or a deliberate formal tone**. Using only the Latinate forms makes you sound like a textbook; using only phrasal forms in a formal report sounds casual. Choose by register, not habit.

---

## 4. When to Use / When Not To
- **Use** phrasal TAKE verbs in conversation, interviews, emails, workplace talk, news, and most spoken narrative.
- **Be careful** in very formal academic or legal writing: prefer *undertake, assume, resemble, retract, absorb* where the tone demands it.
- **Don't** use *take up on* as a general synonym for "accept." It only works for **offers, invitations, or challenges** made to you.
- **Don't** treat *take off* as one meaning. It has four, and context decides.

## 6. American vs. British Notes
- **US:** *takeout* (food you carry out) — "Let's order takeout." **UK:** *takeaway* — "Let's get a takeaway."
- **Take-away** as a **lesson learned** is used in both varieties in business English: *"The key takeaway is..."*
- **UK:** *take the mickey* (tease), *take the biscuit* (be the worst), *take a punt* (gamble). **US:** *take a rain check* (postpone politely) is distinctly American.
- **UK** commonly says *take a decision*; **US** prefers *make a decision* (revisit Lesson 3).
- **Take off** (a day): both say *take a day off*; Britons also say *take a day's leave / annual leave*.

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

1. **Wrong pronoun position**
   - ❌ *Take off it.* / *Take out them.*
   - ✅ *Take it off.* / *Take them out.*
   - *Why:* Spanish clitics go before the verb (*quítatelo*), so you have no habit of "pronoun inside the verb phrase." In English, pronouns in separable verbs get **sandwiched**.

2. **Adding "of" to *take after***
   - ❌ *She takes after of her mother.*
   - ✅ *She takes after her mother.*
   - *Why:* Spanish *salir a* / *parecerse a* / *sacar de* may pull in prepositions. English *after* already is the preposition.

3. **Confusing take off and take out**
   - ❌ *Take out your jacket* (when you mean remove it from your body).
   - ✅ *Take off your jacket.*
   - *Rule:* **off** = from a surface/body; **out** = from inside a container.
   - ✅ *Take your keys out of your pocket.*

4. **take up + infinitive**
   - ❌ *I took up to play guitar.*
   - ✅ *I took up the guitar.* / *I took up playing the guitar.* / *I took up guitar.*
   - *Why:* Spanish *empezar a + infinitivo* seduces you to use *to*. *Take up* needs a noun or gerund.

5. **Literal translation of *tomar***
   - ❌ *I took the offer up* for "I accepted the invitation." (grammatically ok but unnatural)
   - ✅ *I'll take you up on that.*
   - ❌ *take a photo of me out* ✅ *take a photo of me* (don't add particles that aren't needed).

6. **Passive confusion with *take aback***
   - ❌ *I was aback.* / *It surprised aback me.*
   - ✅ *I was taken aback.* / *Her reply took me aback.*

7. **Using *take for* where Spanish uses *tomar por***
   - ❌ *Do you take me as stupid?*
   - ✅ *Do you take me for an idiot?* / *Do you think I'm stupid?*

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ "Phrasal verbs are informal slang." — Most are neutral and central to professional English: *take on responsibility, take over a project, take down notes*.
- ❌ "You can always split the verb and particle." — Only separable ones. *Take after* never splits.
- ❌ "Each phrasal verb is one meaning." — *Take off* alone has at least four meanings (clothes, plane, success, time off).
- ❌ "If I know *take* and *off*, I can guess *take off*." — Only partly. Removal meanings are transparent (Lesson 44's spectrum: literal end). *Take aback* and *take to* sit at the **idiomatic end** and must be learned as units.

---

## 9. Pronunciation Note
In phrasal verbs, the **particle usually receives the stress**:

- *take **OFF*** (/teɪk ˈɔːf/), *take **OVER***, *take **IN***, *take **UP***.

But when the verb **becomes a noun**, stress shifts to the **first syllable**:

| Verb (particle stressed) | Noun (verb part stressed) |
|---|---|
| to take **OFF** | a **TAKE**off |
| to take **OVER** | a **TAKE**over |
| to take **OUT** | **TAKE**out |
| to take **AWAY** | **TAKE**away |

Also practice the **linking**: *take it off* → /teɪ ki toff/ ("tay-ki-toff"). Native speakers don't pause between words; the /k/ attaches to *it*, and the /t/ attaches to *off*. This connected rhythm (Lesson 1) is why "take-it-off" sounds like one word.

## 10. Memory Technique

### Direction-of-particle map
Link the particle to a **physical direction**, then to the meaning:

| Particle | Direction | Typical meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **off** | away from a surface | removal, departure, sudden success |
| **out** | away from inside | extraction |
| **away** | away from here | removal / subtraction |
| **down** | lowering | writing, dismantling |
| **on** | toward you / onto you | accepting |
| **over** | across to you | assuming control |
| **up** | rising / filling space | beginning, occupying |
| **in** | into you | absorbing |
| **back** | returning | retraction |
| **apart** | splitting | dismantling |

### Three tricks
1. **Draw it.** For each family, sketch a stick-figure hand with an arrow. Visual encoding beats word lists.
2. **The "person vs object" test** for separability: if the object is a **person you resemble or like** → inseparable. If it's a **thing you move** → separable.
3. **Spaced-retrieval card:** write *take ___ your jacket* on the front, answer *off* on the back; then *take ___ a hobby* → *up*; *take ___ your father* → *after*. Review on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30 (spaced repetition).

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1:** **take on = accept + something heavy.** Natives use it for *work, responsibility, challenge, opponent, employee, accent, new meaning*. If it can be a "load," you can *take it on*.

> **Pattern 2:** **take + [person] + up on + [offer].** The structure is fixed. *"I'll take you up on that."* *"She took him up on his offer."*

> **Pattern 3:** **be taken + particle** is very common in the passive: *be taken aback, be taken in, be taken apart, be taken over, be taken ill (UK: become suddenly sick).*

> **Pattern 4:** **take X for Y** always means a **mistaken** belief: *I took her for her sister.* If you want a correct belief, use *think of as* or *see as*.

> **Connection to Lesson 35/36 (become-chart):** compare *take to* (start liking gradually) with *get used to* and *grow on* — all describe change in feeling, but only *take to* focuses on an **immediate** reaction.

> **Connection to Lesson 43 (dependent prepositions):** *take after, take to, take for* are inseparable because the particle behaves like a preposition tied to a person/noun, just like *depend on*.

## 12. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1 — Basic and transparent**
- Please **take off** your shoes.
- She **took out** her phone.
- Can you **take away** these plates?
- He **took down** the poster.

**Level 2 — Common daily use**
- The plane **took off** twenty minutes late.
- I'm **taking** Friday **off**.
- You really **take after** your dad.
- I'll **take back** what I said.
- My brother **took up** photography last year.

**Level 3 — Work and social life**
- We've **taken on** three new clients this quarter.
- She **took over** the team when Mark left.
- I'd love to **take you up on** that offer.
- Let me **take down** your details.
- Their startup really **took off** after the demo went viral.

**Level 4 — Abstract and idiomatic**
- I was **taken aback** by how direct the feedback was.
- He was **taken in** by a very convincing email.
- She **took to** the new city right away.
- Everyone **took him for** the manager.
- The journalist **took** the minister's argument **apart** in five minutes.

**Level 5 — Native-like, mixed registers**
- "I didn't want to **take on** more than I could handle, but the opportunity was too good to pass up."
- "She's so quiet that people **take her for** shy, but she's actually just observing."
- "After the merger, the company was **taken over** by a rival, and half the staff were let go."
- "It's a lot to **take in**; can we pause and go through it again?"

## 13. Collocations & Related Expressions

**Collocations**
- take **on** a challenge / responsibility / role / client / opponent
- take **over** a company / a role / a project / the world
- take **off** (the) weight, a day, a coat, a mask
- take **up** a hobby / a position / a lot of space / a cause
- take **in** the view / information / a stray / a deal
- take **out** a loan / insurance / a mortgage / the trash
- take **down** notes / a message / a statement / a website

**Related expressions**
- **takeaway** (n.) — a key lesson; or UK take-out food.
- **takeover** (n.) — acquisition of a company.
- **takeoff** (n.) — launch of a plane.
- **take it or leave it** — final offer.
- **take it easy** — relax.
- **take for granted** — fail to appreciate.
- **take someone's word for it** — believe without proof.
- **take it from me** — trust me.
- **take the lead** — go first / lead.

**Contrast with similar verbs**
| Idea | Verb | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Start a hobby | take up / pick up | *pick up* is more casual or accidental ("pick up Spanish"); *take up* is deliberate |
| Accept work | take on / accept | *take on* implies effort or weight |
| Resemble | take after / look like | *take after* = inherited traits, includes personality |
