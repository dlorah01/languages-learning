# Lesson 56 — Academic Vocabulary and Nominalization
### Phase 5: Vocabulary as a System | Module 6 of 8

---

## 0. Where this fits
Lesson 55 introduced the formal end of the register ladder. This lesson goes into the highest rung that most learners actually need: **academic English**, the language of essays, theses, journal articles, reports, conference talks, and serious journalism.

It is also the lesson that connects Phase 5 back to grammar. Academic style is not made mostly of "difficult words." It is made of a small number of **structural habits**:

1. **Nominalization:** packing actions and qualities into noun phrases (*the implementation of the policy*).
2. **Precise, low-frequency but widely shared vocabulary** (the "academic word" families: *analyse, assume, consistent, derive, factor, significant*).
3. **Hedging and stance:** claiming exactly as much as the evidence allows (*the results suggest*, *it appears that*).
4. **Impersonal, objective framing:** passives, abstract subjects, and reporting verbs (*It has been argued that…*).
5. **Cohesion through noun phrases:** referring back with *this development*, *such an approach*, *the latter*.

If you master these five habits, your writing moves from "correct" to "academic," even with a modest vocabulary. This lesson builds on Lessons 12 (passive), 21 (relative clauses), 25 (ellipsis and substitution), 26 (parallelism), 27 (punctuation), 28 (discourse markers), 52 (collocations), 53 (intensifiers), 54 (synonym precision), and 55 (register).

## 1. Intuitive Explanation
Compare two ways of saying the same thing.

> **Everyday:** *Because prices went up so quickly, people stopped buying as much, and a lot of small shops had to close.*
>
> **Academic:** *The rapid increase in prices led to a reduction in consumer spending, resulting in the closure of many small businesses.*

The second version is shorter, denser, and sounds "academic." Look at what changed:

- *prices went up quickly* → **the rapid increase in prices** (verb + adverb became noun + adjective)
- *people stopped buying* → **a reduction in consumer spending**
- *shops had to close* → **the closure of many small businesses**
- the loose connectors (*because… so… and*) became **verbs of cause** (*led to, resulting in*)

That is nominalization at work. Events became **things** (nouns) that can be counted, described, compared, and used as the subject of the next sentence. A sentence about *people doing things* became a sentence about *processes and their consequences*, which is what academic writing studies.

> **Core idea:** everyday English tells stories about **people doing things** (verbs). Academic English builds arguments about **processes, concepts, and relationships** (nouns).

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation

### 2.1 Nominalization defined
**Nominalization** (also *grammatical metaphor*, in Halliday's terms) is the process of turning a verb, adjective, or clause into a **noun or noun phrase**.

| Source | Nominalization |
|---|---|
| verb *decide* | the **decision** |
| verb *fail* | the **failure** |
| adjective *different* | the **difference** |
| adjective *able* | the **ability** |
| clause *prices increased rapidly* | the **rapid increase in prices** |
| clause *they discovered that the drug is safe* | the **discovery of the drug's safety** |

### 2.2 Why academic writers nominalize
| Reason | Explanation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Abstraction** | Concepts become "things" that can be discussed | *globalization, inequality, sustainability* |
| **Density** | A lot of meaning in a compact space | *the effects of long-term exposure to pollution on child development* |
| **Objectivity** | Removes the human agent, focuses on process | *The analysis showed…* (not *We looked and saw…*) |
| **Cohesion** | A nominalization sums up the previous sentence and becomes the subject of the next ("given–new" flow) | *Prices rose sharply. **This increase** forced many families to…* |
| **Evaluation** | Nouns can carry attitude when modified | *a dangerous escalation, a remarkable improvement* |
| **Classification** | Naming a phenomenon allows analysis and comparison | *urbanization, migration, inflation* |

*grow rapidly* → *rapid growth*; *respond immediately* → *immediate response*; *change significantly* → *significant change*; *improve steadily* → *steady improvement*; *rise sharply* → *a sharp rise*.

### 2.6 Hedging and stance (claiming exactly as much as the evidence allows)
Academic English avoids absolute claims. Compare:

> ❌ *Social media destroys teenagers' mental health.* (absolute, unsupported)
> ✅ *There is growing evidence that heavy use of social media may be associated with poorer mental health among teenagers.* (hedged, precise)

**Hedging devices**
| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Modal verbs** | *may, might, could, can, would, should* |
| **Lexical verbs** | *seem, appear, suggest, indicate, tend to, believe, assume, estimate, propose, argue* |
| **Adverbs** | *possibly, probably, perhaps, presumably, apparently, arguably, generally, typically, largely, mainly, partly, somewhat, relatively, approximately, roughly* |
| **Adjectives** | *possible, probable, likely, unlikely, potential, apparent, tentative, preliminary, general* |
| **Nouns** | *possibility, tendency, likelihood, suggestion, assumption, indication, estimate* |
| **Quantifiers/frequency** | *some, many, several, a number of, a majority of, often, frequently, usually, in some cases* |
| **Phrases** | *to some extent, in most cases, to a certain degree, as far as we know, it appears that, there is some evidence that, on the whole, in general* |
| **Distancing** | *It is generally believed that… / According to X… / X claims that…* |

**Boosters (used with strong evidence)**
*clearly, definitely, undoubtedly, indeed, in fact, certainly, demonstrate, show, prove (rare), establish, it is evident that, there is no doubt that, strongly suggests.*

**Balance:** too many hedges sound uncommitted (*It could perhaps possibly be that…*); too few sound overconfident. Aim for a **calibrated** claim: strong where evidence is strong, tentative where it isn't.

**Strength ladder of verbs of evidence**
`suggests < indicates < shows < demonstrates < proves (rarely used)`
`may be associated with < is linked to < correlates with < contributes to < causes`
(Note: *correlation* is not *causation*; academic writing treats the difference seriously.)

### 2.7 Impersonal and objective style
- **Passive (Lesson 12):** *Data were collected from 200 participants.* (focus on action, not agent)
- **Abstract subjects:** *The findings suggest…; The evidence points to…; This study examines…*
- **Impersonal *it*:** *It is widely accepted that…; It has been argued that…; It should be noted that…*
- **There-structures:** *There is a growing body of research on…*
- **Avoiding first/second person (varies by field and culture):** many fields now accept *I* or *we* for methods and arguments (*I argue that…; We collected…*), especially in the humanities and social sciences. Check your department's conventions. Avoid *you* (*you can see that…*) in formal writing.
- **Avoiding contractions** (*do not*, not *don't*), **slang**, **phrasal verbs when a formal verb exists** (Lesson 55), and **vague words** (*thing, stuff, a lot of, nice, good*).

### 2.8 Cohesion through noun phrases (given–new and "labelling nouns")
Academic prose links sentences by **re-naming** an idea with a nominalization that summarizes it:

> *Several countries have reduced taxes on fuel. **This trend** has been criticized by environmental groups. **Such measures** are said to encourage car use, but **the policy's supporters** argue that…*

**Labelling (shell) nouns** that summarize previous information:
*this trend, this development, this phenomenon, this finding, this approach, such a view, these factors, this issue, this problem, this situation, this change, this shift, the former / the latter, this tendency, such evidence, these measures, this argument, this assumption, this limitation.*

Use them with **specific modifiers** for precision: *this sharp decline*, *this unexpected finding*, *this controversial approach*.

### 2.9 Reporting verbs and citation language
| Stance | Verbs |
|---|---|
| **Neutral** | *state, note, report, describe, observe, explain, discuss, mention, point out, outline* |
| **Positive/strong** | *demonstrate, show, establish, confirm, prove, highlight, emphasize, stress* |
| **Tentative** | *suggest, propose, hypothesize, speculate, imply, indicate* |
| **Critical** | *claim, allege, assert, contend, maintain, insist* (author is uncommitted or sceptical) |
| **Disagreement** | *challenge, dispute, question, criticize, refute, contradict, reject* |

**Patterns**
- *According to Smith (2019), …*
- *Smith (2019) argues that…*
- *As Smith (2019) points out, …*
- *Smith (2019) suggests that…; Smith and Jones (2020) found that…*
- *In a study of 500 students, Lee (2018) reports…*
- *(Smith, 2019)* — parenthetical citation (APA/Harvard style)
- *Several studies have shown (e.g., Brown, 2017; Green, 2020) that…*
- Tense: **present** for general truths and the author's current position (*Smith argues…*), **past** for specific completed studies (*Lee found…*), **present perfect** for the general state of research (*Researchers have shown…*).

## 3. Why Native Speakers Do This
Academic English is not "natural" to anyone. Native speakers **learn it at university** through exposure and feedback, just like you. The difference is that natives have absorbed millions of everyday chunks first, so the academic ones attach more easily.

Why does academic writing look this way?

1. **Historical:** European scholarship was written in Latin and French for centuries. English academic style inherited long Latinate vocabulary and abstract noun phrases.
2. **Epistemic:** knowledge claims need to be **checkable** and **cautious**, so hedging and impersonal structures are conventions of honesty, not decoration.
3. **Communicative:** readers are experts who want information fast; dense noun phrases, topic sentences, and signposting help them skim.
4. **Social:** using the conventions marks you as a member of the academic community.

A final note: modern style guides (including those of many scientists and editors) now **push back against excessive nominalization.** Too many abstract nouns produce "zombie prose" (Helen Sword's phrase), in which the action disappears:

> ❌ *The implementation of the utilization of new methods led to the facilitation of improvements in the effectiveness of teaching.*
> ✅ *Using new methods improved teaching.*

So the real skill is **control**: use nominalization where it helps (concepts, cohesion, density) and use strong verbs where action and clarity matter.

## 4. When to Use / When Not To
**Use academic vocabulary and nominalization when**
- writing essays, theses, abstracts, reports, grant proposals, and journal articles;
- presenting research or professional analysis;
- writing formal reports in business, policy, law, and science;
- reading complex texts (you must recognize these patterns to understand them).

**Don't**
- Don't use them in casual speech. *"The implementation of the plan was completed"* at a dinner table sounds absurd.
- Don't stack nominalizations (*the implementation of the evaluation of the assessment*). Break them with verbs.
- Don't choose a long word when a short one is as accurate: *use* not *utilize*, *show* not *demonstrate* (when "demonstrate" adds nothing), *help* not *facilitate*, *about* not *approximately* (when precision is unneeded).
- Don't hide the actor when responsibility matters (*Mistakes were made* vs. *We made mistakes*).
- Don't use hedges everywhere. Be direct about well-supported facts: *Water boils at 100°C at sea level*, not *Water may tend to boil at around 100°C*.
- Don't use rare "impressive" words you haven't seen in context. Misused academic words are worse than plain ones.
- Don't copy phrases from sources (plagiarism). Learn the **patterns**, not the sentences. Paraphrase and cite.
- Don't confuse formal with vague: *a lot of factors* → *several interrelated factors*; *things* → *aspects/variables/issues*.

## 5. Frequency & Register

**Frequency:** the 570 Academic Word List families make up roughly 10% of running words in academic texts, a very high coverage for a small list. Beyond this, each discipline has its own technical vocabulary (e.g., *regression* in statistics, *tort* in law). Prioritize:

1. **High-frequency academic verbs and nouns** (the lists in 2.5).
2. **Light verbs with nominalizations** (2.4).
3. **Hedging and reporting devices** (2.6, 2.9).
4. **Signposting phrases** (2.10).
5. **Discipline-specific vocabulary**, as you read in your field.

**Register:** academic English sits at levels 4–5 of the formality scale (Lesson 55). It differs from legal and bureaucratic English (even more fixed and formulaic) and from popular science (more narrative and informal).

| Feature | Academic | Everyday |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | abstract, nominalized | person |
| Verb | passive, stative, light verb | active, dynamic |
| Sentence length | long, subordinate | short |
| Lexical density | high (many content words) | lower |
| Hedging | frequent | rare |
| Contractions | none | frequent |
| Phrasal verbs | rare | frequent |

## 6. American vs. British Notes
| Feature | US | UK |
|---|---|---|
| **Spelling** | *analyze, organize, behavior, center, color, program, labor, defense, judgment* | *analyse, organise/organize (Oxford -ize), behaviour, centre, colour, programme (non-computer), labour, defence, judgement* |

| **Data** | plural *data* in formal science (*the data show*); singular increasingly accepted in general writing | same; either increasingly accepted |
| **Collective nouns** | singular (*the committee is*) | singular or plural (*the committee are*) |
| **Dates** | *March 3, 2024* | *3 March 2024* |

| **Punctuation** | commas and periods inside quotes | punctuation inside only if part of the quoted text |
| **Style** | "while," "among" | "whilst," "amongst" (more formal/dated) |
| **Tone** | more direct, confident claims | more hedged, understated |
| **First person** | increasingly accepted | traditionally more restricted (varies) |

**Practical tip:** choose one spelling system and use it consistently. Set your word processor's language accordingly.

## 7. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

### 7.1 Spanish academic style transfer
Spanish academic prose often favours **long, cumulative sentences**, ornate openings, and very general introductions. English academic prose prefers **clear topic sentences, a direct thesis, and shorter, controlled sentences**.

| ❌ Spanish-influenced | ✅ English academic |
|---|---|
| *Since the beginning of time, humanity has…* | *Over the past century, … / In recent decades, …* |
| *In today's globalized world, …* (cliché) | *Globalization has increased… (be specific)* |
| *It is a fact known by all that…* | *It is widely recognized that… / Research has shown…* |
| *In the present work we will try to…* | *This paper examines… / This essay argues…* |
| *I think that / In my opinion* (overused) | *This essay argues that… / It can be argued that…* |
| *In conclusion, we can say that…* | *In conclusion, … / Overall, …* |
| *As it is well known, …* | *As is well known, … / It is well established that…* |
| *The theme of this work is…* | *The topic/focus of this paper is…* |
| *It is important to emphasize on…* | *It is important to emphasize…* |
| *We will make a deep analysis* | *We will conduct a detailed analysis* |
| *To do a research* | *To conduct research* |
| *Realize a study* | *Carry out a study* |
| *Make an investigation about* | *Investigate / conduct an investigation into* |

### 7.3 Grammar and punctuation habits
- **Comma splice (very common):** ❌ *The results were clear, they supported the hypothesis.* → ✅ *The results were clear; they supported the hypothesis.* / *The results were clear and supported the hypothesis.* (Lesson 27)
- **Starting sentences with *Also*, *And*, *But*, *Because*:** avoid in formal writing (*Also* → *In addition,* / *Moreover,*).
- **Missing subject or overuse of impersonal *it/there*:** ❌ *Is important to note that…* → ✅ *It is important to note that…*
- **Overlong sentences:** Spanish allows long chains with gerunds and commas. In English, split at 30–40 words.
- **Misusing *which/that*:** *that* (defining, no comma), *which* (non-defining, comma). (Lesson 21)
- **Dangling modifiers:** ❌ *Having analyzed the data, the results were clear.* → ✅ *Having analyzed the data, we found the results clear.* (The participle's subject must match the main clause's subject.)
- **Parallelism errors:** ❌ *The study aims to identify, evaluating, and to measure…* → ✅ *The study aims to identify, evaluate, and measure…* (Lesson 26)
- **Article errors:** ❌ *The inflation increases* (general) → ✅ *Inflation increases.* ❌ *In the society…* → ✅ *In society…* (Lesson 18)
- **Tense in citations:** *Smith (2019) found…* (past) / *Smith argues…* (present) / *Researchers have shown…* (present perfect).
- **Passive overuse or underuse:** know when the actor is irrelevant (use passive) and when it matters (use active).
- **Using *we* generically:** ✅ in many papers (*we argue*), but ❌ in an essay by one author unless allowed (use *I* or impersonal).
- **Using *since* for cause in the first position, ambiguous with time:** ❌ *Since the economy grew, … * (ambiguous) → ✅ *Because the economy grew, …*
- **Using *while* ambiguously:** can mean *although* or *at the same time*. Choose a clearer connector if confusing.

### 7.5 Over-correct or "thesaurus" errors
- Using rare synonyms inappropriately: *The orchestration of the manuscript is replete with ostentatious verbiage.* ✗
- Mixing registers: *The study was, like, really important and it demonstrates that…*
- Using *utilize* everywhere (prefer *use*).
- Misplacing *whereas/while/whilst*: *whereas* = contrast (in the middle of a sentence), *while* may be time or contrast.
- Over-hedging: *It might possibly perhaps suggest that, to some extent, the results may be…*

## 8. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ **"Academic English means long, complicated sentences."** — Good academic writing is *clear* first; complexity must serve precision.
- ❌ **"More nominalizations = better."** — Beyond a point, they cause "zombie nouns." Use strong verbs where action matters.
- ❌ **"Academic writing never uses *I*."** — Fields differ. Humanities, social sciences, and many science journals accept *I/we* (methods, argument).
- ❌ **"Passive is always required in academic writing."** — It's useful but overused; many style guides advise active when the agent matters.
- ❌ **"Hedging shows weakness."** — It shows accuracy and epistemic honesty; bold claims without evidence are weak.
- ❌ **"Academic vocabulary = specialist vocabulary."** — The AWL words are general across disciplines. Specialist vocabulary is separate.
- ❌ **"Using difficult words will impress examiners."** — Clarity and accuracy do. Misused rare words lose marks.
- ❌ **"I can write academic English by translating my Spanish text."** — Rhetorical structure differs (English prefers explicit thesis, topic sentences, signposting).
- ❌ **"Data is always plural."** — Formal science: plural; general writing: often singular. Be consistent and know your audience.
- ❌ **"Contractions are acceptable in essays if the tone is clear."** — Avoid in formal academic writing.
- ❌ **"A thesaurus will upgrade my vocabulary."** — Only if you check collocation, register, and meaning (Lesson 54).
- ❌ **"Paraphrasing = replacing words with synonyms."** — Real paraphrase changes structure and wording; always cite.

- Use **chunking and pauses** at clause boundaries.
- Place **stress on key content words**: *The RESULTS SUGgest that INflation is LINKED to...*

## 10. Memory Technique

### 10.1 Word-family tables (the best single technique)
For every academic word you learn, fill a row: verb, noun (event), noun (person/thing), adjective, adverb. Add the collocations of the noun and 1 sentence. Example:

| Verb | Noun | Person | Adj | Adv | Collocations | Sentence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *assess* | *assessment* | *assessor* | *assessable* | — | *conduct/make an assessment; risk assessment* | *The report provides an assessment of the risks.* |
| *contribute* | *contribution* | *contributor* | *contributory* | — | *make a contribution to; contribute to a debate* | *Diet contributes to heart disease.* |
| *significant* (adj) | *significance* | — | *significant* | *significantly* | *of great significance; significant difference* | *There was a significantly higher rate.* |

Aim to build **three rows a day**. Review in the spaced schedule (Lesson 51).

### 10.2 The "Nominalization transformer" (daily drill)
Take a plain sentence and transform it in three steps:

1. **Find the action or quality** (verb/adjective).
2. **Turn it into a noun** (suffix machine, 2.3).
3. **Choose a new verb** (light verb, 2.4).

> *They decided quickly, and this caused problems.*
> 1. decided → decision; quickly → quick
> 2. *the quick decision*
> 3. *The quick decision led to several problems.* ✅

Practice 5 sentences a day. Then **reverse it** (de-nominalize) to keep control:
> *The utilization of resources led to the facilitation of growth.* → *Using resources helped growth.*

### 10.3 The "So what?" test for clarity
After each academic sentence, ask: *Who does what?* If you can't answer, you have probably nominalized too much. Rewrite with a human or concrete subject where it improves clarity.

### 10.4 Collocation banks by noun hub (Lesson 52 method)
Make hubs for academic nouns:
- **evidence:** *provide/present/gather/collect/cite evidence; strong/compelling/empirical/anecdotal evidence; evidence suggests/indicates/supports*
- **argument:** *make/advance/put forward/support/undermine/refute an argument; a convincing/persuasive/weak/valid argument*
- **impact/effect:** *have/exert an impact on; a significant/profound/negative/lasting impact; adverse effects*
- **role:** *play/assume a role; a key/central/crucial/minor role*
- **framework:** *a theoretical/conceptual/legal framework; provide/establish a framework*
- **analysis:** *conduct/carry out an analysis; a detailed/critical/statistical analysis; analysis shows/reveals/suggests*

### 10.5 Hedging ladder
Memorize a ladder for one claim:
`X causes Y → X leads to Y → X contributes to Y → X is associated with Y → X may be associated with Y → There is some evidence that X may be associated with Y.`
Practice moving a claim up and down the ladder according to evidence strength.

### 10.6 The "academic sentence bank"
Collect sentence frames (not whole sentences) in a notebook:
- *This study examines the extent to which ___.*
- *___ has been shown to be associated with ___.*
- *One possible explanation for this is that ___.*
- *These findings are consistent with / contradict earlier research.*
- *Although ___, it remains unclear whether ___.*
- *A key limitation of this approach is ___.*
- *Further research is needed to determine ___.*
- *In contrast to ___, ___ appears to ___.*

Fill the gaps with your own topic. Label each frame by function (introduce, explain, contrast, limit, conclude).

### 10.7 Read like a writer
Pick one article in your field per week. Highlight (a) nominalizations, (b) hedges, (c) reporting verbs, (d) transitions. Copy 5 useful chunks into your notebook with their function.

### 10.8 Spaced retrieval cards
- Front: *The ___ (n) of the policy was delayed.* (implement) → **implementation**
- Front: *Evidence ___ that…* (strength: weak) → **suggests**
- Front: *Smith (2019) ___ that the policy failed.* (neutral reporting) → **states / reports / notes**
- Front: *Hedge: "X causes Y."* → **X may contribute to Y.**
- Front: *Verb forms: analyse, ___, ___, ___* → **analysis, analyst, analytical**
Review on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 30.

### 10.9 Weekly writing cycle
1. Write a 150-word paragraph on a topic from your field.
2. Underline the plain verbs and vague nouns.
3. Upgrade 5 of them with academic vocabulary and nominalizations.
4. Add one hedge and one cohesive "this + noun" link.
5. Check the register and collocations (Lessons 52–55).
6. Add the best 3 chunks to your notebook.

## 11. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern 1: Verb → noun, adverb → adjective.** *increase rapidly → a rapid increase.* Convert, then add a light verb.

> **Pattern 2: Cause–effect with nominalizations.** *The increase in X led to / resulted in / gave rise to / contributed to a decrease in Y.* (Learn the four verbs and their strength.)

> **Pattern 3: This + labelling noun.** *This trend / This finding / This development / This phenomenon…* summarises the previous sentence and starts the next.

> **Pattern 4: Hedge by modals, verbs, adverbs.** *may suggest / appears to indicate / is probably associated with.* One hedge per claim is usually enough.

> **Pattern 5: Evidence verbs on a ladder.** *suggests < indicates < shows < demonstrates.* Match the verb to the evidence's strength.

> **Pattern 6: Reporting verbs encode stance.** *Smith notes / argues / claims / admits / contends.* Choose the stance deliberately (Lesson 54).

> **Pattern 7: Passive for process, active for argument.** *Samples were collected… / We argue that…* (when allowed).

> **Pattern 8: Noun phrases pre- and post-modify.** *a [significant] [increase] [in the number of students] [applying for postgraduate courses].* Learn to **unpack long noun phrases** by finding the head noun (*increase*).

> **Pattern 9: Comparative and contrast structures.** *X is considerably higher than Y. / Whereas X…, Y… / In contrast to X, Y… / X differs from Y in that…* (Lesson 28).

> **Pattern 10: Limitations and future research.** *One limitation is… / The sample size was small… / Further research should…* Show critical awareness.

> **Pattern 11: Nominalization + *of* + object; *by* + agent.** *The destruction of the forest by logging…* Know when to include or omit the agent.

> **Pattern 12: Avoid vague quantifiers.** Replace *a lot of* with *a considerable amount of / a large number of / a substantial proportion of* + data when possible (*around 60% of respondents*).

> **Connection to Lesson 12 (passive):** impersonal academic style relies on passive and *it* constructions.

> **Connection to Lesson 21 (relative clauses):** academic prose uses reduced relatives (*the factors affecting growth*) and non-defining clauses.

> **Connection to Lesson 26 (parallelism):** lists of nominalizations must be parallel.

> **Connection to Lesson 28 (discourse markers):** signposting is essential for coherence.

> **Connection to Lessons 52–55:** collocation (light verbs), intensifiers (*considerably higher*), synonym precision (reporting verbs), and register (levels 4–5) all converge here.

> **Connection to Lesson 57 (next):** professional English shares many academic features (nominalization, hedging) but prioritizes clarity and action.

## 12. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1 — Basic transformation**
- *They improved the system.* → *The improvement of the system…*
- *The company grew fast.* → *The company's rapid growth…*
- *People are poor.* → *Poverty is a problem.*
- *We decided to stop.* → *The decision to stop…*

**Level 2 — Cause and effect**
- *Because more people moved to cities, housing became expensive.* → *Increased urbanization led to a rise in housing costs.*
- *Because the weather changed, crops failed.* → *Climate change resulted in widespread crop failure.*
- *When students sleep badly, they perform worse.* → *Poor sleep is associated with lower academic performance.*

**Level 3 — Academic sentences**
- *This study examines the relationship between income and health outcomes.*
- *The findings suggest that early intervention may reduce long-term costs.*
- *A growing body of evidence indicates that biodiversity loss threatens food security.*
- *Smith (2019) argues that the policy had limited impact.*
- *The results were consistent with previous research.*

**Level 4 — Dense paragraph (study the structure)**
> "Rapid urbanization has transformed patterns of employment in developing economies. While this shift has generated new opportunities, it has also intensified inequality, as access to education and housing remains uneven. Consequently, policymakers face the challenge of balancing economic growth with social inclusion. Recent evidence suggests that targeted investment in public services may mitigate some of these effects, although the long-term impact remains uncertain."

*What to notice:* nominalizations (*urbanization, employment, inequality, access, growth, inclusion, investment, impact*), labelling noun (*this shift*), hedges (*may mitigate, suggests, remains uncertain*), connectors (*While, Consequently, although*), light verbs (*generated, intensified, face the challenge, mitigate*).

**Level 5 — Native-like academic control**
- "Although the correlation between screen time and sleep disruption is well documented, the direction of causality remains contested: it is plausible that sleep-deprived adolescents seek stimulation online rather than the reverse."
- "By foregrounding the role of institutions, the framework challenges the prevailing assumption that growth is driven primarily by individual entrepreneurship."
- "The apparent contradiction dissolves once the two measures are disaggregated by age group."
- "These limitations notwithstanding, the study offers a robust account of how local policies shape migration decisions."

**Before and after (student paragraph)**
> ❌ *"A lot of people think that technology is bad for children because they use phones too much. But also technology can help children to learn. In my opinion, the thing is that parents must control the time."*
> ✅ *"Public concern about children's excessive use of mobile devices is widespread. However, technology can also support learning when used appropriately. It can therefore be argued that the key issue is not technology itself but the extent to which parents regulate its use."*

**Academic phrase bank (ready to use)**
- *It has been widely documented that…*
- *There is a growing consensus that…*
- *The evidence for … is mixed.*
- *… plays a pivotal role in …*
- *… is attributable to …*
- *… can be understood as …*
- *This raises the question of whether …*
- *The extent to which … remains open to debate.*
- *Taken together, these results suggest …*
- *This is not to say that …*

## 17. Speaking Practice

**Drill 1 — Nominalization flash (say aloud):**
- "They decided quickly" → "The quick decision…"
- "Prices rose sharply" → "A sharp rise in prices…"
- "Students participate actively" → "Active participation by students…"
- "Pollution increased" → "The increase in pollution…"

**Drill 2 — Hedging ladder.** Say one claim at five strengths:
> "Sugar causes diabetes." → "Sugar leads to diabetes." → "Sugar contributes to diabetes." → "Sugar is linked to diabetes." → "Sugar may be linked to diabetes in some people."

**Drill 3 — Stress families.** Say aloud: *ANalyse–aNAlysis–anaLYTical; ECOnomy–eCONomist–ecoNOMic; PHOtograph–phoTOgraphy–photoGRAPHic; deMONstrate–demonSTRAtion.*

**Drill 4 — 90-second academic mini-talk.** Present a short argument (introduce the topic, state a claim, give evidence, acknowledge a limitation, conclude). Use at least five nominalizations, two hedges, and two signposting phrases. Record, listen, and check stress on long words.

**Drill 5 — Summarize and report.** Read a short article abstract, then summarize it aloud using reporting verbs: *The author argues that…, The study finds that…, The evidence suggests that…, The authors acknowledge that…*

**Drill 6 — Seminar language.** Practice: *I'd like to build on what [name] said…; I see your point, but one limitation is…; To what extent does the evidence support…?; Could you clarify what you mean by…?; That raises an interesting question about…*

**Drill 7 — Role-play (viva/defence).**
Examiner: "Why did you choose this method?"
You: "Given the exploratory nature of the question, a qualitative approach seemed most appropriate. It allowed me to capture participants' perspectives in detail, although I acknowledge that the findings may not be generalizable."

**Mini Quiz**
1. Turning verbs/adjectives/clauses into nouns. Reasons: abstraction, density, objectivity, cohesion, classification (any two).
2. decision, failure, difference, ability, importance, growth.
3. A verb with little meaning of its own that combines with a noun to carry the action: *conduct an analysis; make a decision; draw a conclusion; play a role.* Any two.
4. Modals (*may*), lexical verbs (*suggest/appear*), adverbs (*possibly/generally*), adjectives (*likely*), phrases (*to some extent*). Any three with examples.
5. *Suggests* is tentative (weaker evidence); *demonstrates* is strong (clear evidence).
6. A noun that sums up a previous idea (*this trend, this finding*), linking sentences smoothly.
7. They hide actions and produce heavy, unclear prose; fix by using strong verbs and concrete subjects (*Using new methods improved teaching*).
8. Formal science: plural (*the data show*); general writing: often singular. Be consistent and follow conventions.
9. Stress falls on the syllable before the suffix: *eduCAtion, ecoNOMic.*
10. Any three: *Since the beginning of time; In today's globalized world; discuss about; emphasize on; a research; in the other hand; make a research; the actual situation (for current).*
11. *Affect* is usually a verb (influence); *effect* is usually a noun (result).
12. Because it reflects accurate, calibrated claims and honesty about the limits of evidence.
