# Lesson 43 — Preposition + Noun / Adjective + Preposition Collocations (Dependent Prepositions)
### Phase 3: Prepositions | Lesson 7 of 7 (Final Lesson of Phase 3)

---

## 0. Where this fits
This closes Phase 3. Lessons 37–42 built a conceptual, image-based system for understanding prepositions' core spatial/abstract meanings. This final lesson addresses the category that conceptual understanding *cannot* fully solve: **dependent prepositions** — fixed pairings between specific adjectives/verbs/nouns and a particular preposition (interested IN, good AT, afraid OF, depend ON) — where the "correct" preposition is often arbitrary from a pure logic standpoint and must be memorized directly, exactly as this course has repeatedly emphasized for collocations throughout Phases 2 and 3.

---

## 1. Intuitive Explanation

Some adjectives, verbs, and nouns are conventionally, historically "married" to one specific preposition — not because of any deep logical connection to that preposition's core spatial image (Lesson 37's method), but simply because that's the fixed pairing English settled on. "Interested IN" and "good AT" don't derive predictably from "in"'s containment image or "at"'s point image — they're fixed collocations, learned the same way you learned Phase 2's take/make/do collocations.

---

## 2. The Linguistic Explanation — Organized by Category

### 2a. Adjective + Preposition (extremely high-frequency, essential set)
| Adjective + Preposition | Example |
|---|---|
| interested IN | "I'm interested in art." |
| good/bad AT | "She's good at math." |
| afraid/scared OF | "He's afraid of spiders." |
| worried/concerned ABOUT | "I'm worried about the exam." |
| responsible FOR | "She's responsible for the budget." |
| married TO | "He's married to a doctor." |
| similar TO | "This is similar to that one." |
| different FROM (also acceptable: different THAN, especially American English) | "This is different from what I expected." |
| famous FOR | "The city is famous for its architecture." |
| proud OF | "I'm proud of you." |
| tired OF | "I'm tired of waiting." |
| excited ABOUT | "I'm excited about the trip." |
| angry/upset WITH (a person) / ABOUT (a situation) | "I'm angry with him." / "I'm angry about the delay." |
| capable OF | "She's capable of great things." |
| aware OF | "Are you aware of the risks?" |

### 2b. Verb + Preposition (essential set)
| Verb + Preposition | Example |
|---|---|
| depend ON | "It depends on the weather." |
| rely ON | "You can rely on me." |
| believe IN | "I believe in second chances." |
| worry ABOUT | "Don't worry about it." |
| think ABOUT / OF | "I'm thinking about my future." (considering) / "What do you think of this?" (opinion) |
| apologize FOR | "I apologize for being late." |
| succeed IN | "She succeeded in convincing them." |
| consist OF | "The team consists of five people." |
| deal WITH | "I need to deal with this problem." |
| wait FOR | "I'm waiting for the bus." |
| listen TO | "Listen to me." (note: NOT "listen me" — a very common Spanish-speaker error, since *escuchar* is directly transitive without a preposition) |
| look AT / FOR / AFTER | "Look at this." (observe) / "I'm looking for my keys." (searching) / "Can you look after my dog?" (take care of) |
| complain ABOUT | "She complained about the service." |
| congratulate ON | "Congratulations on your promotion!" |
| insist ON | "He insisted on paying." |

### 2c. Noun + Preposition
| Noun + Preposition | Example |
|---|---|

| solution TO | "We need a solution to this problem." |
| answer TO | "I don't know the answer to that question." |
| interest IN | "her interest in music" |
| relationship WITH / BETWEEN | "my relationship with him" / "the relationship between the two events" |
| increase/decrease IN | "an increase in sales" |
| difficulty WITH/IN | "I have difficulty with this task." / "difficulty in understanding" |

## 3. Why These Are Genuinely Arbitrary (and Why That's OK)
Unlike the core prepositions covered in Lessons 38–42, dependent prepositions frequently resist derivation from the core spatial image — "good AT" doesn't obviously connect to "at"'s point-in-space/time image, and "interested IN" doesn't obviously connect to "in"'s containment image, at least not in an immediately intuitive way for a learner. These pairings crystallized historically through usage patterns, the same way Phase 2's take/make/do collocations did. This is precisely why this lesson closes Phase 3: after six lessons of conceptual, image-based learning, it's equally important to acknowledge honestly that a meaningful subset of preposition usage simply requires direct memorization — consistent with this entire course's balanced, honest approach to collocations throughout.

---

## 4. Why Native Speakers Don't Find This Arbitrary
Native speakers don't consciously notice these pairings as "arbitrary" because they were acquired through the same chunking mechanism (Lesson 3) as every other high-frequency collocation — "interested in" is retrieved as a single unit, not constructed by separately choosing "interested" and then deriving which preposition logically follows. This is exactly the model you should adopt: learn adjective/verb + preposition pairs as single retrievable chunks, not as two separate word-choice decisions.

---

## 5. When This Matters Most
This category matters constantly — dependent prepositions appear in an enormous share of everyday sentences involving emotions, opinions, relationships, and evaluations (interested in, afraid of, good at, worried about) — arguably one of the highest-frequency grammatical categories in this entire course, despite receiving comparatively little formal attention in most language courses.

---

## 6. Frequency & Register
Used across all registers — this is foundational, high-frequency vocabulary, not a formal/informal distinction. Getting the specific preposition wrong is immediately noticeable regardless of context, precisely because these pairings are so deeply automated in native speakers' minds.

---

## 7. American vs. British Note
A few genuine variations exist: "different from" is standard in both varieties, but "different than" is notably more accepted/common in American English (and sometimes considered less correct in more traditional British usage, though widely heard); "talk to" vs. "talk with" show minor regional preference without a strict split. These are worth flagging as areas of legitimate flexibility rather than hard errors.

---

## 8. Common Mistakes Made by Spanish Speakers

| Mistake | Why | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "I'm interested on this topic." | Direct interference from *interesado en* (using "on" as a rough phonetic/conceptual association, or confusion with other "on" patterns) | "I'm interested in this topic." |
| "She's married with a doctor." | Direct translation from *casada con* | "She's married to a doctor." |
| "I depend of my family." | Direct translation from *depender de* | "I depend on my family." |
| "Listen me!" | Spanish *escuchar* is directly transitive (no preposition needed); English "listen" requires "to" | "Listen to me!" |
| "I'm agree with you." (a related, adjacent error — "agree" doesn't need "be," it's a full verb) | Confusing *estar de acuerdo* (using "be") with English "agree" (a standalone verb) | "I agree with you." |

---

## 9. Common Misconceptions
- ❌ "If I know the core spatial image of a preposition (Lessons 37-42), I can always derive the correct dependent preposition logically." — As this lesson establishes, dependent prepositions are frequently NOT derivable this way — they require direct memorization as fixed chunks, the same as any other collocation category in this course.
- ❌ "Dependent prepositions are a minor, low-priority grammar point." — Given how frequently adjectives/verbs expressing emotions, opinions, and relationships appear in everyday communication, this is actually one of the highest-frequency, highest-impact categories for sounding natural.
- ❌ "The Spanish preposition for a similar-meaning word is a reliable guide to the English one." — As the error table shows, this approach fails constantly and specifically for dependent prepositions, more than almost any other grammatical category covered in this course.

---

## 10. Pronunciation Note
Adjective/verb + preposition pairs are stored and produced as fluent chunks (per Lesson 3), with the preposition itself typically unstressed and reduced, while the adjective/verb carries the primary stress: "inTERested in," "GOOD at," "afRAID of" — practicing this natural stress pattern alongside the correct preposition reinforces both simultaneously.

---

## 11. Memory Technique
**Build a dedicated "Dependent Preposition Chunk List"** — directly extending this entire course's chunk-journaling method (Lesson 3) — organized by category (adjective+prep, verb+prep, noun+prep) as shown in Section 2. Since these pairings resist logical derivation, the most efficient strategy is simply consistent exposure and active recall drilling, treating each pairing as a single retrievable unit rather than two separate grammatical choices.

---

## 12. Patterns (Not Isolated Rules)

> **Pattern (the honest closing pattern for this entire phase):** Prepositions operate on a spectrum from highly predictable (core spatial/temporal/abstract uses, Lessons 38-42, following the three-layer extension method) to highly arbitrary (dependent prepositions, this lesson, requiring direct chunk memorization). Skilled learners apply the conceptual method where it helps and switch to direct memorization where the pattern genuinely doesn't hold — recognizing which mode is needed is itself a valuable, learnable skill.

---

## 13. Real-Life Examples (Progressively Harder)

**Level 1:**
- "I'm afraid of the dark." (adjective + preposition)
- "She depends on her parents financially." (verb + preposition)

**Level 2 — same adjective, different preposition depending on context:**
- "I'm angry with my brother." (a person) vs. "I'm angry about the situation." (a circumstance/event)

**Level 3 — verb + preposition affecting meaning (look at/for/after):**
- "Look at the sunset." (observe) / "I'm looking for my glasses." (searching) / "She looks after her grandmother." (takes care of) — three genuinely distinct meanings from the same base verb, distinguished entirely by the preposition

**Level 4 — combining multiple dependent prepositions correctly in one natural, sophisticated passage:**
- "I'm really interested in psychology, but I'm not particularly good at statistics, which worries me about my ability to succeed in this program — still, I believe in working hard, and I'm confident I can rely on my professors for support." (interested in, good at, worried about, succeed in, believe in, rely on — six dependent prepositions in one natural passage)
