# Lesson 4 — Asking Questions: W-Fragen and Yes/No Inversion

**Phase:** 1 — The Sentence Machine
**Status:** ✅ Done

---

## 1. The core idea

Two verb positions are known so far: position 2 (main clauses) and final position (subordinate clauses). Questions introduce a third pattern: **the verb jumps to position 1.**

### Type A — Yes/No questions (Ja/Nein-Fragen)

Take a normal statement and swap the verb and subject:
- Du **sprichst** Deutsch. → **Sprichst** du Deutsch?
- Er **kommt** heute. → **Kommt** er heute?

**Mental model:** the verb physically hops over the subject to the front of the line — everything else stays the same, just a verb-subject swap.

### Type B — W-Questions (W-Fragen)

Same V2 logic as a normal statement — a question word occupies position 1 instead of a topic:
- **Wo** wohnst du?
- **Was** machst du heute?
- **Warum** lernst du Deutsch?

This is the V2 rule from Lesson 1 again — the question word sits in position 1, the verb stays in position 2.

## 2. Why this matters

⚠️ **Common Spanish-speaker mistake:** Spanish allows questions with no inversion in casual speech — intonation alone can turn a statement into a question ("¿Tú hablas alemán?"). German has no such shortcut — you cannot ask a question by intonation alone while keeping statement word order.
- ❌ Du sprichst Deutsch?
- ✅ Sprichst du Deutsch?

(Rising intonation on a statement can function as a surprised echo-question — "Du sprichst Deutsch?!" — but that's a marked, emotional exception, not the default.)

## 3. The essential W-words

| German | Meaning | Note |
|---|---|---|
| wer | who | subject |
| wen | whom (accusative) | direct object |
| wem | to whom (dative) | indirect object |
| was | what | |
| wo | where (location) | |
| wohin | where to (movement) | |
| woher | where from | |
| wann | when | |
| warum / wieso / weshalb | why | near-synonyms, warum most common |
| wie | how | |
| welche/r/s | which | agrees with gender/case |

`wer/wen/wem` is an early taste of the case system (Phase 2) — German distinguishes "who is doing it" from "who is receiving it," where Spanish/English mostly just say "who."

## 4. Progressively harder examples

1. Wo arbeitest du?
2. Was hast du heute gemacht?
3. Warum lernst du gerade Deutsch, und nicht Französisch?
4. Weißt du, wann der Bus kommt? *(embedded question — "wann" pushes the verb to the end: **wann der Bus kommt**, not "wann kommt der Bus")*

## 5. The embedded-question trap (connects to Lesson 2)

- Direct question: **Wo wohnst du?** (verb in position 2)
- Embedded/indirect question: Ich weiß nicht, **wo du wohnst**. (verb pushed to the end — the W-word now acts as a subordinating connector, exactly like weil/ob/dass)

So W-words behave like normal position-1 question starters in direct questions, but flip into verb-final subordinators the instant they're embedded inside a bigger sentence ("Ich weiß nicht, ...", "Kannst du mir sagen, ...", "Es ist unklar, ..."). This pattern makes indirect/polite requests sound dramatically more natural — a clear marker of intermediate-vs-advanced German.

## 6. Speaking exercise

Say these out loud, then answer about yourself in full sentences:
- Wo wohnst du? → Ich wohne in ...
- Warum lernst du Deutsch? → Ich lerne Deutsch, weil ...
- Was machst du normalerweise am Wochenende?

Convert one into an embedded question:
> Direct: Wo arbeitest du?
> Embedded: Kannst du mir sagen, **wo du arbeitest**?

## 7. Thinking-in-German habit

Silently ask yourself a W-question about your day, then answer it in your head:
> *Was mache ich heute? Ich gehe einkaufen, und dann arbeite ich von zu Hause.*

Ask-then-answer mimics how native speakers structure internal planning.

## 8. Real-life mission

Ask a real W-question out loud to someone today — e.g. "Wo ist der Ausgang?" or "Wann macht das hier zu?" Try to catch at least the verb in their answer.

## 9. Mini quiz

4. Ask "where are you going" using **wohin**, not wo.
