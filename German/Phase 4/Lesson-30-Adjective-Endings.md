# Lesson 30 — Adjective Endings: One Mental Model for the Whole Table

**Phase:** 4 — Nouns, Adjectives, Gender
**Status:** ✅ Done

---

## 1. The core idea

Adjective endings have a fearsome reputation — most textbooks show three separate tables (weak, strong, mixed declension) and expect memorization of all three. This lesson gives you **one underlying principle** that generates all three tables, so you're deriving endings from logic rather than recalling a lookup table under conversational pressure.

## 2. The core principle: "someone has to carry the case information"

Every noun phrase needs its gender/case information marked **somewhere** — you already know this from Lesson 10-11's articles (der/den/dem). The entire adjective-ending system boils down to one rule:

**If the article already clearly marks the case/gender, the adjective ending can be weak/lazy (-e or -en). If the article is missing or already ambiguous, the adjective ending has to work harder and carry the case information itself (using the "strong" endings — which are borrowed directly from the der/die/das article endings themselves).**

**Mental model:** think of case-marking information as a **fixed amount of signal that must appear once per noun phrase** — either the article says it clearly, or the adjective has to say it instead. It's like a relay race baton: someone has to be holding it, but it doesn't matter whether it's the article or the adjective, as long as one of them is.

## 3. Weak declension: after der/die/das (definite article already marks everything)

When a definite article (der, die, das, dieser, jeder, etc.) is present, it's already carrying full case/gender information, so the adjective just adds **-e** (singular) or **-en** (almost everywhere else) — genuinely simple:

| | Nom | Akk | Dat | Gen |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| masc | der gute Mann | den guten Mann | dem guten Mann | des guten Mannes |
| fem | die gute Frau | die gute Frau | der guten Frau | der guten Frau |
| neut | das gute Kind | das gute Kind | dem guten Kind | des guten Kindes |
| plural | die guten Kinder | die guten Kinder | den guten Kindern | der guten Kinder |

Notice: **-e** only appears in the nominative singular (all genders) and the neuter/feminine accusative singular — everywhere else it's **-en**. This is a genuinely learnable shortcut: mostly -en, with a small set of -e exceptions concentrated in the nominative.

## 4. Strong declension: no article at all (adjective must carry everything itself)

When there's no article — talking about something in general, unmarked — the adjective itself takes the **der/die/das-style endings**, because nothing else in the phrase is signaling case/gender:

| | Nom | Akk | Dat | Gen |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| masc | gut**er** Wein | gut**en** Wein | gut**em** Wein | gut**en** Wein**s** |
| fem | gut**e** Milch | gut**e** Milch | gut**er** Milch | gut**er** Milch |
| neut | gut**es** Brot | gut**es** Brot | gut**em** Brot | gut**en** Brot**s** |
| plural | gut**e** Kinder | gut**e** Kinder | gut**en** Kindern | gut**er** Kinder |

**Notice the pattern directly:** "guter" (masc. nom.) looks like "der," "gutes" (neut. nom.) looks like "das," "guter" (fem.) looks like "der" (dative/genitive feminine article). The adjective is **literally borrowing the article's own endings**, because it's doing the article's job in the article's absence. This is why memorizing this table separately is unnecessary once you know the der/die/das table from Lessons 10-11 — you already have the endings, just relocated onto the adjective.

## 5. Mixed declension: after ein/kein/mein etc. (article present, but ambiguous in two spots)

Indefinite articles (ein, kein, mein, dein...) mark case/gender clearly in most forms — but **masculine nominative and neuter nominative/accusative** are ambiguous (ein could theoretically be any gender in those slots, since "ein" itself has no ending there). So the adjective has to "fill the gap" exactly in those specific spots, using strong endings, and stays weak everywhere else:

| | Nom | Akk |
|---|---|---|
| masc | ein gut**er** Mann *(strong — "ein" alone doesn't show gender here)* | einen gut**en** Mann *(weak — "einen" already shows masc. accusative)* |
| fem | eine gut**e** Frau *(weak — "eine" already shows feminine)* | eine gut**e** Frau *(weak)* |
| neut | ein gut**es** Kind *(strong — "ein" alone doesn't show neuter here)* | ein gut**es** Kind *(strong, same reason)* |

**Mental model:** ein/kein/mein are "articles with a blind spot" — they work fine most of the time, but in exactly the two spots where "ein" itself carries no revealing ending, the adjective has to step in and cover for them, borrowing the same strong endings from section 4. Everywhere else, the indefinite article is doing its job fine, so the adjective can relax into the weak -e/-en pattern from section 3.

## 6. The one-page summary

Instead of memorizing three tables, apply this decision process every time:

1. Is there a definite article (der/die/das/dieser)? → adjective is weak (-e/-en, mostly -en).
2. Is there no article at all? → adjective is strong (borrows der/die/das endings directly).
3. Is there an indefinite article (ein/kein/mein)? → weak almost everywhere, EXCEPT masculine nominative and neuter nominative/accusative, where it's strong.

⚠️ **Common Spanish-speaker mistake:** Spanish adjectives only ever mark gender and number (bueno/buena/buenos/buenas) — never case, since Spanish has no case system for nouns at all. The instinct to treat German adjective endings as a similarly small, fixed set (just matching gender) misses that case is doing most of the real work here — the same adjective ending changes across Nominativ/Akkusativ/Dativ/Genitiv even when gender stays constant, which has no Spanish parallel at all.

## 7. Progressively harder examples

1. Der neue Kollege ist sehr nett. *(weak, definite article, nominative)*
2. Ich habe einen neuen Kollegen kennengelernt. *(mixed, "einen" already marks masc. accusative, so weak -en)*
3. Guter Kaffee ist teuer. *(strong, no article, masculine nominative — "guter" mimics "der")*
4. Ich trinke gern kalten Tee mit frischer Zitrone. *(strong throughout — no articles, masculine accusative "kalten" mimics "den," feminine dative "frischer" mimics "der")*

## 8. Speaking exercise

Say these out loud, applying the three-step decision process:

- Der ___ (klein) Hund bellt. *(kleine — weak, definite, nom.)*
- Ich habe ___ (klein) Hund. *(einen kleinen — mixed, but "einen" already marks it, so weak -en)*
- ___ (klein) Hunde sind süß. *(Kleine — strong, no article, plural nominative mimics "die")*

Now describe one real object near you right now using an adjective, first with a definite article, then with no article at all, consciously noticing the ending change.

## 9. Thinking-in-German habit

Today, narrate a short description of something using an adjective in two different article contexts:
> *Der frische Kaffee riecht gut. Ich trinke gern frischen Kaffee am Morgen.*

Notice the same adjective "frisch" takes different endings purely based on the article situation, not based on the noun itself changing.

## 10. Real-life mission

Notice one adjective+noun phrase today (a product label, a sign, a menu item) and consciously work out why it has the ending it has, using the three-step process from this lesson.

## 11. Mini quiz

1. Which declension type applies with no article at all: weak, strong, or mixed?
2. Fill in: "Ich mag ___ (frisch) Brot." (no article — strong, neuter accusative)
3. Fill in: "Das ist ___ (alt) Auto." (no article... wait, "das" is a definite article — so weak: "das alte Auto")
4. Why does "ein gutes Kind" use a strong ending, but "einen guten Mann" uses a weak one?
