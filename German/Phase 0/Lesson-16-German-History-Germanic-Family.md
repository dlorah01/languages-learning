# Lesson 16 — Where German Comes From: History & the Germanic Family Tree

**Phase:** 0 — Foundations
**Status:** ✅ Done

---

## 1. The core idea

Understanding where German came from gives a mental map for why so much of what's already been learned works the way it does, and connects German, English, and (more distantly) Spanish into one coherent picture.

**The big picture:** German and English are close cousins. Spanish is a much more distant relative. All three trace back to Proto-Indo-European (~4,000–6,000 years ago), but split into different branches at very different points.

## 2. The family tree, simplified

```
Proto-Indo-European
├── Germanic branch
│   ├── West Germanic
│   │   ├── German
│   │   ├── English
│   │   ├── Dutch
│   │   └── Yiddish
│   └── North Germanic
│       ├── Swedish, Danish, Norwegian, Icelandic
└── Italic branch
    └── Latin
        ├── Spanish
        ├── Portuguese
        ├── French
        ├── Italian
        └── Romanian
```

**Mental model:** German and English are siblings who grew up in the same house and later moved to different cities — still sharing a huge amount of vocabulary and structure despite divergence. Spanish is more like a cousin from a different household (Latin) — related far back, but the resemblance shows up mostly in borrowed academic/scientific vocabulary, not core grammar or basic words.

## 3. What this explains about things already learned

**a) Why German and English share so much basic vocabulary:**
Haus/house, Wasser/water, Mann/man, Buch/book, trinken/drink, kommen/come — **cognates**, descended from the same Germanic root, drifted apart over ~1,500 years. A systematic sound-shift pattern: German "z" often corresponds to English "t" (Zeit/time, Herz/heart, Salz/salt); German "pf" often corresponds to English "p" (Pfund/pound, Apfel/apple).

**b) Why Spanish and German share academic vocabulary but not core vocabulary:**
Spanish descends from Latin directly. German borrowed heavily from Latin and Greek in the medieval/Renaissance periods for academic, scientific, legal, religious concepts — hence "Universität," "Information," "Musik," "Telefon," "Präsident" look familiar to a Spanish speaker, while "Haus," "trinken," "gehen" don't.

**c) Why English feels "easier" despite being the same family:**
English simplified dramatically after Old Norse contact (Viking settlement) and French contact (Norman Conquest, 1066), stripping out much of the case system and V2 word order German retained. German preserved more of the original West Germanic grammatical machinery; English streamlined it.

## 4. The High German Consonant Shift

Around the 6th–8th centuries CE, the **Second (High German) Consonant Shift** swept southern German-speaking regions but not the north (or England). This is why:
- English "make" ↔ German "machen" (k → ch)
- English "water" ↔ German "Wasser" (t → ss)
- English "ship" ↔ German "Schiff" (p → ff, roughly)
- English "pepper" ↔ German "Pfeffer" (p → pf)

Not a rule to actively apply, but recognizing these correspondences turns unfamiliar vocabulary into "the German version of a word I already know."

## 5. Why this matters for the goal

Understanding German as a living historical system, not an arbitrary rulebook, reframes "why does German do this weird thing" into "this is the trace of a specific historical event" — more memorable, and closer to how native speakers experience their own language (not as arbitrary rules, but as something that evolved naturally, the same way Spanish did).

## 6. Exercise (conceptual, lighter than usual)

Scan for cognates while listening to podcasts: every time a word sounds like English, notice it consciously — "Garten/garden," "Freund/friend," "Sommer/summer," "Winter/winter," "Hand/hand." This trains using English (not Spanish) as a decoding shortcut for unfamiliar Germanic vocabulary.

## 7. Real-life mission

Next time reading something in German — a sign, a menu, a headline — actively hunt for one English cognate before reaching for a translation app.

## 8. Mini quiz (conceptual)

2. Name one German/English cognate pair not mentioned above.
3. Why do German and Spanish share academic vocabulary despite different language branches?
4. What historical event pushed English's grammar to simplify relative to German's?
