# Lesson 23 — Passive Voice: When the Doer Disappears

**Phase:** 3 — Verbs in Depth
**Status:** ✅ Done

---

## 1. The core idea

Passive voice shifts focus from who's doing an action to what's happening to something — "the door is opened" rather than "someone opens the door." German uses passive constructions noticeably more than casual English or Spanish, especially in bureaucratic, professional, and instructional contexts — which matters directly for your life here (Behörden forms, workplace emails, product instructions all lean passive).

German actually has **two distinct passives** with genuinely different meanings, not just stylistic variants — this is the part most courses under-explain.

## 2. Vorgangspassiv — the "process" passive (an action happening)

Built with **werden** + past participle. This describes an action being done to something, with focus on the process itself:

**Die Tür wird geöffnet.** (The door is being opened / gets opened.)

- `wird` (werden, conjugated, position 2) ... `geöffnet` (past participle, final position — the same Satzklammer shape you've seen constantly)

Tense changes by conjugating `werden`:
- Present: Die Tür **wird** geöffnet.
- Past: Die Tür **wurde** geöffnet.
- Perfekt: Die Tür **ist** geöffnet **worden**. *(note: werden's own past participle in this construction is "worden," not "geworden" — a genuine exception worth memorizing as a fixed chunk)*

## 3. Zustandspassiv — the "state" passive (a resulting condition)

Built with **sein** + past participle. This describes the **resulting state** after an action, not the action itself:

**Die Tür ist geöffnet.** (The door is open — a state, describing how things currently are.)

**Mental model — this is the crucial distinction:** Vorgangspassiv (werden) is a **video** — it captures the action happening. Zustandspassiv (sein) is a **photograph** — it captures the resulting condition, frozen after the action's already done. Compare:

- Das Geschäft **wird** um 20 Uhr **geschlossen**. *(process — the shop is being closed [by someone] at 8pm, an event happening)*
- Das Geschäft **ist** geschlossen. *(state — the shop is closed, describing its current condition, possibly for hours or days)*

⚠️ **Common Spanish-speaker mistake:** Spanish's "ser" vs. "estar" distinction for passive-like constructions ("la puerta es abierta" vs. "la puerta está abierta") maps surprisingly well onto werden vs. sein here — genuinely one of the few places Spanish intuition transfers cleanly. The trap is instead purely mechanical: forgetting that German's Zustandspassiv uses **sein**, not werden, since English "is closed" gives no visual cue about which auxiliary German wants.

## 4. Mentioning who's doing it: von vs. durch

Passive sentences often omit the doer entirely (that's often the point — de-emphasizing who acted), but when you do want to mention them:

- **von** + Dativ — for an active agent (a person or force actively doing something): Das Buch wurde **von** der Autorin geschrieben.
- **durch** + Akkusativ — for a means, cause, or indirect mechanism: Die Straße wurde **durch** den Sturm beschädigt. *(the storm as a cause/mechanism, not a conscious agent)*

**Mental model:** von = "by someone/something with agency," durch = "by means of/because of, no real agency." A person almost always takes von; a natural force or process often takes durch.

## 5. Why German uses passive more than you'd expect — and the alternative you'll hear constantly

Passive is everywhere in German instructions, signs, and bureaucracy — "Hier wird nicht geraucht" (No smoking here — literally "here is not smoked"), "Rauchen ist hier verboten." But in casual speech, Germans very often prefer **man** + active verb as a lighter alternative to full passive:

- Passive: Hier wird Deutsch gesprochen. *(German is spoken here)*
- Man-construction: Hier spricht man Deutsch. *(one speaks German here — same meaning, more conversational)*

You met "man" briefly in Lesson 6 (sollen example) — it's genuinely one of your best tools for sounding natural while avoiding full passive constructions in speech, since passive can feel a bit stiff/written in casual conversation, similar to Genitiv's register profile from Lesson 12.

## 6. Progressively harder examples

1. Das Formular muss ausgefüllt werden. *(modal + passive infinitive — an extremely common bureaucratic construction: "werden" as a bare infinitive at the very end, after the modal, forming a passive-under-a-modal structure)*
2. Die Fenster wurden gestern geputzt, und jetzt sind sie sauber. *(Vorgangspassiv describing the cleaning event, Zustandspassiv-adjacent "sauber" describing the result — notice "sauber" here is just an adjective, not technically Zustandspassiv, but the same conceptual contrast)*
3. Der Brief wurde von meinem Kollegen geschrieben, weil ich keine Zeit hatte. *(von + weil, two lessons stacked)*
4. Das Gebäude wurde durch das Erdbeben stark beschädigt. *(durch — impersonal cause)*
5. Man sagt, dass dieses Restaurant sehr gut ist. *(man + dass — natural alternative to a passive "es wird gesagt, dass...")*

## 7. Speaking exercise

Say these out loud, choosing werden or sein based on process vs. state:

- Der Laden ___ um 18 Uhr geschlossen. *(wird — process, the closing event)*
- Der Laden ___ schon geschlossen. *(ist — state, already closed)*
- Das Auto ___ repariert. *(context-dependent! "wird repariert" = being fixed right now; "ist repariert" = already fixed)*

Now produce, out loud, one modal+passive sentence about something that "must be done" in your own life right now — "Die Wäsche muss gewaschen werden" or similar.

## 8. Thinking-in-German habit

Today, silently narrate one task using modal+passive, and rephrase the same idea using "man":
> *Das Zimmer muss aufgeräumt werden. Eigentlich räumt man das Zimmer jeden Tag auf, aber ich habe es heute noch nicht gemacht.*

## 9. Real-life mission

Notice one passive construction today in real life — a sign, an instruction, an email at work ("Bitte werden Sie gebeten...") — and consciously identify whether it's Vorgangspassiv or Zustandspassiv.
