# Lesson 24 — Reported Speech & Konjunktiv I: The Journalist's Mood

**Phase:** 3 — Verbs in Depth
**Status:** ✅ Done

---

## 1. The core idea

Konjunktiv I is a verb mood used almost exclusively for **reported speech** — repeating what someone else said without claiming it's true yourself. English does this with "he said that..." plus normal grammar; Spanish shifts tense (present → imperfect) inside reported speech. German instead shifts the **verb form itself** into a distinct mood, precisely to signal "this is someone else's claim, not necessarily fact."

**Er sagt, er sei krank.** (He says he is [claims to be] sick.)

Compare to simply reporting a fact: **Er ist krank.** (He is sick — you're asserting this yourself.)

**Mental model:** Konjunktiv I is a **quotation mark built into the verb**. It lets a speaker report someone else's claim while staying neutral about whether it's actually true — genuinely useful for journalism, gossip, workplace communication ("She said the meeting is at 3" without vouching for it), and any situation where you want distance from a claim's truth.

## 2. Where you'll actually encounter this: reading, not speaking

Be honest with yourself about priorities here: Konjunktiv I is heavily used in **written news reporting** and **formal indirect speech**, but in casual spoken German, most speakers just use **dass + normal indicative**, or skip "dass" and use normal word order with a comma:

- Formal/written: Er sagte, er **sei** müde. *(Konjunktiv I)*
- Casual/spoken: Er sagte, dass er müde **ist**. / Er sagte, er **ist** müde. *(plain indicative — what you'll actually say and hear constantly)*

Given your priorities (speaking first), treat this lesson primarily as a **reading/listening skill** — recognizing Konjunktiv I when you encounter it in news articles, formal reports, or literature — rather than something you need to actively produce yet. That said, a few fixed phrases are worth having ready for polished spoken use (section 5).

## 3. The forms — built from the infinitive stem

Konjunktiv I takes the infinitive stem and adds a specific set of endings, largely independent of the normal present-tense conjugation:

| Person | Ending | sein (irregular) | haben | machen |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ich | -e | sei | habe | mache |
| du | -est | seiest | habest | machest |
| er/sie/es | -e | sei | habe | mache |
| wir | -en | seien | haben | machen |
| ihr | -et | seiet | habet | machet |
| sie/Sie | -en | seien | haben | machen |

Notice: for most verbs, the **ich, wir, sie/Sie** forms look identical to normal indicative present tense — which means Konjunktiv I is often only *visibly* distinct in the **er/sie/es** and **du** forms. This is why "er sei" (not "er ist") is the form you'll actually notice most in news reporting — third person is where the mood shows up clearly.

## 4. When Konjunktiv I collapses into Konjunktiv II

Here's a genuinely useful practical rule: when a Konjunktiv I form is **identical** to the normal indicative (which happens often, especially in plural forms), German speakers/writers switch to **Konjunktiv II** instead, purely to keep the "this is reported, not asserted" signal visible:

- "sie haben" (indicative) = "sie haben" (Konjunktiv I, identical!) → switches to **sie hätten** (Konjunktiv II) to stay distinguishable.

You'll meet Konjunktiv II properly next lesson — for now, just recognize that these two moods are functionally linked: Konjunktiv I is the "default" reported-speech mood, and Konjunktiv II steps in as a backup whenever Konjunktiv I would be invisible.

## 5. A few genuinely useful spoken fixed phrases

Even though full Konjunktiv I production isn't a speaking priority, a handful of fixed expressions using it are common enough in everyday register to learn as chunks:

- **Es sei denn** — unless (literally "it be denied" — a fossilized Konjunktiv I phrase): Ich komme, es sei denn, es regnet. *(I'm coming, unless it rains.)*
- **Wie dem auch sei** — be that as it may / whatever the case: a genuinely common conversational filler.
- **Lang lebe der König** — long live the king (the classic "may it be" construction pattern, useful for recognizing the mood's core meaning: a wish or claim, not an assertion of fact)

## 6. Progressively harder examples (mostly for recognition)

1. Die Regierung erklärte, die Situation sei unter Kontrolle. *(classic news-reporting register)*
2. Er behauptet, er habe das Geld nie bekommen. *(reported claim, neutral distance from truth)*
3. Sie sagte, sie komme später, weil sie noch arbeiten müsse. *(Konjunktiv I stacked with weil — notice "müsse" is also Konjunktiv I, modal verbs get the treatment too)*
4. Ich komme heute Abend vorbei, es sei denn, du hast schon Pläne. *(the one genuinely spoken-register use from section 5)*

## 7. Speaking exercise (light — recognition-focused this time)

Read these out loud and identify whether each is Konjunktiv I or plain indicative, then say the casual spoken alternative:

- Er sagt, er sei beschäftigt. → casual version: *Er sagt, er ist beschäftigt.*
- Sie behauptete, es gebe kein Problem. → casual version: *Sie behauptete, dass es kein Problem gibt.*

Now produce, out loud, one sentence using "es sei denn" about a real plan of yours — "Ich gehe heute spazieren, es sei denn, es regnet."

## 8. Thinking-in-German habit

Today, silently practice reporting something someone told you recently, first in casual spoken form (dass + indicative), then noticing how a news article would phrase the same claim with Konjunktiv I:
> *Mein Kollege sagte, dass das Meeting verschoben wurde. (spoken) / Der Kollege erklärte, das Meeting sei verschoben worden. (written/formal, for recognition only)*

## 9. Real-life mission

Find one German news headline or article snippet today (even just browsing) and try to spot a Konjunktiv I verb form — "sei," "habe," "könne," "müsse" are the most common ones to watch for.
