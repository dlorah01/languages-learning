# Lesson 35 — Discourse Markers & Spoken Shortcuts: How Real Conversations Actually Flow

**Phase:** 5 — The Native Layer
**Status:** ✅ Done

---

## 1. The core idea

Every grammar lesson so far has taught you "complete" sentences. Real spoken German — the kind you hear in your podcasts, at work, with neighbors — is full of **fillers, contractions, and connector words** that organize conversation without adding grammatical content. This lesson catalogs the ones you'll hear constantly and should start using, since their absence is one more marker of "textbook German" rather than lived-in German.

## 2. Filler words and hesitation markers

German fillers work similarly to English "um," "like," "you know" — they buy thinking time and signal you're not finished speaking:

- **äh / ähm** — the direct equivalent of "um/uh," extremely common, completely normal even in professional speech
- **also** — genuinely one of the most useful words in casual German, meaning roughly "so / well then," used to open a thought, transition, or summarize: **Also, ich denke...** *(So, I think...)*
- **naja** — "well..." with a slight reluctant/hedging flavor: **Naja, es geht so.** *(Well, it's so-so.)*
- **halt** — you met this in Lesson 32 as a modal particle ("that's just how it is"), but it also functions as a pure filler mid-sentence: **Ich wollte halt nicht stören.** *(I just, you know, didn't want to bother [you].)*
- **sozusagen** — "so to speak," used when hedging a word choice: **Er ist, sozusagen, mein Chef.**

## 3. Discourse organizers — structuring what you're about to say

These help sequence and connect ideas in a conversation, similar to English "first of all," "by the way," "anyway":

- **Übrigens** (by the way) — Übrigens, hast du das schon gehört? *(By the way, have you heard about this?)*
- **Erstens... zweitens... drittens...** (firstly... secondly... thirdly...) — genuinely used in spoken explanations, not just formal writing
- **Jedenfalls** (anyway / in any case) — used to return to a main point after a digression: Jedenfalls, wie ich schon sagte... *(Anyway, as I was saying...)*
- **Trotzdem** (nevertheless/still) — you've seen "trotz" as a genitive preposition (Lesson 12); "trotzdem" is its adverbial cousin, used to pivot back after acknowledging a counterpoint: Es regnet, trotzdem gehe ich raus. *(It's raining, but I'm going out anyway.)*
- **Außerdem** (besides/furthermore) — adds an additional point: Ich habe keine Zeit, außerdem bin ich müde. *(I don't have time, besides I'm tired.)*

## 4. Common contractions in casual spoken German

Native speech routinely contracts small words together — recognizing these is essential for listening comprehension, since your podcasts will use them constantly even though textbooks write things out in full:

- **'nen** = einen: Ich hab 'nen Termin. *(colloquial for "Ich habe einen Termin.")*
- **haste / biste / kannste** = hast du / bist du / kannst du: Haste Zeit? *(very casual, common among younger/informal speakers)*
- **wa?** = a casual tag question, roughly "right?" (regional, especially northern Germany): Das ist cool, wa?
- **drauf / drin / dran / drum** = darauf/darin/daran/darum (casual contractions of the da-compounds from Lesson 15): Ich hab keinen Bock drauf. *(I don't feel like it — genuinely very common casual phrase)*

⚠️ **A note on register:** these contractions are genuinely common in casual spoken German among friends/informal contexts, but would sound out of place in professional writing or formal speech. Recognizing them for listening is essential immediately; actively producing the heaviest contractions (haste, biste) is optional and depends on how casual you want to sound — but "drauf/drin/dran" contractions are common enough across registers that they're worth adopting actively.

## 5. Genuinely useful conversational connectors for your speaking priority

These are worth actively building into your speech now, since they make you sound like you're genuinely engaging in a conversation rather than delivering isolated sentences:

- **Stimmt!** (True! / That's right!) — a quick, natural agreement, more conversational than "Das ist richtig"
- **Genau!** (Exactly!) — extremely common agreement marker, genuinely one of the most-used words in casual German conversation
- **Echt?** (Really?) — casual surprise/interest marker, inviting the other person to continue
- **Ach so!** (Oh I see! / Ah, got it!) — signals sudden understanding, genuinely very common and useful
- **Keine Sorge.** (No worries / don't worry.) — reassurance, connects to Lesson 34's "das ist mir egal" register of everyday reassurance phrases

## 6. Progressively harder examples

1. Also, ich denke, wir sollten jetzt gehen. *(also as a conversation-opener)*
2. Genau, das stimmt! Übrigens, hast du das neue Restaurant schon ausprobiert? *(agreement + topic shift)*
3. Ich hab halt keine Lust drauf, aber jedenfalls, lass uns weitermachen. *(halt as filler + drauf contraction + jedenfalls as return-to-topic)*
4. Naja, es ist kompliziert — außerdem habe ich noch keine Zeit gehabt, mich richtig damit zu beschäftigen. *(naja hedge + außerdem + infinitive construction from Lesson 27)*

## 7. Speaking exercise

Say these out loud, practicing the natural rhythm of fillers and connectors:

- Also... ich glaube, das ist eine gute Idee.
- Ach so! Das wusste ich nicht.
- Genau, das ist richtig.

Now narrate a short opinion on any topic out loud, deliberately including "also" to open, one filler ("äh" or "halt") mid-thought, and "jedenfalls" to close.

## 8. Thinking-in-German habit

Today, notice how much your internal monologue has been "clean," textbook-style sentences — try adding natural discourse organization to a silent thought:
> *Also, ich muss heute noch einkaufen gehen. Ach so, und ich sollte auch meine Mutter anrufen. Naja, mal sehen, ob ich Zeit für beides habe.*

## 9. Real-life mission

Use "Genau!" as your agreement response at least once today instead of a plain "Ja" — it's genuinely one of the most common, low-risk, high-payoff swaps for sounding more natural in daily conversation.
