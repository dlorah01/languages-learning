# Lesson 37 — Register Control: Sie vs. du and When to Switch

**Phase:** 5 — The Native Layer (final lesson of this phase)
**Status:** ✅ Done

---

## 1. The core idea

This closes Phase 5 with the single most socially consequential grammar point in German: the Sie/du distinction. Spanish has a similar formal/informal split (usted/tú), so the *concept* isn't new — but German's social rules for when to switch, who initiates, and what happens if you get it wrong are distinct enough from Spanish/Colombian norms to deserve dedicated attention, especially since you live here and navigate this constantly.

## 2. The mechanics, briefly (you've been using both already)

**Sie** (formal "you," capitalized always, even mid-sentence) takes third-person-plural-style verb conjugation:
- Wie geht es Ihnen? *(How are you? — formal)*
- Haben Sie Zeit? *(Do you have time? — formal)*

**du** (informal "you") takes the familiar second-person conjugation you've used throughout this course:
- Wie geht's dir? *(How are you? — informal)*
- Hast du Zeit?

You've been producing "du" forms constantly in this course's exercises, which was a deliberate default choice — but real life requires knowing when Sie is actually expected.

## 3. When Sie is the default — genuinely important for daily life in Germany

- **Any stranger, especially adults you don't know:** shopkeepers, government office (Amt) staff, doctors, most service interactions
- **Professional contexts with people you don't know well:** new colleagues, clients, your boss (unless explicitly invited to use du — see below)
- **Anyone noticeably older than you, in a first interaction**
- **Written formal communication:** official emails, letters, forms

**Mental model:** think of Sie as the **default starting position** for any adult stranger or professional relationship — similar to Spanish "usted" with someone you've just met, but German extends this default further into ongoing professional life than many Latin American workplace cultures do. Colombian workplace culture often shifts to "tú" faster among colleagues than German workplace culture shifts to "du" — this is a genuine cultural calibration point worth being deliberate about.

## 4. When du is the default

- **Children, teenagers, and (usually) fellow students**
- **Close friends, family, romantic partners** — obviously
- **Increasingly common in casual/creative workplaces, tech startups, and among younger colleagues** — genuinely shifting over recent years, so context-dependent
- **Universal online/social media culture and among younger Germans generally** — du has become the default in many digital and youth contexts even with strangers

## 5. Who initiates the switch, and how

This is the part with genuine social weight, unlike English (which has no equivalent decision point at all): switching from Sie to du is a **social event**, not just a grammar choice, and there's a real etiquette around who offers it:

- **Traditionally, the older or higher-status person offers the switch** to the younger/lower-status person — a boss to an employee, an older person to a younger one. Offering it yourself, upward, can read as presumptuous.
- The actual phrase for this moment: **"Wollen wir uns duzen?"** (Shall we use "du" with each other? — literally "shall we du each other," using "duzen" as a genuine verb meaning "to address with du") or simply **"Sagen wir doch du zueinander."** (Let's just say "du" to each other.)
- Once offered and accepted, it's often marked with a small ritual — clinking glasses, a handshake, sometimes literally saying **"Ich bin [Name]"** as the two people re-introduce themselves on first-name/du terms, since Sie often pairs with last names (Herr/Frau + Nachname) while du pairs with first names.
- **Siezen** (to address with Sie) and **duzen** (to address with du) are both real, commonly used verbs worth knowing — Wir siezen uns noch. *(We still use "Sie" with each other.)*

⚠️ **Common Spanish-speaker mistake, specifically for Colombians:** Colombian Spanish (particularly certain regions) sometimes uses "usted" even among close friends and family in ways that don't map onto a formality signal at all — this is a genuinely different system from Spain/Mexico's usted/tú split, and definitely different from German's Sie/du, which is consistently tied to formality/social distance, not regional habit or affection. Don't assume your Colombian usted/tú instincts transfer directly; German's system, while conceptually similar to *standard* Spanish formality marking, is more rigidly tied to social status/context than some Colombian usage patterns.

## 6. What happens if you get it wrong

Using "du" prematurely with someone expecting Sie can land as overly familiar or even disrespectful, especially with older people or in formal/bureaucratic contexts — genuinely worth erring toward Sie when uncertain, since the social cost of over-formality is much lower than the cost of premature familiarity. Conversely, sticking rigidly to Sie once someone has clearly signaled a wish to switch to du can read as cold or standoffish. The safe default, especially as a foreigner still calibrating: **start with Sie in any ambiguous adult context, and let the other person (or an obvious peer-group norm) guide the switch.**

## 7. Register beyond Sie/du: politeness layering with Konjunktiv II

Recall Lesson 25's Konjunktiv II politeness forms — these stack with Sie/du choice to create a full spectrum of formality:

- Most formal: **Könnten Sie mir bitte helfen?** (Could you please help me? — Sie + Konjunktiv II + bitte)
- Formal-neutral: **Können Sie mir helfen?** (Sie + present tense)
- Casual-polite: **Kannst du mir helfen?** (du + present tense)
- Very casual: **Hilfst du mir mal?** (du + mal, from Lesson 32's modal particles)

**Mental model:** think of formality as a dial with multiple independent controls — Sie/du, Konjunktiv II vs. present tense, and modal particles all contribute independently to the overall formality "volume" of a sentence. You can mix and match based on context, not just flip one binary switch.

## 8. Progressively harder examples

1. Entschuldigung, könnten Sie mir sagen, wo der Bahnhof ist? *(maximally polite, stranger on the street)*
2. Hey, kannst du mir kurz helfen? *(casual, a friend or established du-relationship)*
3. Wollen wir uns duzen? Ich bin Sofía. *(the switch-offer itself, with self-reintroduction)*
4. Wir siezen uns noch, aber wir arbeiten schon seit einem Jahr zusammen. *(describing an ongoing Sie relationship, using "siezen" as a verb, plus seit from Lesson 13)*

## 9. Speaking exercise

Say these out loud, choosing Sie or du based on context:

- Talking to your landlord for the first time: "___ Sie mir bitte sagen, wann die Miete fällig ist?" *(Könnten)*
- Talking to a close friend: "___ du heute Zeit?" *(Hast)*
- A colleague you've worked with for two years suggests switching: "Wollen wir uns ___?" *(duzen)*

Now think of three real relationships in your life here (a colleague, a neighbor, a shop you frequent) and consciously identify which register you currently use with each, and whether it matches the norms from this lesson.
