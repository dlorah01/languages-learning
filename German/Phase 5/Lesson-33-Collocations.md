# Lesson 33 — Collocations: The Word Pairs That Make You Sound Native

**Phase:** 5 — The Native Layer
**Status:** ✅ Done

---

## 1. The core idea

A collocation is a pairing of words that native speakers use together habitually, even when other combinations would be grammatically valid and technically understandable. English "make a decision" (not "do a decision"), Spanish "tomar una decisión" (not "hacer una decisión") — every language has these arbitrary-feeling but rock-solid pairings. German is no different, and this lesson starts building your collocation vocabulary deliberately, since this is genuinely one of the biggest gaps between "grammatically correct" and "sounds native."

**Mental model:** think of collocations as **word marriages** — the words aren't logically forced to pair up, but they've been "together" in the language's habits for so long that swapping one partner out sounds immediately foreign, even if the substitute is a perfectly good synonym in isolation.

## 2. Verb + noun collocations — the biggest category

German has strong habits about which verb pairs with which noun, and these frequently don't match English/Spanish logic:

- **eine Entscheidung treffen** (to make a decision — literally "to hit/strike a decision," not "machen")
- **Angst haben** (to be afraid — literally "to have fear," not "sein ängstlich" as a default)
- **Zeit verbringen** (to spend time — "verbringen," not the more literal-seeming "ausgeben," which is reserved for spending money)
- **Geld ausgeben** (to spend money — "ausgeben" here, the counterpart distinction from above)
- **einen Fehler machen** (to make a mistake — this one DOES match English/Spanish logic, a nice break)
- **Erfolg haben** (to be successful — literally "to have success")
- **eine Frage stellen** (to ask a question — literally "to place/pose a question," not "fragen eine Frage")
- **Rücksicht nehmen (auf)** (to be considerate of/take into account — literally "to take backward-view")

⚠️ **Common Spanish-speaker mistake:** the instinct to default to "machen" (make/do) for any "make X" collocation, since it's the closest general-purpose verb, is extremely common and immediately noticeable — "eine Entscheidung machen" instead of "treffen" is a textbook non-native error. There's no shortcut here: each verb+noun collocation genuinely needs individual memorization, ideally as a fixed chunk rather than two separate vocabulary items.

## 3. Adjective + preposition collocations

Certain adjectives pair with fixed prepositions, similar to Lesson 15's verb+preposition combos, but for adjectives instead:

- **stolz auf** (proud of) — Ich bin stolz auf dich.
- **zufrieden mit** (satisfied with) — Ich bin zufrieden mit meiner Arbeit.
- **böse auf** (mad/angry at [someone]) — Sie ist böse auf mich.
- **verantwortlich für** (responsible for) — Er ist verantwortlich für das Projekt.
- **abhängig von** (dependent on) — connects back to Lesson 15's abhängen von, same preposition logic extends to the adjective form.

## 4. Adverb intensifier collocations — degree words that pair habitually

German has a rich system of degree/intensity adverbs that don't just mean "very" interchangeably — they carry different registers and habitually pair with different kinds of adjectives:

- **sehr** — the default, neutral "very": sehr gut, sehr müde
- **ziemlich** — "fairly/rather," slightly less strong than sehr, common in casual speech: ziemlich kalt heute
- **echt** — "really/genuinely," very casual/colloquial: echt cool, echt schade *(schade = a shame/pity — genuinely common expression)*
- **total** — casual intensifier, similar register to "echt": total verrückt *(totally crazy)*
- **absolut** — stronger, more formal/emphatic: absolut notwendig *(absolutely necessary)*

**Mental model:** these aren't interchangeable synonyms — they're different **registers of the same underlying "very"** — sehr is your safe neutral default, echt/total are casual-conversational, absolut is formal/emphatic. Matching the wrong register (using "absolut" in a casual chat, or "echt" in a formal email) is a subtle but real naturalness signal.

## 5. Genuinely common fixed everyday expressions worth learning as chunks

Beyond structured categories, some phrases are just worth memorizing directly because of sheer frequency:

- **Es kommt darauf an.** (It depends. — connects to Lesson 15's da-compounds directly: darauf ankommen)
- **Das macht nichts.** (That's okay / no big deal / it doesn't matter.)
- **Kein Problem.** (No problem — genuinely identical to English here)
- **Auf jeden Fall.** (Definitely / in any case.)
- **Es tut mir leid.** (I'm sorry.)
- **Das kommt drauf an, ob...** (It depends on whether... — the extended version of the darauf-ankommen phrase, using "drauf" as the casual spoken contraction of "darauf")

## 6. Progressively harder examples

1. Ich muss eine wichtige Entscheidung treffen, aber ich habe Angst vor Fehlern. *(two collocations + a Lesson 15 fixed preposition, "Angst vor")*
2. Sie ist total zufrieden mit ihrem neuen Job. *(intensifier + adjective+prep collocation)*
3. Es kommt darauf an, wie viel Zeit wir haben. *(fixed phrase + da-compound + embedded question from Lesson 4's logic)*
4. Ich bin stolz auf dich, weil du so viel Erfolg hattest. *(adjective collocation + weil + verb+noun collocation)*

## 7. Speaking exercise

Say these out loud, filling in the correct collocation verb or preposition:

- Ich muss eine Entscheidung ___. *(treffen)*
- Ich bin stolz ___ meine Familie. *(auf)*
- Das ___ nichts. *(macht)*
- Ich habe ___ vor der Prüfung. *(Angst)*

Now produce, out loud, one true sentence using "eine Entscheidung treffen" about something real in your life, and one using "es kommt darauf an" answering a hypothetical question.

## 8. Thinking-in-German habit

Today, silently narrate a small decision or plan using at least two collocations from this lesson:
> *Ich muss eine Entscheidung treffen. Es kommt darauf an, ob ich Zeit habe. Ich bin ziemlich müde, also werde ich wahrscheinlich früh schlafen gehen.*

## 9. Real-life mission

Use "Es kommt darauf an" out loud today when answering a question that genuinely has a conditional answer — a very natural, very common, very native-sounding phrase for hedging an answer.
