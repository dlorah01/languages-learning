# Lesson 36 — Austrian & Swiss German: What Changes Outside Germany

**Phase:** 5 — The Native Layer
**Status:** ✅ Done

---

## 1. The core idea

Standard German (Hochdeutsch) as taught in this course is genuinely universal — Austrians and Swiss speakers write and mostly speak formally in the same standard German you're learning. But living in the German-speaking world, you'll encounter genuine differences in vocabulary, pronunciation, and a few grammar points when dealing with Austrian or Swiss German speakers, media, or travel. This lesson gives you a working map so these differences register as "oh, that's the Austrian/Swiss version" rather than confusion.

## 2. Why these differences exist — a quick connection to Lesson 16

Recall Lesson 16's Germanic family tree: German dialects spread across a large geographic area, and Austrian German and Swiss German (specifically Swiss Standard German, distinct from spoken Swiss dialects/Schwyzerdütsch, which is almost its own language) developed under different political and cultural histories — Austria as its own long-standing state with its own administrative vocabulary, Switzerland shaped by its multilingual federal structure and relative isolation from German unification. These aren't "incorrect German" — they're standard, legitimate national varieties, similar to how British, American, and Australian English are all standard despite real differences.

## 3. Vocabulary differences — the most noticeable layer

**Food and everyday life** (genuinely useful for travel or dealing with Austrian/Swiss colleagues):

| Germany | Austria | Switzerland | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kartoffel | Erdapfel | Härdöpfel | potato |
| Tomate | Paradeiser | — | tomato |
| Sahne | Obers | Rahm | cream |
| Januar | Jänner | Januar | January |
| Junge | Bub | Bueb | boy |

**Administrative/bureaucratic vocabulary** (especially relevant if you ever deal with Austrian institutions):
- Führerschein (Germany) = Führerschein (Austria, same) but "Billa," "Spar" are Austrian supermarket chains you'd need to know locally, similar to how "Rewe" or "Edeka" are German-specific.

## 4. Pronunciation differences

**Austrian German** tends toward:
- A generally softer, more melodic intonation pattern than standard German German
- Word-final "-ig" pronounced as [ɪk] rather than northern German's [ɪç] (so "richtig" sounds closer to "richtik") — actually this matches SOUTHERN German pronunciation broadly, not just Austrian specifically, worth knowing since you'll hear regional variation within Germany too

**Swiss Standard German** tends toward:
- No voiced consonants at the start of words the way northern German has them — Swiss speakers often pronounce "b," "d," "g" closer to their unvoiced counterparts
- A notably different "r" sound, sometimes closer to a rolled/tapped r reminiscent of Spanish, genuinely easier for you specifically to produce than the standard uvular r from Lesson 18
- Swiss German speakers switch to Swiss Standard German (written/formal) for writing and formal contexts, but speak actual Swiss dialects (Schwyzerdütsch) casually — these dialects are different enough from standard German that even native German speakers from Germany often can't understand them without exposure. Don't expect to understand casual Swiss German conversation even once your standard German is excellent — that's a genuinely separate skill.

## 5. A few grammar differences worth knowing

**Perfekt with "sein" more broadly in Austrian/southern German:** some verbs that take "haben" in northern standard German take "sein" in Austrian and southern German usage — e.g., "ich bin gesessen" (Austrian/southern) vs. "ich habe gesessen" (standard northern German) for "I sat/have sat." Not something to actively worry about producing, but useful to recognize if you hear it and think it's an error — it isn't, it's regional standard usage.

**Diminutives are more common in Austrian and southern German speech generally** — connecting back to Lesson 28's -chen/-lein neuter diminutive rule, southern/Austrian speech uses diminutives (often -erl instead of -chen: Kaffeerl instead of Kaffeechen/just Kaffee) more casually and frequently than northern German speech, which uses them more sparingly.

## 6. Why this matters for you specifically, living in Germany

Given that you live in Germany (not Austria or Switzerland), this lesson is primarily a **recognition/travel/media** skill rather than something requiring active production shift. You'll encounter these differences through: Austrian/Swiss colleagues or friends, travel, German-language media that includes Austrian/Swiss voices (news, entertainment), and potentially some regional variation even within Germany's own south (Bavaria shares some features with Austrian German, given geographic proximity). Treat this lesson as building tolerance for variation, not as a competing standard to learn actively — your standard German remains the right target for daily life in Germany.

## 7. Progressively harder examples (mostly for recognition)

1. Kannst du mir bitte die Erdäpfel geben? *(Austrian — "kannst du mir bitte die Kartoffeln geben?" in standard German)*
2. Ich bin heute lange im Büro gesessen. *(Austrian/southern sein-usage, vs. northern "ich habe gesessen")*
3. Ich hätte gern einen Kaffee mit Obers. *(Austrian — "Sahne" in standard German)*
4. Grüezi! Wie gaht's dir? *(genuine Swiss German dialect greeting — not standard German at all, included just so you recognize it exists; "Grüezi" = hello, roughly, in Swiss dialect)*

## 8. Speaking exercise (recognition-focused)

Say these standard German phrases out loud, then say the Austrian equivalent using this lesson's vocabulary table:

- Ich esse gern Kartoffeln. → Austrian: *Ich esse gern Erdäpfel.*
- Es ist Januar. → Austrian: *Es ist Jänner.*

No production drilling needed beyond this — the goal is recognition, not fluency in the variants.

## 9. Thinking-in-German habit

No new habit today — instead, if you listen to any Austrian or Swiss content this week (news, a show), consciously notice one vocabulary or pronunciation difference and mentally file it as "that's regional, not an error."

## 10. Real-life mission

If you ever interact with an Austrian or Swiss person (colleague, at a hostel while traveling, etc.), listen for one of the vocabulary differences from this lesson and see if you can identify it in real time.
