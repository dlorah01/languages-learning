# Lesson 22 — Futur I & II: Talking About What Hasn't Happened Yet

**Phase:** 3 — Verbs in Depth
**Status:** ✅ Done

---

## 1. The core idea

German has two grammatical future tenses, but the twist is the same shape as Lesson 20's Perfekt/Präteritum surprise: **native speakers barely use Futur I in casual speech.** Present tense + a time word does most of the everyday work. Understanding why, and when Futur I actually does get used, is more useful than memorizing the conjugation.

## 2. Futur I: werden + infinitive

Structurally, this is a modal-style Satzklammer you already know from Lesson 6 — `werden` conjugates in position 2, the main verb stays as an infinitive at the end:

**Ich werde morgen arbeiten.** (I will work tomorrow.)

- `werde` (conjugated werden, position 2) ... `arbeiten` (infinitive, final position)

## 3. Why present tense usually wins instead

In everyday spoken German, the present tense + a time marker (morgen, nächste Woche, bald) is the default way to talk about the future — genuinely more common than Futur I for straightforward planned events:

- Ich **arbeite** morgen. *(present tense — this is what a native speaker says, not "ich werde morgen arbeiten")*
- Wir **fahren** nächstes Jahr nach Italien.

**Mental model:** think of German future expression like English's "I'm working tomorrow" vs. "I will work tomorrow" — both exist, but the present-progressive version is the unmarked, default choice for scheduled/planned events, while "will" gets reserved for something more specific. German present tense plays that same "default future" role; werden-Futur is reserved for the cases below.

## 4. When Futur I actually gets used

Futur I isn't obsolete — it does real work in specific situations:

**a) Predictions and assumptions (often with "wohl" or "wahrscheinlich"):**
- Es wird morgen wohl regnen. *(It'll probably rain tomorrow — a genuine prediction, not a scheduled fact)*

**b) Promises, resolutions, and emphasis:**
- Ich werde das nie wieder tun! *(I will never do that again! — emotional emphasis, not neutral scheduling)*

**c) Expressing present-tense probability (a genuinely useful, distinct function — see below)**

⚠️ **Common Spanish-speaker mistake:** Spanish future tense ("trabajaré mañana") is used comfortably and neutrally for scheduled future events, so the instinct is to reach for werden-Futur the same way. In German this reads as slightly more formal/emphatic than intended — default to present tense + time word for ordinary plans, and save werden for genuine predictions or emphasis.

## 5. The surprising extra function: Futur I as present-tense probability

This is worth flagging explicitly because it has nothing to do with future time at all — Futur I can express a guess about **right now**:

- Er wird wohl zu Hause sein. *(He's probably at home [right now] — not "he will be at home" in the future sense at all!)*
- Sie wird wohl krank sein, deswegen ist sie nicht hier. *(She's probably sick, that's why she's not here.)*

**Mental model:** werden here isn't marking future time, it's marking **uncertainty/inference** — "I'm inferring this is probably true." This overlaps conceptually with modal verbs' role (Lesson 6) of expressing degrees of certainty, just via a different grammatical route.

## 6. Futur II: the future-perfect, for finished-by-a-future-point actions

Less common, but worth recognizing: werden + past participle + haben/sein (infinitive) — expressing something that **will have been completed** by some future point, or (paralleling section 5) a guess about something **already completed**:

- Bis nächste Woche werde ich das Projekt fertig gemacht haben. *(By next week I will have finished the project.)*
- Er wird wohl schon angekommen sein. *(He's probably already arrived — a guess about a completed past action, the Futur II equivalent of section 5's trick)*

You won't use Futur II often in speech, but recognizing it when you hear or read it (news, formal writing) is worth having.

## 7. Progressively harder examples

1. Ich fahre nächste Woche nach Spanien. *(present tense as default future — most natural option)*
2. Es wird bestimmt bald regnen, der Himmel ist schon grau. *(genuine prediction — Futur I earns its place here)*
3. Ich werde nie vergessen, wie schön dieser Tag war. *(emphasis/promise usage)*
4. Er ist nicht ans Telefon gegangen — er wird wohl beschäftigt sein. *(present-probability usage, Section 5)*
5. Bis Ende des Jahres werde ich mein B2-Zertifikat bekommen haben. *(Futur II, future-perfect — a genuine goal statement for your own learning journey!)*

## 8. Speaking exercise

Say these out loud, choosing present tense or Futur I appropriately:

- (scheduled plan) Ich ___ morgen einen Termin beim Arzt. *(habe — present, most natural)*
- (prediction) Es ___ wahrscheinlich kalt ___ morgen. *(wird ... sein — Futur I, genuine prediction)*
- (present guess) Sie antwortet nicht — sie ___ wohl beschäftigt ___. *(wird ... sein — probability, not future time)*

Now produce, out loud, one genuine prediction about the weather or your week using Futur I with "wohl" or "wahrscheinlich," and one plan using simple present tense + a time word.

## 9. Thinking-in-German habit

Today, narrate one actual plan using present tense + time word, and one genuine guess/prediction using Futur I with "wohl":
> *Ich arbeite morgen von zu Hause. Es wird wohl regnen, also bleibe ich drinnen.*

## 10. Real-life mission

Use present tense + a time word to describe a real upcoming plan today ("Ich fahre nächste Woche nach ...") — resist the urge to reach for werden here, since this is exactly the context where natives skip it.
