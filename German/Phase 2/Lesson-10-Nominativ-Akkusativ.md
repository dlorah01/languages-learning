# Lesson 10 — Why Cases Exist: Nominativ and Akkusativ

**Phase:** 2 — The Case System
**Status:** ✅ Done

---

## 1. The core idea — cases solve a problem word order solves in English/Spanish

English and Spanish track "who's doing what to whom" mostly through word order. "The dog bites the man" vs. "The man bites the dog" mean opposite things purely from position.

German can be far more flexible with word order (Lesson 1: front almost anything into position 1) precisely because it has a backup system for tracking roles: **case**. The article/ending on a noun tells you its grammatical role, so the noun can move around without the meaning breaking.

- Der Hund beißt den Mann. (The dog bites the man.)
- Den Mann beißt der Hund. (Same meaning — emphasizing "the man" by fronting him, but "der Hund" is still nominative and "den Mann" is still accusative, regardless of position.)

**Mental model:** case endings are **name tags**, not word order. In English/Spanish you know the subject by where they're standing in line. In German everyone wears a name tag (der/den/dem/des, plus matching pronoun/adjective changes) that says "I'm the subject" or "I'm the object," so they're free to stand wherever the sentence wants them for emphasis. This is why Lesson 1's word-order flexibility works at all — case is the machinery underneath it.

## 2. Nominativ — the "namer," the subject

Marks whoever/whatever is doing the action — the grammatical subject; also the dictionary form.
- **Der Mann** liest.
- **Die Frau** arbeitet.
- **Das Kind** spielt.

## 3. Akkusativ — the direct object

Marks the thing directly receiving the action. Only the **masculine** article changes between Nominativ and Akkusativ — feminine, neuter, and plural stay identical:

| | Nominativ (subject) | Akkusativ (direct object) |
|---|---|---|
| masculine | der Mann | **den** Mann |
| feminine | die Frau | die Frau |
| neuter | das Kind | das Kind |
| plural | die Männer | die Männer |

- Ich sehe **den Mann**. (der → den, masculine)
- Ich sehe **die Frau**. (no change — feminine)
- Ich sehe **das Kind**. (no change — neuter)

**Mental model:** masculine nouns are the only ones that "put on a different name tag" as the object — everyone else keeps their badge. Roughly 3 out of 4 nouns don't change at all between these two cases.

⚠️ **Common Spanish-speaker mistake:** Spanish marks direct objects with a preposition for people ("veo *a* la mujer") but not things ("veo el libro") — the "personal a," a totally different mechanism than German's article-based case marking. There's no clean one-to-one mapping; German cares about grammatical gender, not whether the object is a person.

## 4. Pronouns follow the same logic, more dramatically

| Nominativ | Akkusativ |
|---|---|
| ich | mich |
| du | dich |
| er | ihn |
| sie | sie |
| es | es |
| wir | uns |
| ihr | euch |
| sie/Sie | sie/Sie |

Some of these appeared as reflexive pronouns in Lesson 8 (mich, dich) — same forms, different job. `er → ihn` is the most noticeable non-reflexive change; very high-frequency ("Ich liebe ihn," "Ich sehe ihn nicht").

## 5. Progressively harder examples

1. Der Kellner bringt den Kaffee.
2. Ich liebe ihn, aber er liebt mich nicht. (pronoun cases + nicht + aber)
3. Den Film mag ich nicht, aber das Buch mag ich sehr. (fronting the accusative object — direct callback to Lesson 1)
4. Ich muss den Bericht heute noch schreiben, weil mein Chef ihn morgen braucht. (modal + accusative noun + weil + accusative pronoun)

## 6. Speaking exercise

Choose der/den correctly, out loud:
- Ich sehe ___ Mann. (accusative — masculine)
- Ich sehe ___ Frau. (accusative — feminine, no change)
- Ich brauche ___ Kaffee. (accusative — masculine)

Then say example #3 first in neutral order (Ich mag den Film nicht), then fronted (Den Film mag ich nicht) — feel the emphasis shift while the grammar stays stable.

## 7. Thinking-in-German habit

Narrate two things you like and one you don't, fronting the object of at least one sentence for emphasis:
> *Ich mag Kaffee sehr. Den Tee mag ich nicht so sehr. Ich trinke lieber Wasser.*

## 8. Real-life mission

Order something today using an accusative masculine noun consciously — "Ich hätte gern **den** Kaffee" or "Ich nehme **den** Salat" — and notice the der→den shift in your own speech.
