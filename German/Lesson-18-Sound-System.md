# Lesson 18 — The Sound System: IPA, Vowels, Umlauts, and Rhythm

**Phase:** 0 — Foundations (final lesson of this phase)
**Status:** ✅ Done

---

## 1. The core idea

A systematic map of German sounds, so pronunciation becomes one coherent system rather than scattered tips. IPA used sparingly — just enough to pin down sounds that don't exist in Spanish.

## 2. Vowels: the length distinction Spanish doesn't have

Spanish has five vowel sounds, each one length. German vowels come in long and short pairs, and the difference changes meaning:
- **Stadt** [ʃtat] (short a) — city vs. **Staat** [ʃtaːt] (long a) — state
- **Ofen** [ˈoːfən] (long o) — oven vs. **offen** [ˈɔfən] (short o) — open

**Mental model:** German vowel length is like holding a musical note — long vowels held roughly twice as long, and native listeners use this duration to distinguish words. Spanish speakers often flatten both to the same length, making "Staat" sound like "Stadt."

**Spelling rule of thumb:** a doubled vowel (Boot, Meer), a vowel + "h" (Bahn, ihm), or a vowel + single consonant + another vowel (Name, wohnen) usually signals long. A vowel + double consonant (Bett, Mann, offen) usually signals short.

## 3. The umlauts: ä, ö, ü — genuinely new sounds

Not accent marks — distinct vowel sounds absent from Spanish, requiring new muscle memory:
- **ä** [ɛ] — like "e" in English "bed," between Spanish "e" and "a." Try: Mädchen, Käse.
- **ö** [ø] — round lips as for Spanish "o," but try to say "e." Try: schön, hören.
- **ü** [y] — round lips as for Spanish "u," but try to say "i." Try: müde, für.

**Mental model for ö/ü:** "lip-shape mismatches" — mouth shaped for one vowel while the tongue tries to produce a different one. The physical awkwardness is a good sign, not a mistake.

⚠️ **Common Spanish-speaker mistake:** collapsing ö → o and ü → u. Creates real ambiguity: "schon" (already) vs. "schön" (beautiful) become indistinguishable. Deliberately over-exaggerate lip-rounding while practicing.

## 4. Consonants: sounds absent from Spanish

**The two "ch" sounds (from Lesson 1, full system here):**
- Soft ch [ç] after i/e/ä/ö/ü and consonants: ich, nicht, München, durch
- Hard ch [x] after a/o/u: ach, Buch, auch, Woche

**The German r:** unlike Spanish's tapped/trilled "r," standard German "r" is often **uvular** — produced further back, closer to French "r" or a gargling motion, especially word-initially (rot, Reise). At the end of a word/syllable it often nearly disappears into a vowel-like sound (Mutter, hier) — this "vocalized r" is genuinely tricky for Spanish speakers.

**The "s" sounds:** German "s" before a vowel is voiced, like English "z" (sagen, sehen sound like "zagen," "zehen"); Spanish "s" is always unvoiced. "ß"/ss is unvoiced, like English "s" (dass, Fuß).

**"z" is always [ts]:** Zeit, Zimmer, zu — never like English "z" (connects to Lesson 16's consonant shift, German z ↔ English t).

## 5. Word and sentence stress

German word stress overwhelmingly falls on the first syllable of the root, especially for native Germanic words — different from Spanish's more variable, written-accent-marked stress:
- **TRINK**en, **AR**beiten, **FREUND**lich

Exception: separable prefixes take the stress (Lesson 7) — **AUF**stehen, **AN**kommen — while inseparable prefixes and borrowed/Latinate words stress elsewhere: verSTEHen, Universi**TÄT**, Musik.

Sentence-level stress falls on the word carrying the most new information — part of why the "hold pitch, drop at the end" rhythm from Lesson 2 works.

## 6. Progressively harder examples (pronunciation-focused)

1. schon / schön — short o vs. rounded ö, minimal pair
2. Staat / Stadt — long a vs. short a, minimal pair
3. Ich möchte müde nach Hause gehen. (ch, ö, ü, and vocalized final r in "Hause" all together)
4. Zucker ist zu teuer, aber ich kaufe ihn trotzdem. (z as [ts], uvular r at word-start, vocalized r in "aber")

## 7. Speaking exercise — shadowing drill

Say each pair out loud, exaggerating the distinction, 5 times each back to back:
- schon vs. schön
- Ofen vs. offen
- ich vs. ach
- Bett vs. Beet (short e vs. long e — Beet = flowerbed)

Record yourself saying a short sentence from a recent lesson (voice memo), then compare it against your podcast — self-monitoring is a high-leverage pronunciation tool.

## 8. Thinking-in-German habit

During today's silent narration, consciously monitor ö/ü sounds and the ich/ach distinction as you think — pronunciation awareness in silent internal speech transfers to spoken accuracy over time.

## 9. Real-life mission

Listen for one ö or ü word in today's podcast and pause to repeat it immediately, matching the speaker's mouth shape — immediate shadowing after real native audio beats shadowing from a written list.

## 10. Mini quiz

2. Describe in your own words how to produce ü.
3. Ich or ach: which "ch" sound in "München"? In "auch"?
4. Where does stress fall in "aufstehen"? In "verstehen"? Why the difference?
