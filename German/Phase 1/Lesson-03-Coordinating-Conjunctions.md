# Lesson 3 — The Conjunctions That Don't Break Word Order

**Phase:** 1 — The Sentence Machine
**Status:** ✅ Done

---

## 1. The core idea

Lesson 2 taught that `weil`, `dass`, `wenn`, `ob`, `als` are "verb magnets" — they always push the conjugated verb to the end. Not all connecting words do this. German has a small, closed set of words that connect two full sentences **without touching word order at all** — the **coordinating conjunctions**.

Memory device — **ADUSO**:

| German | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **A**ber | but |
| **D**enn | because/for |
| **U**nd | and |
| **S**ondern | but rather (after a negation) |
| **O**der | or |

These five glue two independent, normal-word-order sentences together side by side, like two Lego blocks placed next to each other rather than one nested inside the other:

**Ich lerne Deutsch, und ich lebe in Berlin.**
- Ich lerne Deutsch. (normal V2)
- ich lebe in Berlin. (still normal V2 — "ich" stays in position 1)

Compare to a subordinating conjunction, where the second clause's verb is exiled to the end:
**Ich lerne Deutsch, weil ich in Berlin lebe.**

Same meaning-space (reason), completely different mechanics.

## 2. The trap: denn vs weil

Both translate as "because," but they behave differently:
- **weil** (subordinating) → verb to the end: Ich bleibe zu Hause, weil ich krank **bin**.
- **denn** (coordinating) → normal word order: Ich bleibe zu Hause, denn ich **bin** krank.

Both correct, roughly same meaning. `denn` skews written/formal; `weil` dominates spoken German (and is even heard colloquially with normal word order — nonstandard, don't produce it yourself yet).

**Mental model:** ADUSO conjunctions are a **comma with a label** — they announce a relationship between two otherwise-independent, fully-formed sentences. Subordinating conjunctions are **grammatical operators** — they reach into the clause and physically rearrange it.

## 3. Sondern — the "but rather" trap

`sondern` only appears after a **negation**, when correcting/replacing an idea rather than just contrasting it:
- Ich bin nicht müde, **sondern** hungrig.
- Ich trinke keinen Kaffee, **sondern** Tee.

No prior negation to correct → use `aber`:
- Ich bin müde, **aber** glücklich. (contrast, no correction)

⚠️ **Common Spanish-speaker mistake:** Spanish "pero" covers both cases, so the aber/sondern distinction tends to collapse to `aber` by default. Rule of thumb: after any "nicht/kein...", if naming what's true *instead*, it's `sondern`, not `aber`.

## 4. Progressively harder examples

1. Ich mag Kaffee, aber ich trinke lieber Tee.
2. Ich gehe heute nicht arbeiten, denn ich bin krank.
3. Ich bin nicht aus Deutschland, sondern aus Kolumbien.
4. Willst du Kaffee, oder trinkst du lieber Tee?
5. Ich lerne Deutsch, weil ich hier lebe, und ich möchte fließend sprechen. (mixing subordinating + coordinating in one sentence — very natural)

## 5. Speaking exercise

Out loud, noticing how "flat" these feel vs. the verb-delay rhythm from Lesson 2:
- Ich bin müde, aber ich arbeite weiter.
- Ich bleibe zu Hause, denn es regnet.
- Ich trinke keinen Wein, sondern Bier.

Then produce one original sentence with `denn` and one with `weil` describing the same real reason from your life:
> Ich lerne Deutsch, denn ich lebe in Deutschland.
> Ich lerne Deutsch, weil ich in Deutschland lebe.

## 6. Thinking-in-German habit

Continue the `weil` narration from Lesson 2, and add one `aber`-sentence today — a small contrast about your day:
> *Ich bin müde, aber ich fühle mich gut.*

Goal: start stringing two connector types together in your head, not just isolated sentences.

## 7. Real-life mission

In a real conversation today, use **denn** instead of your default `weil` at least once. It'll feel slightly formal/composed — that's correct.

## 8. Mini quiz

1. Which conjunction: "Ich esse kein Fleisch, ___ Gemüse." (correcting a negation)
2. Fix the word order if needed: "Ich bin zu Hause, denn ich habe Fieber."
3. Combine using `weil`: "Ich bin glücklich." + "Ich habe eine neue Arbeit."
4. Combine the same two sentences using `denn` instead — what changes in word order?
